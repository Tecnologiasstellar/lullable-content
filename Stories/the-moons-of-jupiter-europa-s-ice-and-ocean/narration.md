Good evening.

Wherever you are, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it end now. It is done with. It has no more claim on you. Let your shoulders drop. Let the bed hold your whole weight. There is nothing left tonight that needs carrying.

Let your hands open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing find its own slower pace. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing will be asked of you in the morning.

Tonight we are going out to a small white moon, very far from here. It is covered in ice, and cracked all over like a glazed bowl. And underneath that ice there is an ocean. A real one. Deeper than any ocean on Earth, and dark all the way down.

We are going to Europa. We will look at it from above, and then we will sink slowly through it. Nothing down there is in any hurry. It has been quietly turning over for a very long time.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin to travel.

§

We start by rising. Softly, and without effort.

The ground lets go. The roof opens quietly above you. You lift into the dark over your own town, and the lights below draw together into a soft scatter.

The coastline appears. Then the curve of the whole world, with its thin blue rim where the air gives out. And then the air is behind you, and there is no sound. There will be none for the rest of the journey. Out here nothing carries it.

Turn outward, and go.

You pass the Moon. You pass the small rust-coloured light of Mars. You cross a wide belt of drifting rock, spread so thin you could pass through it a thousand times and touch nothing.

And then, ahead, something begins to grow that is not a star.

It has a disc. Then bands. Then colour. Cream, and amber, and a soft rust red, laid down in stripes that run all the way around.

Jupiter.

§

Slow down here, and simply look at it for a while.

It is enormous. Eleven times the width of the Earth. You could lay more than a thousand Earths inside it and still have room.

It is not solid. There is no surface to land on. It is a very deep atmosphere, growing thicker and thicker with depth. Far down, the distinction between gas and liquid stops meaning anything.

The bands are winds. They run in opposite directions, side by side, at speeds you would struggle to imagine. But from here they do not look fast. They look like cream stirred once into coffee and then left alone.

It turns quickly for something so large. A day there is under ten hours. And that quick turn is what pulls the clouds into those long horizontal stripes.

There is no sound coming off it. No smell. No heat you could feel. Only that vast striped face, turning slowly in front of you, minding its own business.

Now look to the side of it. There are four small points of light out there, in a rough line.

§

Those four have been known for a little over four hundred years, and the story of how is a good one.

In January of sixteen ten, an Italian named Galileo Galilei pointed a small handmade telescope at Jupiter. It was not a good telescope by our standards. It was barely better than a pair of opera glasses.

He saw three little dots near the planet, arranged in a line. He drew them.

The next night he looked again. The dots had moved. A few nights later there were four.

He kept watching, night after night, and drawing what he saw. And slowly the pattern became undeniable. The dots were not stars. They were going around Jupiter.

That was the first time anyone had watched something orbit a body other than the Earth. A German astronomer named Simon Marius was very likely watching the same thing, at the same time. It is his names that stuck. Io. Europa. Ganymede. Callisto.

Four dots in a line, in a cheap telescope, on a cold January night.

§

Let us look in on the other three before we go to ours, because they are worth the detour.

The innermost is Io. It is the most volcanically active place in the solar system. It is being squeezed so hard by Jupiter that it keeps turning itself inside out. It repaves its own surface, over and over. It is yellow and orange and sulphurous, and it has almost no craters, because nothing stays long enough to keep a mark.

Furthest out is Callisto. It is the opposite. Nothing has happened there for a very long time. Its surface is the oldest and most cratered of the four, a dark grey record of everything that ever arrived. It has simply been sitting there, keeping count.

And between them is Ganymede, which is the largest moon in the whole solar system. Five thousand two hundred kilometres across. Bigger than the planet Mercury. It even generates its own magnetic field, deep inside, which no other moon does.

Four worlds, in a row, entirely unalike. Then the second one out.

§

Europa comes up white.

Not grey, like our Moon. Not brown. A pale, clean, faintly blue-white, like fresh snow in shadow.

It is small. Three thousand one hundred kilometres across, which makes it a little smaller than our own Moon. It sits about six hundred and seventy thousand kilometres out from Jupiter. It goes all the way round in three and a half days.

Come closer, and the white resolves into something odd.

It is covered in lines.

Long dark curves, running for hundreds and hundreds of kilometres, crossing over one another at every angle. Some are single. Some are doubled, with a paler stripe running between them. They are reddish-brown against the white, and they cover the entire globe.

It looks exactly like a cracked glaze. Like an old plate, or an egg tapped once with a spoon.

And there is something else, which takes a moment to notice, because it is an absence.

§

There are almost no craters.

Take that in for a second. Everything else out here is pocked. Our Moon is pocked. Callisto is pocked all over. Mercury, Mars, every old surface in the solar system carries the record of what has hit it.

Europa is smooth. It is, in fact, the smoothest object we know of anywhere.

There are no mountain ranges. No deep valleys. Nothing rises or falls by very much at all. If you shrank Europa to the size of a billiard ball, it would be smoother than the billiard ball.

Which tells you something immediately. Things have certainly hit Europa, at the same rate they have hit everything else out here. So if the marks are not there, the surface must be new.

And it is. The best estimates put it somewhere between twenty and a hundred and eighty million years old. Which sounds enormous, but out here it is nothing. The surface of Callisto is four thousand million years old.

So something has been resurfacing this moon. Recently, and repeatedly, and gently enough to leave it flat.

§

To understand what, we have to look at the orbit. And the answer is in a very small number.

Europa's path around Jupiter is almost a perfect circle. Almost. Its eccentricity — the measure of how far off circular it is — is about nine thousandths.

That is a tiny departure. If you drew the orbit on paper, no eye could tell it from a circle.

And that tiny departure is the reason there is an ocean.

Because it means Europa is sometimes slightly nearer Jupiter, and sometimes slightly further away. And Jupiter's pull is immense. So as the moon moves in and out, the pull strengthens and eases. Strengthens and eases. Twice every three and a half days.

The whole moon stretches, very slightly, and relaxes. Then stretches again.

It is being kneaded. Continuously. The way you work dough with the heel of your hand, in and out, without ever stopping.

§

And kneading makes heat. That is not a metaphor.

Bend a paperclip back and forth and the bend gets warm. Squeeze a rubber ball repeatedly and it warms in your hand. Whenever material is worked, some of that work turns into heat inside it.

Europa is being worked on a scale that is hard to hold. The tidal bulge on that moon rises and falls by something like thirty metres, every few days, forever.

So the inside of Europa is warm. Not warm as you would recognise it. But warm enough that water there stays liquid. And that is hundreds of millions of kilometres from the sun. Sunlight out there is a twenty-fifth as strong as it is on your face at noon.

There is no other way to do it out there. The sun is not doing this. Jupiter is.

§

Now, there is a lovely question underneath this, which is why the orbit stays slightly off-circular at all.

Ordinarily, all that flexing should have smoothed it out long ago. Tidal kneading costs orbital energy. Left alone, Europa's orbit would have relaxed into a true circle. The flexing would have stopped. The ocean would have frozen through.

It has not, because Europa is not left alone.

Io, Europa and Ganymede are locked together in a rhythm. For every four laps Io makes around Jupiter, Europa makes exactly two, and Ganymede makes exactly one. Four, two, one.

Because the ratio is exact, they meet up in the same relative positions again and again. And each time they do, they tug on one another, always in the same direction.

Those repeated small tugs keep pulling all three orbits slightly out of round. Which keeps the flexing going. Which keeps the heat on.

Three moons, holding one another slightly off balance, and an ocean that stays liquid because of it. Nobody arranged this. It simply settled into that pattern and stayed.

§

So let us talk about the ocean itself.

Under the ice, the water is thought to run about a hundred kilometres deep.

A hundred kilometres. The deepest place in any ocean on Earth is about eleven. So this is nearly ten times deeper than the deepest trench we have, everywhere, all the way around a whole moon.

And when you work out the volume, you get something like three billion billion cubic metres. Which is between two and three times all the water in every ocean on Earth combined.

Every sea. Every trench. All of it, doubled, and then some. On a moon smaller than ours.

There is more liquid water on Europa than on the surface of the Earth. It is simply all in the dark, under a lid.

§

Which raises a fair question. How can anyone possibly know that, when nobody has ever been down there?

The answer is a lovely piece of detective work. It came from a spacecraft called Galileo, which spent eight years in the Jupiter system.

Jupiter has a colossal magnetic field, and it is tilted relative to Jupiter's spin. So as Jupiter turns, that field sweeps back and forth across Europa. From Europa's point of view, it sits in a magnetic field that keeps changing.

Now, here is the physics. A changing magnetic field, passing through anything that conducts electricity, sets up a current in it. And that current makes a magnetic field of its own, pushing back.

So Galileo went past and measured the field around Europa. And it found exactly that. A field being generated by Europa itself, changing in step with Jupiter's, in the way an induced field does.

Something inside that moon conducts electricity. Ice does not. Rock does not, much. Salty water does.

The ocean was found from the outside, by magnetism, without anybody touching it.

§

Now, the layer of ice on top. And here I have to be honest with you, because this part is not settled.

Two pictures compete.

In one, the shell is thick. Something like ten to thirty kilometres of solid ice, moving slowly, with the ocean sealed well below it. Some careful modelling puts the average near thirty. In that picture the ice behaves like glacier ice on Earth. Rigid over hours. Flowing over centuries.

In the other, the shell is thin. Only a few kilometres. In that picture the ocean is close to the surface, and the cracks may open all the way down.

The argument matters. It decides whether anything from the ocean can reach the top. And whether anything on top can get down.

Nobody is being lazy. It is a difficult thing to measure from far away, and the evidence points both ways. It will very likely be settled by radar, from an orbiting spacecraft, in the next several years.

Until then, it is simply an open question. You are allowed to find that restful rather than frustrating. Somebody will know. It does not have to be tonight, and it does not have to be you.

§

Before we land, there is one more thing about that rhythm, and it changes the sky.

Europa turns once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to go once around Jupiter. Three and a half days for both. The two motions are locked together.

This happens a lot. Our own Moon does it, which is why we only ever see one face of it.

And it has a consequence that is lovely to picture. If you stood on the side of Europa that faces Jupiter, Jupiter would hang in your sky and never move.

Not rise. Not set. Not drift across. It would sit in the same patch of sky, at the same height, for your entire life.

It would be enormous. From the surface it spans about twelve degrees of sky. Our full moon spans half a degree. So imagine something twenty-four times wider than the moon you know. Fixed overhead, striped in cream and amber, slowly turning in place.

And it would go through phases, as our moon does. A great banded crescent. Then half. Then a full striped disc. Then thinning back again. All of it in three and a half days, all of it without the thing ever shifting position.

Meanwhile the sun would rise and set on that same three-and-a-half-day rhythm. Small, and pale, and quick.

A sky with one thing that never moves, and one thing that keeps time.

§

Come down close to the surface now, and let us look at the ground.

It is water ice, and it is hard. At these temperatures ice is not the soft stuff you chip out of a freezer. It behaves like rock.

It is very cold. Around a hundred and sixty degrees below zero at the equator. Colder at the poles, down near two hundred and twenty below.

The lines are up close now, and they are wide. Many are several kilometres across. Some have a raised ridge on either side and a valley between, running dead straight to the horizon and beyond it.

And there are regions where the ordinary landscape simply stops.

Blocks of crust, tilted at all angles, sitting in a jumbled matrix. As though a sheet of ice had broken up, drifted a little, rotated, and then set again. Geologists call this chaos terrain. An unusually vivid name, for a feature that has not moved in a very long time.

If you could refreeze a broken harbour in winter, exactly as the floes lay, you would have something like it.

§

The colour in those cracks is worth stopping on.

The white of Europa is water ice. But the lines and the blotches are a warmer shade. Tan, and rust, and dull brown. Something is making them that colour.

The evidence points at salts. Magnesium sulfate, most likely. And, importantly, ordinary sodium chloride. Table salt.

That matters more than it sounds. Table salt is what you would expect if this material came up from a salty ocean below. Rather than being made on the surface, by sunlight and radiation.

There is another ingredient too. Sulphur, drifting across from Io, which throws vast quantities of it out into the Jupiter system. Some of that lands here. Radiation works on it, and darkens it further.

So the rust in the cracks is partly ocean, brought up. And partly Io, blown over. The moons are exchanging material with each other, slowly, across half a million kilometres of empty space.

§

And there is a question hanging over all of this, which has been going back and forth for years. I want to tell it to you properly, because it is a good example of science being honest.

The question is whether Europa sprays.

In twenty thirteen, astronomers using a telescope in orbit reported a faint ultraviolet glow over the moon's southern half. The natural reading was water vapour. A plume, escaping through a crack, throwing ocean out into space.

That would be wonderful, practically speaking. It would mean you could sample an alien ocean without ever landing, by flying through the spray.

Then, a few years later, someone went back to twenty-year-old data. In nineteen ninety-seven the Galileo spacecraft had passed about four hundred kilometres above the surface. Its instruments had recorded an odd bend in the magnetic field and a sudden crowd of charged particles. Nobody had known what to make of it at the time. With newer models, a team suggested the spacecraft had flown straight through a plume without anyone noticing.

Two independent hints. It looked settled.

And then it began to come apart. A reanalysis suggested the original ultraviolet signal may have been an instrument artefact rather than water. A newer, far more sensitive telescope looked and found no plume at all. And a fresh study of the Galileo plasma data argued that the nineteen ninety-seven encounter was probably not a plume either.

So the evidence has been quietly retreating for a decade.

That is not a failure. That is the process working exactly as it should. Somebody proposed something, other people checked it hard, and the claim did not survive the checking. It may yet come back. Plumes might be occasional, and everyone might simply have looked at quiet times.

Nobody knows tonight. Nobody has to.

§

There is an atmosphere here, technically. It is worth mentioning mostly because of how it is made.

It is oxygen. And it is so thin as to be almost nothing. The pressure at the surface is around a hundred million millionth of the pressure you are breathing right now.

But the way it gets there is elegant.

Charged particles and ultraviolet light strike the ice all day. That is enough to break the water molecules apart into hydrogen and oxygen.

Hydrogen is light, so it drifts away and is gone. Oxygen is heavier, so it lingers, held loosely near the surface.

Nobody delivered it. There is no life making it. It is simply what happens when light lands on ice for long enough. Water, coming apart, one molecule at a time, for millions of years.

§

Now let us do the part we cannot really do, and go down.

Imagine the ice opening quietly and letting you through.

The first stretch is bright, in a diffuse way. Light comes through the upper ice the way daylight comes through a snowbank, blue and even and directionless.

Then it dims. Then the blue deepens. And then, somewhere in the first few kilometres, it goes entirely.

Now you are in solid ice in complete darkness, still sinking, and it is very quiet. Nothing here is moving quickly. The ice creeps, over centuries. On any human timescale it is perfectly still.

Then, at some depth — nobody yet knows precisely which — the ice ends. And you are in water.

Salt water. Just above freezing. Utterly black, and it goes down for a hundred kilometres beneath you.

There is no light down here. There never has been. Not once, anywhere in this ocean, in its entire history.

§

Keep sinking. There is a floor, eventually, and it is rock.

That is the last thing worth knowing tonight. Europa's ocean is not a sealed capsule of water. It sits directly on a rocky mantle.

And that rock is being kneaded too, by the same flexing that warms the ice. So the seafloor down there may well be warm in places. Water meeting warm rock does chemistry. On Earth, at exactly such places, minerals dissolve and settle and build slow chimneys in the dark.

Whether anything at all is happening on Europa's floor, nobody knows. It is the honest answer, and it is the reason two spacecraft are on their way.

One of them left Earth in October of twenty twenty-four. It has already been past Mars once to borrow speed, and it comes back past us to borrow a little more. It reaches Jupiter in the spring of twenty thirty, and then it will make forty-nine slow passes over this moon. A second one, from Europe, arrives about a year later.

They are crossing right now, while you lie there. Very quietly, for years, in the dark.

§

There is one detail about that first spacecraft that I find genuinely soothing, and it is about patience.

It is not going to orbit Europa. It is going to orbit Jupiter instead.

The reason is that Europa sits deep inside Jupiter's magnetic field. That field holds a great deal of trapped charged particle energy. Electronics do not last long sitting in it. A spacecraft parked in close orbit would be worn out within months.

So the plan is the other way round. The spacecraft settles into a long looping orbit around Jupiter. Most of the time it is far out, in the quiet, where nothing is wearing on it. Then it swings in, passes low over Europa, gathers everything it can in a few hours, and climbs back out again.

Out in the calm it does two things. It rests. And it sends home what it just collected, slowly, over the following days.

Forty-nine of those passes. One at a time, over years.

There is something very unhurried about that design. It could have gone in close and worked hard and been finished quickly. Instead it will spend most of its life out in the quiet part, handing back a little at a time.

It is a machine built to wait well.

§

And here is the last thing, which brings all of it back into your own garden.

You can see these moons yourself.

Not the ice. Not the cracks. But the four dots, in a line, exactly as they were drawn in sixteen ten. They sit at around magnitude five, which is bright. Ganymede is the brightest of them, then Io, then Europa, then Callisto.

They would be visible to the unaided eye, if it were not for Jupiter. The planet is simply so much brighter that its glare drowns them.

So you need something to separate them out. And here is the part I love. Any ordinary pair of binoculars will do it. Ten by fifty. The kind people keep in a drawer for birdwatching.

In fact a modern pair of binoculars gives a cleaner, sharper view than anything Galileo ever had. His lenses were poor by any standard we would accept now. They magnified more, and showed less.

So on some clear night, when Jupiter is up, you could take a pair of binoculars outside and rest your elbows on a wall to keep them steady. And there they would be. Three or four small points, in a rough line, on either side of a bright dot.

One of those points is a white moon with a hundred kilometres of dark salt water underneath it.

You will not see any of that. You will see a dot. But you will know, and knowing changes the dot.

§

Let yourself grow still now, the way that ocean is still.

Feel your weight where it rests. You do not have to hold it up. Let the last of the day go out of your shoulders, and your hands, and your face.

Your breathing has already slowed. You did not do that on purpose. It happened while you were looking at something else.

Far away, the ice creeps. The moon stretches and relaxes, and stretches again, and will do so tonight and tomorrow and for a very long time after. The dark water turns over slowly beneath it.

Nothing down there needs watching. Nothing down there is waiting.

§

And now let all of it go.

You do not need to keep the depths, or the numbers, or the names of the four moons. You do not need to remember any of this in the morning. It was only somewhere gentle to rest your attention while your attention was letting go.

Let the ice fade. Let the water fade. Let the dark come in, soft and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
