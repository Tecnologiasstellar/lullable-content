Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, let it end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to walk, very slowly, along a shore at first light. A blue-grey shore in the south of England, where the tide is out and the stone is wet. A woman walked here every morning, two hundred years ago, with a basket and a small hammer. And the cliffs gave her the oldest things anyone had ever seen. Nobody hurried. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

We begin at the edge of the water, a little before sunrise, in the winter of 1812.

The town is Lyme Regis, on the coast of Dorset, where the west of England meets the sea. It is a small town. A steep main street runs down to the water, with houses shouldered close together on either side. Their shutters are closed. A few chimneys have begun to smoke. In summer this place fills with visitors, who come for the sea air and the bathing. But it is winter now, and the town belongs to the people who live in it.

Ahead of you the sea is the colour of pewter. It is calm this morning. The air is cold and damp, and it smells of salt and wet weed and woodsmoke. Your breath hangs in front of you for a moment, and then it is gone.

To your right, a long curved wall of stone reaches out into the water. The people here call it the Cobb. It shelters the little harbour, and a few boats lie inside it, rocking gently on their ropes. You can hear them if you listen. A soft knock of wood on wood. A rope creaking. And under everything, the slow breath of the sea on the shingle.

The tide is going out. It has been going out for hours. And as it goes, it uncovers something.

Look down, now, at what the sea is leaving behind.

The beach here is not sand. It is a shelf of dark stone, flat and wide, running out from the foot of the cliffs. Ledge after ledge, like the steps of a very old staircase, each one a little lower than the last. The stone is a deep blue-grey, almost black where it is wet. The people here call it the Lias.

It is slippery underfoot, so we will walk slowly. Between the ledges lie pools of still water, and in them, small shapes. A limpet. A tuft of red weed. A little crab, perfectly still, waiting for the tide to come back.

The ledges have names, given to them by the men who once quarried this stone. The harder bands made a fine cement, and were cut and carted away for building. Table Ledge. Broad Ledge. Plain, working names, for plain, hard stone. The quarrymen are gone now. The names have stayed.

Walk east along the shore, and the cliffs grow taller and darker. That is Black Ven. Beyond it lies the small village of Charmouth, where a river comes down to the sea. The shale there is threaded with thin white seams of a fibrous mineral. The men who mapped these cliffs called it beef, because it looks like the grain of cooked meat. Shales with beef, they wrote in their notebooks. And that is still its name.

The ledges are covered, twice a day, and uncovered, twice a day. The tide here is pulled by the moon, as it is everywhere. And when the moon is new, or full, the pull is strongest. Then the water goes out further than usual. It uncovers the lowest ledges, the ones that stay drowned the rest of the month. Those are the mornings a fossil hunter waits for.

Every morning the water draws back and shows the stone. And every morning the stone is a little different than it was. The sea has been at it all night. That is the whole secret of this place, and we will come to it.

Now turn, and look up at the cliffs.

They rise behind the beach, dark and layered, not very tall. And they are striped. Band after band of stone, laid one on top of another, all the way to the grass at the top. A pale band, a hand's width thick. Then a dark band, thicker and softer. Then pale again. Then dark. Over and over, like the pages of a closed book seen from the side.

The pale bands are limestone. Hard, and grey-blue, and cold to the touch. The dark bands are shale. Soft, and crumbling, and black with old mud. Together they make the Blue Lias. About a hundred feet of it here, band on band on band.

Every one of those bands was once the floor of a sea. Not this sea. An older one. A warm, shallow sea that lay over this ground for a very long time, when the world was arranged quite differently. Mud settled to the bottom of it. Slowly. A grain at a time. Then the water changed a little, and lime settled instead. Then mud again.

Each band took thousands of years to lay down. And there are hundreds of them. So the cliff you are looking at is a clock. The bottom is the oldest. The top is the youngest. And the whole of it is close to two hundred million years old.

You do not have to hold that number. Just let it be large. Let it be a soft, deep, quiet thing behind the cliffs.

Let us stay with the cliffs a moment longer, because of what they do in winter.

The shale is soft. When the rain comes, it soaks in. When the frost comes, it swells and loosens. And when the winter storms roll up the Channel, the sea pushes at the foot of the cliff. And a little of the cliff lets go. Not much. A slice, here and there. A little slump of dark mud, sliding gently down onto the beach.

And inside that mud are the things that were lying on the sea floor, two hundred million years ago. Shells. Bones. Small coiled stones. They have been sealed in the dark all that time. And now, quietly, the cliff gives them up.

The sea does the rest. It washes the new mud with every tide. It carries the soft stuff away, a little at a time. And it leaves the hard things behind, rinsed and shining, lying loose on the ledges. Every winter the beach is scoured clean. Every winter the cliff sets out something new.

That is why the best time to walk here is a winter morning, after rain, at low tide. And the first person along the beach at dawn will see it first.

For most of thirty years, that first person was a woman named Mary Anning.

Let us look at what she is looking for. The commonest thing here is an ammonite.

There is one at your feet now, if you like. A coiled stone, about as wide as your palm, curled into a tight spiral like a ram's horn. Its surface is ridged, with fine ribs running across it from the centre outward. Pick it up. It is cold, and heavier than it looks. Some of them here are shot through with a brassy golden mineral. They shine a little in the low light.

An ammonite was a living creature. It was a relative of the squid and the octopus. A soft animal with tentacles, living in the last and largest chamber of a coiled shell. The rest of the shell, behind it, was divided into small rooms, sealed off one by one as the animal grew. A thin tube ran back through all of them. With it the creature could let a little gas into the chambers, or a little water. And so it could rise, or sink, or hang quite still in the warm water.

So this stone in your hand was once a small floating house. It drifted with the current. It rose and sank in a green sea. And when its long life ended, it settled down into the soft mud. The mud closed over it. And the sea went on.

At the far end of the beach, there is a ledge. Hundreds of them lie there, side by side in the stone. Some wider than a serving dish. People call it the ammonite pavement. Nobody laid it. The sea did.

The name is old, too. A writer in ancient Rome looked at these coiled stones and thought of the curled horns of a ram. The horns of Ammon, he called them, after a god who wore such horns. The name softened, over the centuries, into the word we use now.

And here is a small, deep thing about them. The ammonites in the lowest bands of the cliff are smooth, with hardly a rib. Higher up, they grow ribs. Then a keel along the outer edge. Then they grow large. Each band has its own kind. So a person who knows them can pick up a stone on the beach. Turn it over. And say which band it fell from. The shell is a page number. It tells you where in the book you are.

There are other things in the stone, smaller and quieter. Let us find two more.

The first looks like a bullet. A smooth, pointed cylinder of stone, about the length of your finger, the colour of dark honey. Country people used to call them thunderbolts. They are belemnites. Each one was the inner skeleton of an animal like a squid. A long, slim swimmer with ten arms, that lived and hunted in the warm water above the mud. The soft body is gone. What is left is the hard point at its tail. It is made of a mineral called calcite, in fine crystals packed side by side.

The second is even smaller. A little five-pointed star, no bigger than a lentil, pressed into the shale. Sometimes there are dozens in a row, stacked like coins. These are the pieces of a crinoid, which is a strange and lovely thing. It looks like a flower, and it is an animal. A long stalk, fixed to the sea floor, or to a drifting log. At the top, a crown of feathery arms. It swayed in the current and gathered whatever floated by. The stalk was built of hundreds of those little stars, threaded together.

So the sea floor here was not empty. It was a garden. Coiled shells drifting above. Slim swimmers moving through. Flowers of stone swaying in the dark. All of it settling, in the end, into the mud, and waiting.

Now let us meet the woman on the beach.

Mary Anning was born in this town in the spring of 1799. Her family lived near the bridge, in a house close to the water. So close that the sea sometimes came up to the door. Her father was a cabinet maker. And on the side, like a good many people here, he collected curiosities from the shore. Ammonites. Thunderbolts. Little stars. He sold them from a table to the visitors who came to Lyme in the summer for the sea air.

The visitors called them curiosities. The people of the town had older names. Snake stones, for the coiled ones. Devil's fingers, for the bullets. And for the round bones of the great creatures, which turned up loose on the beach now and then, verteberries.

He took his children along the beach with him. He showed them where to look. How to read the wet stone for a shape that did not belong to it. How to tap a lump of shale, just so, until it opened along its grain like a book. How to lift a fossil without breaking it. Mary was small, and she watched, and she learned.

By the time she was ten, she knew the beach the way you know your own kitchen in the dark. She knew which ledges the sea uncovered first. She knew which slumps of mud were worth a second look. She went out in the cold with a basket and a little hammer, and she came home with the basket heavy.

Nothing about this was dramatic. It was a walk, at low tide, every morning. That is all it ever was. And it went on for the rest of her life.

And then, one year, the beach gave her something larger.

In the autumn of 1811, her older brother Joseph was walking under the cliffs to the east of the town. And he found a skull. Not a small one. Four feet long, with a jaw full of teeth and a great round eye socket like a saucer. It was set in the stone, and he worked it loose, and the family carried it home.

For a year, nobody knew where the rest of it was. Then Mary found it. She was twelve. She went back to the place, and read the cliff, and saw the line where the bones went in. And through the following months, patiently, she uncovered the rest. Vertebrae. Ribs. The long paddle of a limb. When it was all out, it was something like seventeen feet from nose to tail.

Nobody in England had ever seen anything like it. It looked a little like a fish, and a little like a crocodile, and it was neither. It was a sea creature that breathed air, built like a dolphin, with a long narrow snout. Its eyes were enormous. In the biggest of these creatures, an eye could be as wide as a dinner plate. Each one was held open by a ring of thin bony plates, so that it could see in the dim water. Years later, it would be given the name ichthyosaur. Which only means fish lizard.

It took the learned men a while to agree on what it was. A surgeon in London described the skull first. Then, in 1821, two young geologists wrote a careful paper on all the bones found so far. They concluded that this was a kind of reptile nobody had known about. One that had lived its whole life in the sea. Both of those men came to know her well, and to ask her what she thought.

A gentleman in the town bought it, for twenty-three pounds. It went to London, to a museum on Piccadilly, and people paid to walk past it in the lamplight. Later it went to the British Museum, where it still is. But it began here, in the wet shale, at low tide, under the hands of a girl of twelve.

Let us follow one of those finds home, and into the workshop.

Picture a low room, close to the sea. A wooden table by the window, for the light. On it, a slab of dark stone, still damp, with the shape of a spine just showing through. And beside it, the tools. A small hammer. A few chisels, from the width of a thumb down to the width of a needle. A stiff brush, and a soft one. A knife. A cloth. A bowl of clean water.

The work is slow. The stone that holds a fossil is also the stone that hides it. So she takes it off a grain at a time. A tap. A brush. A look. Another tap. She works from what she can see toward what she cannot. She feels for the edge of bone with the tip of a blade. When the rock is soft, she can lift it away in flakes. When it is hard, she must be patient, and let the chisel do its slow work.

If a bone comes loose, she sets it back where it belongs. She fixes the pieces onto a frame with a little cement. So the whole animal lies as it lay in the sea. And when it is clean, she draws it.

Her drawings were careful, and exact. She copied the scientific papers that visitors lent her, word for word, and picture for picture. Later scholars could barely tell her copies from the printed pages. She had no school for this. She had the beach, and the table, and the lamp, and a very great deal of time.

There is one more thing she did at that table, and it is a quiet kind of genius.

She wanted to know what the bones had been for. So she looked at living things. She got a fish from the boats and opened it. She looked at its spine, and its fins, and the way they joined. She got a cuttlefish, and opened that, and found a small dark bag of ink inside it.

And it was exactly like a small black thing she had been finding in the shale. A hard, dark lump, about the size of a walnut, lying where the belemnites lay. She had wondered what it was. Now she knew. It was an ink sac. The animal in the stone had carried ink, two hundred million years ago. Just as the one on her table did now.

She found something else in the cuttlefish, too. A second small pouch, like the gizzard of a hen, with a few little horny pieces inside it. She wrote it down. She thought it must help the creature to digest. She was right about that as well.

That is how she worked. She did not guess. She compared. The fossil in one hand, the living creature in the other. And slowly, one small certainty at a time, the sea floor of a lost world came into focus. On a kitchen table, in Dorset.

Now let us go back to the beach, in the winter of 1823, for the second great find.

It was the tenth of December. Cold, and the tide low, and the light flat and grey. And in the shale under the cliff, Mary found a skeleton that made no sense at all.

It had a small head. And then a neck, going on and on. Thirty-five bones in the neck alone. Then a broad, flat body, like a turtle without its shell. And four wide paddles, and a short tail. The whole thing was about nine feet long, and nearly complete, and lying just as it had settled.

Think of how it must have moved. Four paddles, rowing together. Slow strokes, in warm water. The small head at the end of that long neck, turning this way and that. Looking for fish among the drifting shells. A creature built for taking its time.

A clergyman named William Conybeare had already guessed at such a creature, from scattered bones found earlier. He had even given it a name. Plesiosaur. It means, more or less, near to a lizard. Which was his careful way of saying: something like a reptile, but not quite anything we know. And now here it was, whole, exactly as he had imagined it. He must have sat down rather quickly when he heard.

The news went up to London. A great anatomist in Paris heard of it, and thought at first that the neck was too long to be true. He looked again, and was satisfied. A duke bought it, for a hundred and fifty guineas. And in February it was carried to the Geological Society, to be shown at a meeting. It was so large it would not fit up the stairs. So they laid it out in the hallway, and the gentlemen came down to see it.

Its long neck had been lying in the dark for two hundred million years. It could wait a little longer in a hallway.

Five years later, in December again, the cliff gave up something that had flown.

It was a tangle of thin bones in the shale. A long tail. Long fingers. And along the fingers, the fine lines of a wing. It was a pterosaur, the first ever found in England. A flying reptile, with a wingspan of four or five feet, and a large, deep head like a puffin's.

A pterosaur's wing is a strange and simple thing. One finger on each hand grew enormously long. Longer than the whole arm. And a thin skin was stretched from the tip of that finger down to the body. So the animal flew, in effect, on its fourth fingers. When it rested, the long finger folded back, and the wing folded with it.

A professor named William Buckland read a paper about it the next year. He took the unusual step of saying, in print, exactly who had found it. Later it was given a new name. Dimorphodon. Which means two kinds of teeth, because it had long ones in the front of its jaw and small ones behind.

So now the sea had given her a swimmer built like a dolphin. A long-necked paddler. And a creature of the air, that had settled on that same warm water once, and folded its wings. All of them lying quietly in the same dark stone, from the same lost sea.

And there is the small matter of the bezoar stones.

All along the beach, among the bones, there lay odd little lumps of pale stone. Rounded, and often spiralled, like a small loaf that had been twisted before it was baked. People had a name for them. Bezoar stones. It sounded old, and mysterious, and nobody was quite sure what they were.

Mary looked at them properly. She broke some open. And inside she found small fish bones, and scales, and here and there the bones of little sea creatures. She thought about it. And she wrote to Buckland with a plain and sensible suggestion. These were not stones at all. They were what the great sea creatures had left behind, after dinner.

He tested the idea, and found she was right. He gave them a new name, coprolites. He read a paper about them. And he told the learned gentlemen who had worked it out. And so the ancient sea gained one more small, ordinary detail. Its creatures had eaten fish, and gone on their way.

There is a friend to meet now, in a house up the hill.

Her name was Elizabeth Philpot. She had come to Lyme with her sisters, some years before. She loved the beach almost as much as Mary did. She was twenty years older. She collected fossil fish, and kept them in careful drawers, and the learned men wrote to her about them. And the two women walked the shore together, and talked, and swapped what they knew.

Her fish were famous, in their quiet way. A great naturalist from Switzerland came to Lyme in 1834 to study them. The two women showed him more than thirty kinds, and told him which was which, with complete certainty. He went home and wrote it all down. And he named one of the fish after her.

When Mary found the fossil ink sacs, it was Elizabeth who had the idea. She ground a little of the dark stuff to a powder. She mixed it with water. And it became ink again. Brown-black, and smooth, and perfectly good to draw with.

So they drew. Ammonites. Fish. The skull of an ichthyosaur, in fine brown lines, done in the ink of a creature that had swum beside it. The drawings went off in letters to friends in Oxford and London. And for a while it was a small fashion in the town. Artists came and asked for fossil ink.

One of those drawings still exists. It went in a letter to Oxford, to the professor's wife, in the early 1830s. The skull of an ichthyosaur, in soft brown lines, on a small sheet of paper. It has not faded. Ink that old does not seem to.

Think of that for a moment, if you like. Ink that had waited in the dark for two hundred million years. Then, one winter afternoon, a woman added a little water, and dipped a pen, and it flowed.

Let us walk up into the town now, to the shop.

By 1826 Mary had earned enough from the beach to buy a house on Broad Street. It is the main street of the town, climbing steeply from the sea. In summer it was busy with visitors. The house had a glass window at the front, which was still rather a fine thing then. She put up a sign. Anning's Fossil Depot.

In the window lay an ichthyosaur, laid out whole on its slab, so that people walking up the hill would stop. The local paper wrote about it, and called it a fine specimen. And inside, on shelves and tables, were the smaller things. Ammonites by the dozen, polished and unpolished. Thunderbolts in a bowl. Little stars in a box. Slabs of fish. A jaw. A paddle. A tray of the strange spiralled stones, now with their new and honest name.

It was an ordinary shop, in the end. People came in out of the sun. They picked things up, and turned them over, and asked what they were. And she told them, plainly, and set a fair price, and wrapped the thing in paper. A small price for a small ammonite. A good deal more for a fine one. A summer visitor went home with a piece of an old sea in a pocket.

And in the back, the tools, and the lamp, and the next slab, waiting.

Some of what left that shop went a very long way.

A professor from Oxford came, and bought, and wrote his papers. A young geologist who drew, and who had grown up nearby, came often. He sat with her on the beach and asked what she thought, and listened. A gentleman came from Paris in the summer of 1824, sent by the great anatomist. He went home with a plesiosaur for the museum there. Another came in 1827, buying for a society in New York.

In Bristol, a new museum was being started. Two of her friends bought its very first fossil from her, in 1823, and gave it to the collection. So the first thing on the first shelf came from this beach.

Her ichthyosaurs went to the British Museum, and to Bristol, and to Oxford. Her plesiosaur went to the Geological Society, and later to the museum in London, where you can still see it. Her pterosaur went to the same place. In every case the thing was found, and freed, and cleaned, and drawn, on this beach, by this woman.

In 1829 she went to London herself, for the first time, as the guest of friends. She walked through the museum and saw her own creatures, laid out under glass, with the crowds moving past. It was a long way from the wet shale. And it was the same stone.

There is a picture to look at, before we go back down to the water.

In 1830 the young geologist who drew, Henry De la Beche, sat down and painted the old sea. Not the bones. The living creatures. He called the picture Duria Antiquior, which is Latin for a more ancient Dorset. As far as anyone knows, it was the first picture ever made of a world before people. Drawn from the fossils themselves, and from what the fossils could tell.

In it, the water is green and clear. Ichthyosaurs move through it with their great eyes open. A plesiosaur lifts its long neck above the surface. Pterosaurs cross the sky on their folded-finger wings. Ammonites drift in the shallows. Belemnites dart. Crinoids sway on the floor. Strange trees stand along a low shore. Almost everything in it had come out of these cliffs. Much of it through Mary's hands.

He had prints made from it, by a professional artist, and sent them out to his friends. The picture hung in studies and museums for years. People stood in front of it, and looked, and saw the sea she had been walking beside all along.

There is a small thing to wonder about, before the light comes.

There is an old tongue-twister, which you almost certainly know. About a woman who sells seashells, down by the seashore. People say, sometimes, that it is about Mary Anning. That she was the woman with the shells, and that the words were made for her.

It is a lovely idea. And nobody can say for certain. The twister is older than the song that made it famous. It was already in print in the 1870s, in a family magazine. And the song, which came along in 1908, does not name her. So the honest answer is that we do not know, and probably never will.

Let it be a wondering, then, and not a fact. A girl on a grey beach, with a basket full of shells that were also stones. A rhyme that everybody knows, with a name that nobody can quite attach to it. Both of them by the seashore. That is close enough for tonight.

Come back down to the beach, now. The light is changing.

Out over the water, the grey has gone thin, and behind it there is a line of pale gold. The sea has begun to take the colour. Just the tops of the small waves, first. Then the flat pools between the ledges, one by one, as the light finds them. The wet stone stays dark. But every pool on it turns to gold.

And on the far ledge, where the water is coming back, a heron is standing. Grey, and tall, and perfectly still. One foot in the water. It has been there all along, perhaps. It is watching the shallows for its breakfast, with the patience of a thing that has nowhere else to be.

Behind you, up the hill, a lamp is lit in a shop window. It is a small yellow square in a grey street. Someone has come down early. They are sitting at the table by the window, with a slab of stone and a brush. Working a bone free from the shale. A tap. A pause. A tap.

The heron does not move. The lamp does not flicker. The tide comes in over the ledges, a finger's width at a time.

And the beach goes quiet.

The tide is covering the stone again. Pool by pool, ledge by ledge. The ammonites at your feet are under water now. The little stars. The thunderbolts. Whatever lay uncovered this morning is being gently put back, for a while, under the sea that made it.

The cliff stands where it has always stood. Band on band, pale and dark, holding its long clock quietly. It is not in a hurry. It has been letting go of a little mud each winter for a very long time. It will go on doing that, patiently, after everyone who ever walked this beach has gone home.

Somewhere in it, tonight, there are creatures nobody has seen. Coiled shells. Long necks. Wide wings. Lying in the dark, side by side, exactly as they settled. They have waited two hundred million years. They can wait until morning.

So let it be quiet now.

Let the tide come in over the ledges. Let the heron stand where it stands. Let the lamp burn in the window on Broad Street, and the brush move, and stop, and move. Let the cliff hold its pages closed.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the names, not the dates, not the shape of a single bone from the whole grey morning. It will all keep itself, as it has for two hundred million years. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, like fine mud on the floor of a warm sea. Let the gold of the water fade. Let the cool of the wet stone come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
