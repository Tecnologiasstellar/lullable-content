Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is over now. It is done with. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing find its own slower pace. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are going somewhere warm and wooden and very old. We are going to stand under the eaves of a temple in Japan, and look up at how it is put together.

There is no metal in it. No nails, no screws, no brackets. It is wood, cut so precisely that it holds itself.

Some of the wood we will look at has been standing there for thirteen hundred years.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

It is early morning, and the light is still grey.

You are standing in a wide courtyard of raked gravel. The gravel is pale and slightly damp, and it makes a soft sound underfoot, somewhere between a crunch and a sigh.

There is mist sitting low over the ground. The air is cold enough to feel in your nose.

Around the edges of the courtyard there are pines. There is a low white wall with a tiled top. And beyond it, a hill that has not woken up yet.

There is a smell here that is very particular. It is wood. A dry, clean, slightly sweet smell. A little like lemon, and a little like a cupboard that has been closed a long time.

Somewhere off to the side, water is running into a stone basin. Nothing else is making any noise at all.

And in front of you there is a building.

Walk towards it, slowly, and then stop just before the steps and look up.

The first thing you notice is the roof. It is enormous — far bigger than the walls underneath it, and it hangs out over the courtyard by several metres on every side.

It curves. Not sharply. The line of it sags gently in the middle and lifts at the corners. The whole roof seems to be resting rather than sitting.

The second thing you notice is what is holding it up out there.

Between the tops of the columns and the underside of the eaves, there is a great deal going on. Blocks. Arms. Layers of shaped wood, stacked and stepping outward, one on top of the next. Each one reaches a little further than the one below.

It looks complicated. It is complicated. But every piece of it has a job, and none of it is decoration.

Step under the eaves now, out of the open air, and stand where the roof is over your head.

Here is the thing about this building that takes a while to believe.

There are no nails in it.

Not hidden ones. Not a few, in the difficult places. None.

Every joint you can see, and every joint you cannot, is made of wood shaped to fit into other wood. Tenons into mortises. Interlocking cuts, keys, wedges, laps.

The tradition has a name for it. Kigumi — wood joining, more or less. There are well over a hundred named joints in it, and a good carpenter knows most of them by feel.

The building is not fastened together. It is fitted together. Every piece is cut to hold the pieces around it. The whole thing stands on nothing but shape, and gravity, and friction.

It would be easy to treat that as a flourish. Something done for pride, the hard way, because it could be.

It is not. It is the practical answer, and there are three good reasons for it.

The first is that wood moves. It takes in moisture in the summer and gives it up in the winter. So it swells and shrinks a little, every year, forever. Metal does not. Put an iron nail in a beam. That beam has four hundred more years of breathing to do, and the wood will work itself loose around it.

The second is rust. Iron in contact with damp wood corrodes, and as it corrodes it expands, and it splits the timber it was meant to hold.

The third is repair. A joint that is only shape can be taken apart. Drive the key out, ease the pieces open, replace the one that has gone soft, and put it back. A nailed building cannot be undone. This one can — and, as we will see, that turns out to be the entire point.

Let us look at one joint properly, because the cleverness of them is quite satisfying.

Suppose you need a beam longer than any tree you have. You have to join two beams end to end, and the join has to be as strong as the wood around it.

So you cut each end into a set of interlocking steps, like two hands with fingers laced. Then you cut a slot across the middle of the join, and drive a hardwood key down into it.

The key pulls the two halves tight against each other. The interlocking faces stop them sliding apart. The more the beam is loaded, the more firmly it grips itself.

From below, once it is in, you can barely see it. A faint line across the underside of a beam, and that is all.

And if it is ever needed, the key can be driven back out, and the beam comes apart into two pieces again.

The wood matters as much as the cutting, and there is one they prefer above all others.

It is called hinoki. A cypress, straight-grained, pale, with a warm pink-cream colour when it is freshly cut.

It is what you have been smelling since you arrived. That clean lemony sweetness is the resin in it. It is still coming off timber that was shaped more than a thousand years ago.

Carpenters will tell you something about hinoki that sounds like a story and turns out to be measurable. Cut and properly dried, it does not begin weakening. For the first two hundred years or so it actually gets a little stronger, and stiffer, as the resins in it set.

Only after that does it start a very slow decline. And the decline is gentle. After a thousand years it is roughly back to the strength it had on the day it was felled.

A material that spends its first two centuries improving. It is a good match for a building nobody intends to finish.

There was a carpenter named Tsunekazu Nishioka. He worked on these temples for most of the twentieth century, and taught a good deal of what is now known.

He said something about timber that I have thought about a lot since.

Do not buy wood, he said. Buy the mountain.

What he meant is that a tree carries its whole life in its grain. A tree that grew on a windy northern slope has grown dense and tight on one side. A tree from a sheltered valley is even all round. A tree that leaned has been quietly resisting that lean for a century.

So when the timber comes to the building, it is placed according to where it grew. North-slope wood goes on the north side, where it will meet the same weather it grew up in. A trunk that spent its life pushing back against a lean is set so that it goes on pushing in a useful direction.

You are not assembling material. You are re-arranging a hillside into a building, with each tree still doing roughly the job it was doing before.

Now let us say where we are, because the numbers here are genuinely remarkable.

The oldest of these buildings stands at a temple called Hōryū-ji, in the flat country near Nara.

The main hall and the pagoda there are the oldest wooden buildings anywhere in the world. They date from the late seventh and early eighth centuries.

Thirteen hundred years. Roughly.

The columns holding up that hall were standing trees on a Japanese hillside. That was a time when almost nothing you can name had happened yet. They were cut, and shaped, and set upright, and they have been standing upright ever since.

Not preserved behind glass. Standing. Holding up a roof. Doing the job.

Walk up to one of those columns and put your hand flat on it.

It is not a cylinder. Run your hand up and down and you will feel it.

The column is narrower at the top than at the bottom, which you would expect. But it also swells outward slightly around the middle, and then draws back in again.

The swell is gentle. A few centimetres over several metres of height. You would not see it unless somebody told you it was there, and then you would never stop seeing it.

The reason is your eye. A perfectly straight tapering column, seen from below, appears to pinch inward at the waist. The swelling cancels that out, and the column reads as straight and solid.

The Greeks did the same thing to their stone columns, for the same reason. A very long way from here, and quite a lot earlier.

Two traditions, no contact between them, both noticing that the eye lies in the same particular way, and both quietly correcting it.

The surface under your hand is worth staying with for a moment.

It is smooth, but not flat. There are long shallow undulations in it, very slight, running up the grain.

The wood is a soft grey-brown on the outside where the weather has got at it. In the sheltered places under the eaves it is still pale.

Where hands have rested — at the height a hand naturally falls, all the way round — it is darker and slightly polished.

That is thirteen centuries of people standing exactly where you are standing, putting a hand out for no particular reason.

The grain stands slightly proud. The softer summer wood has weathered away a fraction faster than the hard winter growth. So the annual rings have come up in low relief, like the contours on a map.

You can count them with a fingertip.

Go and stand at the foot of the pagoda, and look at how it meets the ground. This is where it gets strange.

A five-storey pagoda has a single enormous pillar running up its centre, from the ground to the very top. It is called the shinbashira. It looks exactly like the thing that holds the whole tower up.

It holds up almost nothing.

The weight of the building is carried by the columns around it — an outer ring and an inner set. The central pillar passes up through the middle of all that, largely unattached to it.

And at the bottom, it is not fixed to anything either. It simply rests on a foundation stone. Set down on it, like a vase on a table.

The tallest, heaviest, most central member of the structure is standing there loose.

That looseness runs through the entire building, and it is completely deliberate.

The floors of a pagoda are not one continuous frame. Each storey is essentially its own box, stacked on the one below and resting there.

The columns are not driven into the ground. They stand on flat stones, on the surface. The building rests on the earth rather than being attached to it.

The ground in Japan moves from time to time. And when it does, this arrangement does something rather graceful.

Each storey slides a little against its neighbours, and they do not all go the same way at once. The lower one leans left while the one above it leans right, and the one above that leans back again.

The whole tower moves like a slow snake standing on its tail, passing the motion up and out of itself, storey by storey. Nothing has to resist anything. It simply gets out of the way and comes back.

A rigid building has to be strong enough to refuse. This one is built to agree.

Come back under the eaves, and look up again at those stacked blocks.

They are called bracket sets, and the reason they exist is rain.

The walls are wood. Wooden walls last a very long time if they stay dry, and not very long at all if water runs down them. So the roof has to overhang far enough to keep the rain off — metres out from the wall, all the way round.

But you cannot simply extend a beam that far. It would sag under the weight of the roof, and the leverage at the wall would be enormous.

So instead they build outward in steps. A block sits on the column. An arm sits on the block, reaching out a little. Another block sits on the end of that arm. Another arm, reaching further. And so on, four or five layers up and out.

Each layer only has to carry the short reach of the one above it. The load comes down the staircase of brackets and gathers itself neatly onto the top of the column.

It is a cantilever built out of small honest pieces, none of which is doing anything heroic on its own.

The roof itself is worth climbing up to look at, so let us do that.

Many of these roofs are tiled. But the older and quieter ones are covered in cypress bark. The way that is done is my favourite part of the whole business.

The bark is stripped from living trees. Not felled ones. Living, standing trees. Specialists climb them with a blunt spud and peel the outer bark upward, in long sheets.

Only the outer layer comes off. The inner bark, the part the tree actually needs, is left completely alone. The tree carries on growing. In about ten years the outer bark has come back, and the same tree can be harvested again.

The same trees, over and over, for generations.

Then the bark is trimmed into thin strips and laid on the roof in layers. Thousands upon thousands of them, overlapping. They are built up until the covering is several centimetres thick.

They are held down with small bamboo pins, driven in by hand. A roofer holds a mouthful of the pins and works through them steadily, tapping, all day.

The finished surface is soft-edged and slightly furred, and the curve at the eaves is cut by eye. From a distance it looks less like a roof and more like something the building grew.

The tools deserve a mention, because one of them came back from the dead.

Before there were pull planes, the tool for finishing a surface was a yariganna — a spear plane. It is essentially a long blade like a leaf on a handle, and you draw it along the wood, shaving as you go.

It does not leave a flat machine surface. It leaves a series of shallow scoops, like the inside of a spoon, running along the grain. In low light you can see them.

By the twentieth century, hardly anyone could use one. The tool had been replaced centuries earlier and the skill had gone quiet.

Then restoration work at the old temples ran into a problem. New pieces finished with a modern plane looked wrong beside seventh-century pieces finished with a spear plane. Too flat. Too even.

So carpenters and smiths worked out how to make the tool again, and how to use it. They did it from the marks left on the ancient columns. The wood taught them the tool.

Now the replacement pieces carry the same shallow scoops as the originals. In a hundred years nobody will be able to tell which was which.

Two more tools, and then we will leave the workshop alone.

The first is a pot of ink with a line wound inside it. The line comes out through a wad of ink-soaked wadding. You stretch it across a timber, lift it, and let it go.

It snaps down and leaves a perfectly straight black line, dead thin, along the whole length of the wood.

Every cut on the building begins as one of those lines. Carpenters used to make their own ink pots, and carve them. An old one is a lovely object. Worn smooth, and stained black at the mouth.

The second is a flat steel square, bent at a right angle, with markings along both arms.

On the front it is an ordinary rule. On the back, the scale is stretched. Every unit on that side is the diagonal of a square whose side is one unit on the front.

Which means that if you measure a beam with the back of the square, you are reading its diagonal directly. No arithmetic. No square roots done in the head on a cold morning.

A whole piece of geometry, folded into the markings on a tool, so that the answer comes off the wood already solved.

Learning all this takes a long time, and it is taught in a way that would frustrate most of us.

An apprentice arrives and does not touch a temple for years.

The first job is sharpening. You sharpen your blades, on water stones, over and over. The goal is an edge on a chisel that will pare the end grain of a cypress board cleanly.

That alone can take a couple of years. Then you make your own handles. Then you are allowed to cut joints on scrap, and to take them apart, and to see where they were slack.

Very little of it is explained. The master works, and you watch, and you try, and your work is looked at and mostly not commented on.

It sounds harsh. What it is doing is putting the knowledge into your hands rather than your head. That is where it has to live. A joint that is right feels right going together. There is no way to be told that.

Let us step inside the hall for a moment, quietly.

It is much darker in here. The windows are small and high, and most of the light comes in low through the open doors behind you.

It is cool, and very still, and the air does not move.

The floor is worn boards, and they are cold. Your footsteps sound different in here — closer, wrapped up, with almost no echo. All that wood absorbs sound rather than throwing it back.

Above you the ceiling is a coffered grid of dark timber. And above that, out of sight, is the whole hidden structure of the roof.

There is the smell of old wood again. Under it is something faintly bitter and sweet. That is centuries of incense, held in the beams.

Nobody is here. Nothing is happening. It is simply a large calm wooden room that has been holding its shape for a very long time.

And now the idea that runs underneath all of this, which is at a shrine some way from here, at Ise.

The main buildings there are not old. They are never old.

Every twenty years they are taken down and built again, from new timber, exactly as before. Every peg, every post, every join, in the same way, to the same dimensions.

This has been done sixty-two times. The tradition runs back thirteen hundred years.

There are two identical plots of ground side by side. The shrine stands on one of them. Over several years, a new one is built on the other, complete, before anything is touched.

Then the sacred objects are carried across at night, from the old building to the new one. And the old building is carefully dismantled.

Its timbers are not discarded. They go out to other shrines all over the country, for gates and halls and repairs. They carry on being buildings somewhere else.

That cycle is running right now, as it happens.

The sixty-third rebuilding began in twenty twenty-five, with the first ceremonies for the timber. It will finish in twenty thirty-three.

Which means that somewhere in Japan tonight, in a covered workshop, there are people preparing wood. It is for a building that will replace one currently standing, and perfectly sound.

Nobody is in a hurry about it. There are eight years for the work. Some of it is felling, some is drying, some is cutting joints, and a great deal of it is waiting.

The reason for the twenty years is the part that I find genuinely moving.

The buildings are not what is being preserved. The knowledge is.

A carpenter who works on one of these rebuilds will, if things go well, see three of them in a working life. Once as a young apprentice, fetching and watching and doing the simple parts. Once in the middle of their career, as one of the people actually responsible. And once at the end, as an old hand, standing back and telling the young ones what they saw the first time.

Twenty years is roughly the longest gap you can leave and still have someone in the room who has done it before.

Make it forty, and the chain breaks. Somebody would have to work it out again from drawings. And drawings do not contain the part that matters. The feel of the wood. How hard to drive a key. What a good joint sounds like when it seats.

So the interval is not a religious number, or not only. It is the length of human memory, measured out and built into a schedule.

That same attitude applies to the ancient buildings too, and it changes what "thirteen hundred years old" means.

These structures are not left alone for centuries. Every so often — every few hundred years — a building is completely taken apart.

Every piece is laid out, and looked at, and considered. The great majority are sound and go straight back in. A few have gone soft at an end, or split, and those are replaced with new wood, cut to the identical joint.

Then it all goes back together, and stands for another few centuries.

So a building of that age is not entirely original timber, and it is not a copy either. It is something in between, which we do not really have a word for.

It is more like a very slow river than a monument. The shape stays. The water is gradually exchanged. It is unmistakably the same thing throughout.

And the joints are what makes that possible. Because nothing was ever nailed, everything can always be opened, checked, and closed again.

Let us come back down to the courtyard, and let the evening arrive.

The light has gone amber. The mist has burned off and come back. The gravel has dried out and is paler than it was this morning.

In a workshop at the edge of the grounds someone is still working. There is a low rhythmic sound of a plane being drawn along a board, and then a pause, and then again.

Long curled shavings are collecting on the floor around them. They are thin enough to see light through.

The smell of hinoki is much stronger in there. Warm, clean, sweet, faintly sharp.

The piece being shaped will be fitted into a building in a few months' time. It will probably still be doing its job in four hundred years. The person making it knows both of those things, and is not making a fuss about either.

Now let us simply stand for a while and look at the hall in the last of the light.

It is not doing anything. It has not done anything in thirteen hundred years. It has stood there, held up its own roof, and let the weather go past.

The eaves are dark. The gravel is grey. The columns are worn smooth at the height where hands have touched them.

There is no wind. The water is still running into the stone basin, at the same rate it was this morning. It will still be doing that tomorrow.

Nothing here needs finishing. Nothing here is waiting to be resolved. It was all worked out a very long time ago, by people who were careful, and it has been quietly working ever since.

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the joints, or the loose pillar, or the bark peeled from living trees. Not the eight years of unhurried work going on tonight, in a covered workshop.

It is all there anyway, whether or not anyone is thinking about it this evening. It was there long before tonight, and it will be there afterwards. Taken apart and put back together, gently, for as long as anyone keeps the habit.

Let the words go. Let the picture soften and drift apart.

There is nothing out there that needs you tonight. There is nothing here that does either.

Only old wood, fitted carefully together, holding still in the dark.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
