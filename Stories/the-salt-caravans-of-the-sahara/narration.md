Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders drop, away from your ears. Let the bed take your weight, all of it. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to walk, very slowly, with a caravan of camels across the desert. Under the stars, in the cool of the night, on a road that people have walked for seven hundred years, and walk still. They are carrying salt to a city of scholars. Nobody hurries on this road. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin at dusk, in the open desert, a long way north of a river.

The sand around you is pale and cool, and it goes on in every direction, in long low waves. The sun has just gone down behind the western dunes. The sky above it is the color of apricots. Above that it is green. And above that it is already a deep, soft blue. The air, which was warm an hour ago, is turning cool against your face.

A camp is being made. It looks as a camp on this road looked seven hundred years ago, and as one looks tonight. Very little here has changed.

A small fire is burning in a hollow in the sand. It is fed with a few dry sticks and a little dried camel dung, and it burns low and orange and steady. Beside it kneels a man with a small blackened teapot. He is making tea, and he is in no hurry about it.

The tea is made three times, from the same leaves. The first glass is strong, and a little bitter. The second is sweeter. The third is gentle, and light, and mostly sugar and warmth. He pours from high up, in a long thin stream, so the tea froths in the glass. Then he pours it back into the pot. Then he pours it again. The sound is a soft, thin trickle. That is all there is to hear.

Someone hands you a glass. It is very small, and very hot, and you hold it by the rim. The steam smells of mint and smoke. Around you, in the failing light, a few other people sit with their glasses, saying very little. The day's heat is leaving the sand. You can feel it going.

§

Beyond the fire, the camels are resting.

There are perhaps forty of them tonight. They lie in a loose ring around the camp, each one folded down onto the sand. A camel lies down in a particular way. It kneels first on its front legs. Then it folds its back legs under. Then it settles onto a pad of thick skin on its chest, and tucks its legs in beneath it, and it is down. From there it can rest for hours without moving.

Beside each camel lie its loads. Long, pale slabs, stacked two and two. Coils of rope. A few sacks. Everything is set down in order, so that in the dark, later, each thing can be found by touch.

The camels chew. Slowly, from side to side, with their eyes half closed. Every so often one sighs, a long low breath through soft nostrils. One of them wears a bell, a small bell on a cord at the neck. When she shifts her weight it gives a single dull note. Then it is quiet again.

The smell of the camp is warm and dry. Camel hair, wood smoke, the sweetness of dates. And behind all of it, the clean mineral smell of the salt.

§

The stars have come out now, all at once, the way they do in the desert.

There is no moon yet. The sky is thick with light from edge to edge. The band of the Milky Way runs across it like a spill of flour. It is bright enough to see by. Bright enough to cast a faint shadow on the sand.

This is when the caravan walks. In the heat of the day it rests. In the cool of the evening, and through the night, and in the hours before dawn, it moves. The people who walk this road have done it this way for a very long time.

The fire is covered over. The tea things are packed. One by one, the camels are loaded, and one by one they rise. A camel stands up back legs first, so it tips forward as it rises. Then the front legs come up. And it is standing, tall and calm, with the load settled across its back.

They are tied nose to tail, in a long line. A rope runs from the halter of one to the saddle of the one in front. The guide walks at the head. He does not ride. He walks, with a stick, and the first camel follows him, and the rest follow her.

And then the whole line is moving. Slowly. Very slowly. A camel walks at about the pace of a person, four kilometers in an hour, no more. The feet make almost no sound on the sand. A soft press, and a soft release. Press, and release. Forty camels, and the desert takes the sound and gives back nothing.

§

Let us walk beside the guide for a while, and watch how he finds the way.

There is no road here. Nothing marked. The sand is the same pale sand in every direction, and the dunes lie in long low ridges that all look alike. And yet he never hesitates. He has walked this road since he was a boy, and his father walked it before him.

By night, he steers by the stars. He knows which ones rise where, and at what hour, and in which month. He keeps a certain bright star over the left shoulder of the lead camel. When it climbs too high, he takes another. The sky turns above him all night long, slowly, like a great wheel, and he turns with it.

By day, he reads the sand. The wind here blows mostly from one quarter. It leaves a grain on the surface, little ripples all running the same way. He can read that grain the way you might read the lines on your own palm. The dunes, too, lean in a known direction, steep on one side and gentle on the other. Even the color of the sand tells him something. Paler here, redder there. He has seen it all before.

And every so often, he stops. He crouches. He takes a little sand between his fingers and lets it run. Then he stands, and looks once at the sky, and walks on. Nothing is said. The line follows.

§

Let us think, as we walk, about where the salt comes from.

A very long time ago, when the desert was not a desert, there was a lake here. A wide shallow lake, in a green land, with reeds at its edges. The rains changed. Slowly, over thousands of years, the lake dried. As the water went, it left behind everything it had carried. And what it had carried, mostly, was salt.

So there is a place in the desert, far to the north of here, where the ground is white. A flat pan, wider than a city, glittering a little in the low sun. And under the crust, in layers, lies the salt of that ancient lake. Layer on layer, pressed hard by time, each one a little different from the one above.

Salt is a simple thing. Two elements, sodium and chlorine, locked together in a tiny cube. The cubes stack, and stack, and stack, until they are a rock you can stand on. Break it, and it breaks along the same straight lines, because the cubes are still there inside, all the way down.

The place is called Taghaza. It lies more than seven hundred kilometers north of the river. Later, the diggers moved to another pan not far off, called Taoudenni, where the salt lay even better. But the work was the same. It was patient work, done with simple tools, by people who knew exactly where to look.

§

Picture the salt pit, then, in the cool of the morning.

The diggers open a square in the ground, a few paces on each side. First they take off the topsoil, a reddish clay, about chest deep. Under that come the first layers of salt, rough and grayish, not good enough to sell. Those are set aside. And under those, at last, lie the good layers, three of them, one above the other, pale and clean and hard.

The digging is slow, and cool, because the pit is below the ground. A man works down on his knees, with a short bar and a pick. He does not smash the salt. He finds the line where one layer meets the next, and he works along it, gently, until a sheet comes free.

Then he trims it. He squares the edges with the flat of a blade. He makes it a long, even slab. About as long as your arm and a half. As wide as your forearm is long. As thick as three fingers laid together. It is almost white. It rings faintly when tapped. Held up to the light, the thin edge lets a little of the light through, like ice.

Each slab weighs about thirty kilograms. Some a little more. Sometimes the diggers cut them larger, in great heavy sheets, and then a camel carries only two. But the common slab, the one that goes south by the thousand, is the small one. Four to a camel.

And at the edge of the pit, when the day's cutting is done, the slabs are stacked in the sun to dry. Rows of them. Pale and quiet and waiting.

§

Now let us watch the loading, because it is a craft of its own.

A camel is couched, folded down onto the sand. On its back is a pack saddle, a frame of wood and padding. Over the frame goes a thick pad of woven grass, so nothing hard touches the animal's skin.

Two men work together. They lift one slab, and set it against one side of the frame, on end, with a rope looped around it. Then a second slab goes on the other side, to balance it. Then a third, and a fourth. Two and two. The weight has to be even. If it is not, the load will slip on the first dune, and the camel will know at once, and stop.

The ropes are of twisted palm fiber, or of camel hair. They are tied in knots that hold hard, but come loose with one pull. Every man in the caravan knows those knots in the dark.

When the load is tied, one of the men clicks his tongue. The camel groans, a long complaining sound. A camel always groans when it gets up, whether the load is heavy or not. And then it rises. Back legs, then front. And it stands, with a hundred and twenty kilograms of salt on its back. It looks about. It seems entirely unbothered.

They will carry it that way for two or three weeks. Thirty or forty kilometers in a day. And they will arrive with the salt just as they set out with it, white and square and whole.

§

The rhythm of the road settles into you after a day or two.

In the cool of the late afternoon the caravan is loaded and set walking. It walks through dusk, and into the dark. Somewhere in the deep of the night it stops. The loads come down. The camels fold themselves onto the sand. People sleep a while, wrapped in wool, close together, with the loads for a windbreak.

Before first light they are up again. The cold is sharpest then, a dry cold that gets into the fingers. There is tea, quickly, and a handful of dates. And the line moves off in the gray, and walks into the sunrise. It walks until the heat begins to press, in the middle of the morning.

Then it stops, and rests, through the hot hours. A cloth is stretched over sticks for shade. The camels are turned loose to find whatever dry grass there is. People sleep, or mend a rope, or say nothing at all. The desert is very quiet at midday. Even the wind lies down.

And in the late afternoon, when the shadows begin to lengthen, it starts again. Load, and rise, and walk. Every day the same. It is a rhythm the body learns and then stops noticing. Like breathing. Like the steps of the camels. Like the slow turn of the stars overhead.

§

There are wells along the way, and the caravan walks from one to the next.

A week out from the city there is a village called Araouane. A few low houses of sand-colored brick, half buried in the dunes. And a well. Deep, and old, and worked by hand.

The water is drawn up in a skin bucket, on a long rope. It comes up cool from the dark. It is poured into a trough of hollowed wood, and the camels are led to it a few at a time.

And here is something to sit with. A camel does not drink a little, often. It drinks a very great deal, all at once, and then nothing for days. A thirsty camel can take a hundred liters and more in a few minutes. You can hear it. A long, steady, hollow sound, like water going into a cask.

Then it lifts its head, and water runs from its soft lips, and it walks away. It has filled itself, and it is done.

The people fill their water skins. These are made from the whole skin of a goat, sewn tight. They hang from the saddles, and swing as the camels walk. The water inside tastes a little of the skin, and of the desert. After a day or two it tastes of nothing else. It is cool in the morning and warm by noon.

The well at Araouane is the hinge of the whole road. Everything is measured from it. So many days up to the salt. So many days down to the city. The guide thinks in wells, and not in kilometers.

And the line goes on, south, toward the river.

§

The caravan has its seasons, and it keeps to them.

It goes twice a year. Once in November, when the cool sets in and the nights are long. Once again in March, before the real heat arrives. In between, in the deep of summer, nothing moves on this road. The sand grows too hot to stand on by the middle of the morning. So the road simply rests, and waits, and everyone stays where they are.

The November run is the gentler one. After the rains at the desert's southern edge there is a little grazing left, a thin green haze on the flats. So the caravan starts slowly, and lets the camels eat as they go. They wander a few paces off the line, take a mouthful of thorn, and come back. The pace picks up later, when the grazing gives out, and there is nothing left to stop for.

In the old days the caravans were enormous. Hundreds of camels was ordinary. Thousands was not unheard of. There are accounts of columns that stretched for kilometers across the sand, nose to tail, at walking pace, taking half a morning to pass a single dune.

Think of standing beside that. You would hear it long before you saw it. First the bells, very faint. Then a low sound that is hard to place, which is many soft feet on sand at once. Then the first camels come around the dune, and go by, and go by, and keep going by. And then the sound thins out again, and the desert closes up behind them.

Tonight's caravan is a small one. Forty camels, and a handful of people, and one bell. It is enough.

§

Not everything on this road travels south. Let us think about what comes the other way.

The salt goes down to the river towns, where it is scarce. And from the river towns, other things come back up. Gold, first of all. Not in bars, but in dust, in small leather bags, tied tight. It comes from far to the south, from riverbeds where people wash it out of the gravel. A few grains at a time.

Grain comes north. Millet, mostly, in sacks. It grows in the river country. It feeds the diggers at the salt pits, who have nothing green of their own. Cloth comes north, woven in narrow strips and sewn together, dyed with indigo the color of the night sky. Leather comes north. Kola nuts, wrapped in damp leaves to keep them fresh.

And from the north, with the salt, come dates. Great baskets of them, sweet and dark and sticky, from the oases at the desert's far edge. Dates are traded for millet by volume, three measures of dates for two of millet. It is an old rate, and it hardly changes.

So the road is a kind of slow breath. Salt and dates go down. Gold and grain and cloth come up. Nobody who walks it will see the whole of it. Each caravan walks its own stretch, and hands the goods on, and turns for home.

§

Let us walk beside one camel for a while, and think about her body.

She is built for exactly this. The hump on her back is not water, as people often say. It is fat. Up to thirty-five kilograms of it, stored in one place, high up. There it does not wrap the body and hold in heat. When food is scarce, she draws on it. The hump softens, and leans a little to one side. When she eats well, it fills again.

Her feet are wide. Each one has two toes, joined by a broad leathery pad, about the size of a small plate. When she steps on sand the pad spreads. Her weight is carried on the whole of it, and she does not sink.

Her eyes have two rows of lashes, long and thick, to keep out the blowing sand. Her nostrils can close. She can shut them to a slit when the wind rises, and open them again when it drops. Her lips are soft and split, and she can eat a thorn bush that would cut your hand.

And she does something quieter still, inside. On a hot day she simply lets herself warm up. Her body temperature drifts, several degrees, from cool at dawn to warm by afternoon. A body that stores heat that way does not have to sweat it off. That is water saved, every hour of every day. At night she gives the heat back to the cold air, and begins the next morning cool again.

She can go many days without drinking. She can lose a great deal of her own weight in water, and walk on as though nothing had happened. Then she comes to a well, and takes it all back in ten minutes. No other large animal on earth does this so well.

And she is patient. Not stupid, as people say, but patient in a way that is hard to describe. She will walk all night at the same pace, and stop when asked, and fold down, and rise, and walk again. Somewhere inside her she keeps the whole long road, well by well. She has walked it more times than you have.

§

Someone did write this road down, in the fourteenth century, and it is worth a moment.

A traveler from Tangier, whose name was Ibn Battuta, had spent most of his life on the move. He had been to Mecca, and India, and China, and back. And near the end of his traveling, in the year 1352, he joined a caravan going south across the desert.

He set out from a trading town at the desert's northern edge. Twenty-five days later he came to the salt pan at Taghaza. He did not think much of the place. But he wrote down what he saw, and what he saw was strange enough to remember.

The houses there, he said, were built of salt. Blocks of it, cut from the ground, stacked into walls. The mosque was built of salt too. And the roofs were made of camel skins, stretched over the salt walls, because there was no wood to be had. Nothing grew there. The dates came in from the north. The millet came in from the south. The people lived on those, and on camel meat.

He noted how the salt was cut in thick slabs, and how a camel carried two of the great ones. And he noted the trade. Far to the south, where there was no salt at all, a weight of it could fetch the same weight in gold. He found this remarkable. So did everyone who heard it. That was the whole reason the road existed.

He walked on south, and came at last to the river, and to the court of the king of Mali. And he wrote it all down, in a book that people are still reading tonight.

§

And now, on the twentieth night or so, something changes on the horizon.

It is very faint at first. A darker line against the dark. The guide has seen it long before you have. And in the gray of the morning it comes clear. A city, low and wide, the color of the sand it stands on. Flat roofs, and here and there a taller shape, rising above them.

This is Timbuktu. It stands where the desert ends and the river country begins. A few hours' walk south of the last dunes, the great river bends toward it, and then away again. The city grew up exactly here because of that. It is where the camels stop and the boats begin.

The tallest shapes are mosques. The greatest of them was built in the year 1327, in the reign of Mansa Musa, a king of Mali. He had lately returned from a journey to Mecca. He brought back with him an architect from Granada, in Spain, a man named al-Sahili. And the two of them raised a great mosque of earth.

It is earth, all of it. Mud from the river, mixed with straw, shaped into bricks, and dried in the sun. Then plastered smooth. Wooden beams stand out from the walls in rows, like the rungs of a ladder. After each rainy season, people climb them and plaster the walls again by hand. So the building has been remade, a thin layer at a time, every year, for seven hundred years.

Inside, it is cool and dim. Twenty-five rows of pillars, and low arches, and the smell of dry earth. There is room for two thousand people to pray. And in the mornings, in the soft light, there are people reading.

§

Because this is a city of readers. That is what the salt paid for, in the end.

There are three great mosques here. A second one, called Sankoré, stands in the northern quarter. Around it, in the years after Mansa Musa, the scholars gathered. They taught in their own houses, a few students at a time, sitting on the floor. Law, and the movements of the stars. Arithmetic, and the settling of disputes. Medicine, and the plants of the desert, and which of them heal.

They wrote it all down. On paper. And the paper, too, had crossed the desert by camel. It was made in Italy, and in the south of France, in mills beside rivers. It was carried by ship to Cairo or Tripoli. And from there it was packed in bales and walked across the sand, the same way the salt was walked.

Hold a sheet of it up to the light and you can see the papermaker's mark inside the fiber. Three small crescent moons, in a row, in the Venetian style. That mark is in the books of Timbuktu to this day.

The books were copied by hand, in brown ink, in a beautiful rounded script. Some scribes wrote in Arabic. Some wrote their own languages in Arabic letters. The pages were kept loose, in leather wrappers, and stacked in wooden chests. Tens of thousands of them, in the end. In private houses, on shelves, in the dark, for hundreds of years.

And below the scholars, at the beginning of it all, were the children with their boards.

A student here learned to write on a flat wooden tablet. Mahogany, or baobab, smoothed and cut to about the size of a book. He wrote on it in a thick brown ink, a few lines at a time, and learned them by heart. Then he washed the board clean, and wrote the next lines. The same board, over and over, for years.

In the sixteenth century there were something like a hundred and fifty of these small schools in the city. In one of them, someone counted the boards. There were a hundred and twenty-three. A hundred and twenty-three children in one room, each with a wooden tablet on his knees, all reciting quietly at once.

The same boards are still cut and used in West Africa today. Baobab wood, and brown ink, and a rag, and a little water.

Think of that for a moment, as the camels stand outside the walls. Salt went out of the desert, and paper came into it, and the paper filled with words. That is the exchange, at its root. A rock from a dry lake, and a book of the stars.

§

Let us go down into the market, in the early morning, before it is hot.

The caravan has unloaded on the edge of the city, where the dunes run down to the houses. The slabs are stacked in a low wall. And now they are being sold, and carried, and cut.

Salt is cut with a small saw, and with a blade, into pieces. A whole slab for a merchant. A quarter slab for a household. A piece the size of your palm for a person who needs just a little. The cutting makes a fine dust that sits white on the seller's hands. He wipes them on his robe. He weighs the pieces in a brass scale against small polished stones.

Around him the market is waking up. Millet in baskets. Onions and dried fish. Bolts of indigo cloth, so dark they are nearly black, folded and stacked. Leather sandals. Baskets of dates. The smell of frying dough, and dust, and wood smoke, and the river not far away.

There are porters at the edge of the market who do nothing but carry salt. A whole slab goes up on the head, on a twist of cloth. They walk with it slowly, upright, with their hands loose at their sides. Two of them pass, and the slabs knock together softly. The sound is like two flat stones.

And that is where much of the salt is going. Eight kilometers to the south, at a place called Kabara, the city's boats lie on the water. Long wooden boats, painted at the prow, with a low roof of matting over the middle. When the river is high, the water comes right up to the port.

The slabs are carried down and laid flat in the bottom of the boats. Then the boats push off, and drift down the slow brown river, to the towns downstream. And the salt travels on, by water now, into the green country, where it has always been wanted.

§

And it is wanted. Let us think about why, gently, because it is the reason for all of this.

A body needs salt. Every body. A person in a hot country loses it in sweat, all day, and must take it back in. A pinch in the pot at evening is enough. But it has to come from somewhere.

Cattle need it too, and more than we do. Grass has hardly any. So the herders of the river country buy the rougher salt. The gray layers that no one would put in a pot. They set it out for their animals in the evening. The cows come and lick it, slowly, with their long tongues. Their coats stay glossy. Their milk keeps coming.

Salt keeps things. Fish from the river, split and salted, will last for months. Meat, rubbed with salt and dried in the shade, will last the whole dry season. Butter, salted, does not turn.

And salt was money. In the villages, small pieces of it passed from hand to hand for whatever was needed. A measure of grain. A length of cloth. A bit of salt for it, wrapped in a leaf. It was worth more than the same weight of iron. In some places, more than the same weight of gold. Simply because it was so far from where it lay.

That is why forty camels walked twenty nights across the sand. For something you keep in a jar by the stove.

§

Back in the desert now, for a moment, on the last night before the city.

It is the deep of the night, and the caravan has stopped. The camels are down. The people are asleep, in a row, wrapped in wool. The fire is a few red coals under gray ash.

At the edge of the camp, something is watching. A very small fox, the color of the sand, with enormous ears. Ears far too large for its face. Each one nearly as long as your hand.

This is a fennec. It is the smallest fox there is. It lives in a burrow under the dunes all day, and it comes out at night, when the sand is cool. Its ears are not only for hearing, though it hears very well. They are full of fine blood vessels, close under the skin. On a warm night, the heat of its body flows out through them and into the air. It is how such a small creature keeps cool. Two soft radiators, standing up in the starlight.

It has come for the crumbs. A bit of date. A fallen grain of millet. It moves without a sound, sniffing. Its ears turn this way and that. Its eyes shine faintly in the last of the firelight.

Then it is gone. Nothing moves. And the cold comes down.

The cold before dawn in the desert is a real thing. The sand has given up all its heat to the clear sky, and there is nothing to hold any warmth in. The people pull the wool tighter and sleep on. The camels do not mind. And overhead, the stars have wheeled all the way round. The ones that were rising when you lay down are setting now, low in the west.

§

So let it be quiet now. There is nothing left on the road to watch.

The salt has come to the city. The camels have been unloaded, and led out to the grazing. They are folded down on the sand, chewing, with their eyes half closed. The slabs are stacked in the market. The boats are waiting at the river. Everything is where it should be.

The fire in the camp has gone out. The tea glasses are washed and packed. The ropes are coiled. In a few days the caravan will load with grain, and cloth, and turn north again. It will walk back the way it came, under the same stars. But not tonight. Tonight it rests.

Let your breathing find the pace of the camels. Slow, and even, and soft. Press, and release. In, and out. Nothing to steer by. Nothing to carry.

§

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the wells, not the slabs, not the names, not the years. It will all keep itself, as it has for seven hundred years. Without any help from you tonight.

Let the desert go dark around you. Let the sand give up its warmth, slowly, to the sky. Let the camels sleep in their ring. Let the small fox go home to its burrow under the dune.

Let your thoughts settle, softly, like sand after wind. Let the last of the firelight fade. Let the cool of the night come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
