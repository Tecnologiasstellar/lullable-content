Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight. You do not have to hold yourself up any longer.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your jaw unclench. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for tonight. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going somewhere warm. Not far away, this time. Only across a small town, in the dark, to a room with the light on.

Somebody is already awake there. They have been awake for hours. And they are working, quietly, at something slow, that cannot be rushed and does not need you.

That is the whole of it. There is nothing here you must follow. If your attention drifts, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of.

So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us walk down to the corner.

§

It is a little after two in the morning.

The street is empty and the air is cold and still. Frost has come down on the parked cars. The lamps are on, spaced evenly, and each one holds a small circle of orange light on the wet pavement below it.

Every window on the street is dark. Every one, except a single low window near the corner, where the light is yellow, and warm, and steady.

Walk toward it. Your footsteps are the only sound.

As you come close, something changes in the air. You smell it before you reach the door. Warm flour. Toasted crust. Something faintly sweet, and faintly sour, underneath.

It is one of the oldest good smells there is. People have been walking toward it, in the cold, in the dark, for thousands of years.

The door is not locked. Push it gently, and step inside, and let it swing shut behind you.

§

The warmth meets you first.

It comes at you like something soft, pressing gently against your face and your hands. After the frost outside, it is almost overwhelming. Let your shoulders come down again.

The room is small. The walls are white tile, worn and slightly uneven. There is a long wooden bench down the middle, pale and scarred from many years of use, dusted lightly all over with flour.

Along one wall stand sacks of flour, folded over at the top. There are wooden trays. There are round baskets, stacked. There is a set of scales with a brass pan.

And at the far end stands the oven. It is large, and old, and made of brick, and it is not on yet. It fills that end of the room the way a sleeping animal fills a corner.

Fine flour dust hangs in the air, turning slowly in the lamplight. It never quite settles here. It has been in the air of this room for as long as the room has been a bakery.

There is no music. There is no radio. Just the hum of the cold room, somewhere behind you, and the sound of one person working.

§

The baker is at the bench, and she does not look up.

She is not hurrying. That is the first thing you notice about her. Every movement she makes is unhurried and slightly worn-in, the way movements become when they have been made many thousands of times.

She has a jar in front of her. It is an ordinary glass jar, with a cloth over the top, held on with an elastic band.

Inside it is something pale and thick, somewhere between a batter and a paste. It has risen up the sides. Its surface is domed, and slightly rough, and it is full of holes.

She takes the cloth off, and leans in, and smells it. Then she puts the cloth back.

This jar is the oldest thing in the building. Older than the oven. Older than the bench. It has been fed, every single day, for longer than she has been alive, and she was given it by somebody who was given it by somebody else.

Nothing in it is old, exactly. Every part of it has been replaced a thousand times over. It is old the way a river is old. It has simply never stopped.

§

Let us look inside the jar for a while, because what is happening in there is quietly astonishing.

It looks like paste. It is not. It is a city.

There are two kinds of resident. The first are wild yeasts. They are single cells, far too small to see, and they arrived in this jar the way they arrive anywhere, out of the flour and out of the air.

The second are bacteria. Lactobacilli. Also single cells, also invisible, and there are a great many more of them. In a settled starter like this one, the bacteria outnumber the yeasts by something like a hundred to one.

They are not competing. That is the lovely part. They are helping each other.

The flour is mostly starch. Starch is a long chain, and neither of them can eat it directly. But the flour also carries its own enzymes, and those enzymes patiently cut the starch into shorter pieces. Chiefly one called maltose.

Now, the bacteria are very good at maltose. The yeasts are not. The yeasts largely cannot use it at all.

So the bacteria take up the maltose. And in the process of using it, they leave glucose behind, out in the open, where the yeasts can reach it.

The bacteria eat the thing the yeasts cannot. And they hand the yeasts their supper as they go.

§

Each side pays the other back in a different currency.

The yeasts, feeding on that glucose, do what yeasts do. They give off carbon dioxide. Small bubbles of gas, released slowly, all through the paste. That is what has lifted it up the sides of the jar and left it full of holes.

The bacteria give off acids. Two of them, mostly. One is lactic acid, which is soft and round and mild, and tastes the way yoghurt tastes. The other is acetic acid, which is sharper, and is what vinegar is made of.

In a well kept starter the balance sits at roughly four parts of the soft one to one part of the sharp. That ratio is most of what people mean when they say a loaf tastes good.

And the acid does something else, beyond flavour. It makes the jar sour. Sour enough that most other things cannot live there.

So the starter protects itself. It is not kept clean by anybody. It keeps itself, simply by being slightly too sour for anything else to move in.

That is why the jar has lasted a hundred years on a shelf, in a warm room, with a cloth over the top, and nothing has ever gone wrong with it.

§

She feeds it now.

She pours most of it away, keeping only a spoonful. Then she adds fresh flour, and warm water, and stirs it with a wooden spoon until it is smooth.

It looks like nothing. A jar of pale paste, put back on the shelf near the oven.

But she has just given a hundred billion cells a fresh field. They will spend the next hours working through it, quietly, in the dark under the cloth, and by the time she needs them the jar will have risen again.

She will not check on it. She knows what it does.

§

Let us stay with the jar a moment longer, because of how old this is.

Bread made from ground grain and water is very old indeed. Older than writing. Older than farming, in some places.

For most of that time it was flat. Grain, water, heat. That was all.

And then, at some point, somebody left a bowl of it standing. Probably by accident. Probably overnight, in the warm.

When they came back it had swollen. It smelled sour and faintly alcoholic. It should, by any sensible judgement, have been thrown out.

Somebody baked it anyway. And it came out light, and soft, and better than anything they had eaten before.

Nobody knew why. Nobody would know why for thousands of years. There was no way to see a yeast cell, and no reason to imagine one existed.

So the knowledge was carried as habit instead. Keep back a piece of yesterday's dough. Put it into tomorrow's. Never let the chain break.

That is precisely what the jar on the shelf is. It is a piece of dough that was kept back, and kept back again, and never once allowed to stop.

Every loaf in this room tonight is descended, without a single gap, from a bowl somebody forgot to throw away.

§

Now, the flour and the water.

The flour came from a mill about forty miles away, in paper sacks, on a Tuesday.

Before that it was wheat, standing in a field. Each grain is a small hard seed, and it has three parts.

There is a tough outer coat, the bran, which is the husk. There is a tiny germ, the part that would have become the plant. And filling most of the rest is the endosperm, which is the seed's packed lunch: starch, and protein, laid down to feed the germ until it can reach the light.

Milling simply breaks that open. White flour is the endosperm alone, with the bran and germ taken away. Wholemeal is all three, ground together, which is why it is darker and heavier and goes stale sooner.

The proteins she needs are in the endosperm. The flavour is mostly in the bran and the germ.

She uses white flour with a little wholemeal stirred through it. Enough for taste. Not so much that the loaf goes dense.

None of this is decided tonight. It was decided years ago, and now it is simply what she does.

She lifts flour into the bowl, and it comes out of the sack in a soft pale fall, with a small cloud rising after it. She weighs it. She adds water, a little cooler than a bath.

Then she brings it together with one hand. Not kneading. Only turning it over, and folding it, until there is no dry flour left in the bowl.

It is rough, and lumpy, and unpromising. It looks like nothing anyone would want.

And then she covers it with a cloth, and walks away, and leaves it entirely alone for half an hour.

This is the part that surprises people. She does nothing. She wipes down the bench. She fills a jug. She stands for a moment with her hands on the bench and looks at the middle distance.

And in the bowl, unattended, the dough is already changing.

§

Here is what is happening while she waits.

Flour carries two proteins that matter. On their own, dry, they do nothing at all. But once water reaches them, they begin to unfold, and to find each other, and to join.

They link into long strands. The strands cross, and link again, and build slowly into a fine elastic net through the whole of the dough. That net is gluten.

She did not have to work it in. Water alone will do it, given a little time. All the kneading in the world only speeds up something the dough was going to do by itself.

So the wait costs nothing and does most of the work. When she comes back to the bowl the difference is visible. The rough lumpy mass has smoothed out. It is softer. It stretches instead of tearing.

Half an hour, and no hands at all.

§

Now she adds the starter, and the salt, and works them through.

She turns the dough over on itself. She presses it flat with the heel of her hand, and folds it, and turns it a quarter, and does it again. There is a rhythm to it. Press, fold, turn. Press, fold, turn.

It is not vigorous. It is steady. She could do it half asleep, and at this hour, perhaps she nearly is.

The dough tightens under her hands as she goes. It becomes smooth, and slightly glossy, and begins to pull itself back into a ball when she lets it be.

Then she covers it again, and leaves it in the warm corner near the oven, and lets it be for hours.

§

A word about the salt, because it is doing more than you would think.

Salt is for taste, obviously. A loaf made without it is strange and flat, and people notice immediately even if they cannot say why.

But it is also holding the whole night together.

Salt tightens the gluten net. The strands pull in a little closer, and the dough becomes firmer, and stronger, and better able to hold its gas. Dough made without salt is slack and sticky and hard to shape.

And salt slows the yeasts down. Not by much. Just enough.

That matters more than it sounds. If the yeasts run at full speed, the loaf rises fast and is finished in an hour or two. It will still be bread. But it will taste of very little, because the bacteria will not have had time to make anything.

Flavour needs hours. So the salt holds the yeasts back, gently, and buys those hours.

She adds it by eye now, though she weighed it carefully for years. It is about two spoonfuls for this much flour. A little under two parts in a hundred.

Rather less and the dough goes slack. Rather more and the yeasts are held back too hard, and the loaf never quite rises.

It is a narrow window, and it was worked out long before anybody knew what a yeast was, by people simply noticing what happened.

§

This is the long part of the night, and almost nothing happens in it.

The dough sits under its cloth. The yeasts inside it, spread all through it now, are quietly giving off gas, exactly as they did in the jar.

Each bubble is tiny. But the gluten net is holding them, everywhere, all at once. And so the whole mass slowly begins to inflate, from the inside, at a rate you could never see by watching.

Every hour or so she comes back and folds it. She reaches down one side, lifts, and lays it over the top. She turns the bowl and does it again. Four folds, and it is done, and she leaves it.

Each fold strengthens the net a little, and evens out the warmth. It takes her about twenty seconds.

In between, she cleans. She sets out the baskets and rubs flour into their cloth linings. She sits down on a stool by the oven with a mug of tea.

These are the emptiest hours, and she likes them.

There is no radio on. She tried one, years ago, and found she preferred the quiet.

So the sounds are only these. The hum of the cold room. The tick of the metal shelf as the warmth reaches it. The scrape of a scraper on wood. Her own footsteps on the tiled floor, from the bench to the sink and back.

She drinks her tea slowly. She rests her hands around the mug.

Nobody is going to telephone. Nobody is going to arrive. There is no part of this night that requires her to be quick or clever, and nothing at all that can be improved by worrying.

The dough does not care whether she is watching it. It will do the same thing either way.

There is a great deal of comfort in work like that. It asks for attention, but only a little, and only now and then. The rest of the time it simply asks you to be there.

The clock goes round. The street outside stays dark and empty. The dough rises so slowly that it seems to be doing nothing at all, and she does not once look at it with any anxiety.

§

By four o'clock the dough is ready, and it looks quite different.

It has almost doubled. It is domed and soft. When she lifts the cloth there are bubbles visible under the surface, some small, some the size of a coin.

She tips it out onto the floured bench, gently, and lets it come out under its own weight. It spreads a little, and sighs, and settles.

Then she divides it. She cuts it into pieces with a scraper, one clean stroke each, and weighs them, and adjusts.

Now she shapes. This is the one part of the night that looks like skill, because it is.

She takes a piece and folds the edges into the middle. Then she turns it over, cups her hands around it, and draws it toward herself across the bench, several times, in short movements.

Each pull tightens the outside of the ball a little more. The surface goes from slack to taut, and finally to smooth and drum-tight.

That skin is the whole point. It is what will hold the loaf up. Without it the dough would simply spread out flat and bake into a disc.

She turns each one over, and sets it into a floured basket, and covers it.

§

Then the baskets go into the cold.

She carries them to the little room at the back, where it is only a few degrees above freezing, and slides them onto a rack, and closes the door.

Cold does not stop the fermentation. It only slows it, right down, to a crawl.

And that slowness is a gift, because the yeasts and the bacteria slow at different rates. The yeasts nearly stop. The bacteria carry on, patiently, for hours in the dark.

Which means the loaf stops rising much, but goes on developing flavour all night. In the cold, doing nothing, it gets better.

She shuts the door on them and leaves them there.

There is nothing left to do now but wait, and warm the oven. So let us wait with her.

§

She lights the oven at half past five.

It is brick, and it holds heat the way a stone wall holds the sun. It takes a long time to come up, and once it is up it will stay there for hours.

The room, which was already warm, becomes properly warm. The tiles take it up. The air moves gently above the oven door.

She takes the baskets out of the cold, one at a time, and turns each loaf out onto a board. They come out smooth and pale and cool to the touch, with a faint spiral pattern pressed into the top by the cloth.

Then she takes a blade and cuts the top of each one. A single confident stroke, at a shallow angle, about a centimetre deep.

That cut is not decoration. It is an instruction. It tells the loaf where to open when it swells, so that it does not tear itself somewhere ragged along the side.

And then she slides them into the oven, and throws a cup of water onto the floor of it, and shuts the door.

§

The water turns to steam at once, and the steam matters enormously.

For the first few minutes, the loaf is surrounded by wet air. And wet air keeps the surface of the dough soft, and damp, and stretchy, instead of letting it dry and set hard.

Which buys the loaf time. Because inside, in the dark, the last and best thing is now happening.

The dough is warming from the outside in. And every yeast cell in it, feeling that heat, becomes suddenly and briefly frantic. They produce gas faster than they have all night.

At the same time, all the gas already trapped in the dough is expanding, because warm gas takes up more room. And the alcohol the yeasts made is boiling away into vapour, and adding to it.

So the loaf lifts. Quite quickly, and quite dramatically, in the first ten minutes or so. Bakers call it the oven spring.

It rises. The cut on top opens wide and curls back. And when the inside reaches about sixty degrees, every yeast cell in the loaf dies, all at once, having done the last of its work.

That is the end of the rising. Everything after this is setting.

§

Now the loaf becomes solid, from the inside out.

The starch granules, which have sat in the dough all night as small dry beads, take up water as they warm. They swell. They soften. And somewhere between about sixty and eighty degrees they open and set into a gel.

The gluten net has already given up its stretch by now and gone firm. Between them, they turn the soft airy dough into a fixed, permanent, honeycombed structure.

Whatever bubbles were in the loaf at that moment are the bubbles it will have forever. The crumb is now decided, and cannot be changed.

The middle of the loaf will not go much above a hundred degrees, because it is still full of water, and water will not go past its boiling point while it is still there.

So the inside is, in a sense, being steamed. Gently. Inside its own crust.

§

The outside is having a completely different night.

She opens the door about twenty minutes in and lets the steam out, and from here the surface of the loaf dries, and its temperature climbs.

Past a hundred. Past a hundred and twenty. And at somewhere around a hundred and forty, the crust starts to change colour.

This is the browning. Sugars and proteins on the dry surface begin to react together, and rearrange, and build into hundreds of new compounds that were not there before.

It is the same reaction that browns a roast potato, and toasts a marshmallow, and gives coffee its smell. It only happens on dry surfaces, and only in real heat, which is why a boiled thing never browns.

Those new compounds are where nearly all the aroma comes from. Not the flour. Not the yeast. The crust, made in the last half hour.

Hotter still, and the sugars themselves begin to caramelise, and the colour goes from pale gold to amber to deep chestnut brown.

The smell in the room becomes enormous. It goes out under the door, and into the cold street, where nobody is yet awake to notice it.

§

She takes them out a little after seven, and sets them on a wire rack.

And now, if you stand close, you can hear them.

The crust is very hot and very dry, and the crumb inside is cooler and still damp. As the loaf cools, the crust contracts, and the steam inside moves outward through it.

So it crackles. Softly. A fine irregular ticking, like a small fire a long way off, or like rain starting on a roof.

Bakers say the loaf is singing. It goes on for a few minutes and then it stops.

She leaves them there and does not cut one. They are still setting inside, and a loaf cut hot is a loaf slightly wasted, and she has known that since she was young.

She wipes the bench. She scrapes the last of the flour into her hand. She hangs the cloth over the rail.

§

Outside, the sky has begun to go grey at the edges.

The frost on the cars is thinning. A light comes on in a window across the street. Somewhere further off, a van door closes.

The loaves stand in a row on the rack, dark gold, ticking quietly, cooling in their own good time.

And the jar sits on the shelf by the oven with its cloth on, risen again, fed and quiet, waiting for tomorrow. It will be here long after tonight. It has outlasted everybody who has ever kept it.

The shop opens at eight, and she will not be here for it.

Somebody else will come and unlock the front, and carry the loaves through, and set them in the window while they are still faintly warm. And the first customer will come in at about ten past.

They will not know about the jar. They will not know that the dough was mixed at three in the morning, or that it spent the night in the cold, or that the crust was made in the last thirty minutes and everything before that was patience.

They will buy a loaf, and hold it under one arm, and go home. Which is exactly right. None of it was ever meant to be noticed.

That is a good kind of work. It leaves the room and goes out into the street and disappears into somebody's ordinary morning.

She puts on her coat. She turns off the light over the bench. And she goes home, at half past seven in the morning, to sleep.

§

So let it be quiet now.

Let the oven cool. Let the loaves stand in the dark room and go on ticking, softer and softer, until they stop. Let the flour dust settle slowly through the empty air.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the temperatures, not the names, not one single thing from the whole warm night. It will all keep itself.

Somebody was awake, and working gently, and it had nothing to do with you. That is a good thing to fall asleep on.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let your hands grow heavy. Let the warmth of that room settle over you, and through you, soft and dark and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
