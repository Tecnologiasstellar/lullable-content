# Research sources — Raising the Stones of Stonehenge

https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/history-and-stories/building-stonehenge/ — first earthwork enclosure c. 3000 BC; ditch dug with antler picks, chalk piled into inner and outer banks; hammerstones of various sizes for rough flaking and final smoothing; raising method (pit with one sloping side, timber stakes lining the back, A-frame and plant-fibre rope); lintel lifting by earth ramp or timber crib.
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/things-to-do/exhibitions/collection-highlights/ — antler picks found at the base of the ditch, battered and worn, deliberately left there when the earthwork was finished; ox shoulder blades used as shovels.
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/things-to-do/stone-circle/stones-of-stonehenge/ — each lintel locked to its uprights by mortise and tenon and to its neighbours by tongue and groove; techniques borrowed from woodworking.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge — 30 uprights and 30 lintels in the outer circle, uprights c. 4.1 m high and c. 25 tons; Great Trilithon upright up to 50 tons and c. 6.7 m visible; bluestones c. 2 m tall, c. 2 tons; lintels curved to continue the circle; sarsen phase c. 2600–2400 BC; ditch c. 3100–3000 BC.
https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abc0133 — Nash et al. 2020, Science Advances: 50 of 52 sarsens share a common geochemistry matching West Woods, Marlborough Downs.
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/about/search-news/research-reveals-origin-of-stonehenge-stones/ — West Woods is c. 25 km (about 15 miles) north of Stonehenge; the returned 1958 core made the analysis possible.
https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2015/dec/stonehenge-bluestone-quarries-confirmed-140-miles-away — bluestone quarries at Carn Goedog (spotted dolerite) and Craig Rhos-y-felin (rhyolite), c. 140 miles from Stonehenge; natural vertical pillars levered off the rock face with wedges onto stone platforms.
https://www.southampton.ac.uk/news/2019/03/stonehenge-bluestones.page — charcoal from the quarry track and platform dated to c. 3000 BC.
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2352409X22003601 — extraction technique at the Preseli quarries: wedges worked into natural joints, pillars eased forward, loading platforms below.
https://www.nationalgeographic.com/adventure/article/151207-stonehenge-bluestone-discovery-archaeology — bluestone weights of roughly two to five tons; overland and sea routes both proposed.
https://egqsj.copernicus.org/articles/73/117/2024/ — the glacial-transport theory for the bluestones, and the case against it; presented in the script as an open question.
https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/how-was-stonehenge-built — sledges, log rollers and greased timber tracks; rope teams; ropes of plant fibre.
https://www.nationalgeographic.com/magazine/graphics/see-how-stones-strength-and-smarts-built-stonehenge-feature — up to 1,000 hammerstones from two to 65 pounds to shape one large sarsen; inner faces dressed smoother than outer; tenons carved from above once the stone was standing.
https://www.thebritishacademy.ac.uk/documents/3921/92p231.pdf — Richards and Whitby, The Engineering of Stonehenge: experimental raising of a stone with A-frame and ropes; timber-crib method for lifting lintels; workforce estimates.
https://www.sciencealert.com/it-turns-out-that-building-stonehenge-wasn-t-that-hard-experiment-finds — greased timber rails sharply reduce the number of haulers needed; estimates are soft.
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/history-and-stories/history/description/ — axis aligned on midsummer sunrise to the north-east and midwinter sunset to the south-west; the sun sets between the uprights of the tallest trilithon; the Heel Stone outside the north-east entrance, with a hole beside it for a possible partner stone.
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/history-and-stories/understanding-stonehenge/ — the Avenue: parallel banks and ditches c. 12 m apart running from the north-east entrance, bending down to the River Avon c. 2.8 km away.
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/about/search-news/pr-feast-at-stonehenge/ — midwinter feasting on pork and beef at Durrington Walls; isotope analysis of pig and cattle teeth showing animals brought from west Wales, northern England and north-east Scotland, some as far as 500 miles.
https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aau6078 — multi-isotope analysis (strontium, oxygen, sulphur) showing feasts drew people and animals from across Britain.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Durrington_Walls — houses with chalk plaster floors, central hearths and chalk-and-timber beds and dressers; possibly up to 1,000 houses; the Southern Circle of concentric oak post rings aligned on midwinter sunrise; its own avenue to the River Avon.
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/woodhenge/history/ — Southern Circle c. 40 m across, timbers up to 5 m tall and up to 3 tonnes, roughly 300 mature trees felled; Woodhenge nearby with six concentric rings of posts.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge_Cursus — the Cursus, c. 3 km long and 100–150 m wide, dated 3630–3375 BC by an antler pick from the western terminal ditch, centuries earlier than Stonehenge.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07652-1 — Clarke et al. 2024, Nature: zircon, apatite and rutile grain ages give the Altar Stone a provenance in the Orcadian Basin of north-east Scotland, over 750 km away, probably reaching Salisbury Plain by sea.
https://www.nessofbrodgar.co.uk/altar-stone-2024/ — the 2024 result narrows the source to the Orcadian Basin (Moray Firth to Caithness) and rules out Mainland Orkney; the precise quarry is still unknown.
https://www.wiltshiregeologygroup.org.uk/geology/cretaceous/ — Salisbury Plain chalk formed from coccoliths and other microscopic marine remains in a warm Cretaceous sea; flint nodules formed from biogenic silica such as sponge spicules.
