Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is finished. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders come down, away from your ears. Let the bed take your whole weight. You do not need to hold any part of yourself up.

Let your hands go soft. Let your jaw unclench. Let your breathing slow, all by itself. There is nothing to watch for tonight. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will be asked to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going a very long way out. To a place made of cold dust and cold gas, where new stars are being made, slowly, without any noise at all. We are going to a set of dark columns standing in the light of a nebula. People call them the Pillars of Creation. And once we are there, there will be nothing to do but float, and look.

Nothing out there is in a hurry. Everything is moving. Nothing is rushing. The whole place has been doing what it is doing for longer than our planet has existed.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin to travel.

We begin by leaving. Gently, and with no effort at all.

Imagine the ground letting go of you. Imagine the roof above you opening quietly, like a book. And imagine yourself rising, without sound, into the dark over your town.

The lights below draw together into a soft scatter. The coastline appears, dark against dark. Then the curve of the whole world, with a thin blue line along its edge where the air runs out. And then the air is gone. There is no sound now. There will not be any again on this journey. Out here there is nothing to carry it.

Turn away from the Earth, and face outward.

You pass the Moon, grey and pocked and quiet. You pass Mars, a small rust-coloured light. You pass Jupiter, banded in cream and amber. You pass Saturn, tilted, wearing its wide flat brim. And then Uranus, and Neptune, both a deep and even blue.

And then you are past all of it. Past the last of the planets. Past the cold scattered ice beyond them. The sun is behind you now, and it is small. Still the brightest thing in the sky, but small. A bright pin, and nothing more.

Keep going. We are not nearly there yet.

We are going a distance that is difficult to hold in the mind. So let us take it slowly.

Light is the fastest thing there is. It crosses from the sun to the Earth in about eight minutes. It reaches Neptune in about four hours. In a single year, light travels a distance we call a light year, and that distance is enormous.

The Pillars of Creation are somewhere between five and seven thousand light years away.

That number has moved, by the way. For a long time the best answer was about seven thousand. Then better measurements came in, and the figure came down, closer to five thousand seven hundred. Some careful sources still say six and a half thousand. Nobody is being careless. It is simply very hard to measure, and the answer is still settling.

That is a restful thing about astronomy, if you let it be. The numbers are honest about how well they are known. Nobody out there needs you to be certain tonight.

So: something like six thousand light years. The light arriving in your eye left there six thousand years ago. It has been travelling the whole time.

We will go faster than light, because we are imagining, and imagining is free. But hold the size of it as we go. It is worth carrying for a moment, and then setting down.

Ahead of you now, something begins to change in the dark.

The star field thickens. Then it dims, in patches, as though parts of the sky had been softly smudged. You are coming up on a cloud. Not a cloud of water, like the ones over your house. A cloud of gas and dust, hanging in space, light years across.

It begins to glow.

Not brightly. Not suddenly. It comes up like a coal, deep red at the edges, warming toward the middle. This is the Eagle Nebula. It sits in a constellation called Serpens, the serpent, in the part of it that old star charts call the tail.

Come closer and the glow separates into structure. There are folds in it. There are lanes of darker material laid across the light. There are long curved walls of gas, lit from one side and dark on the other. Like the inside of a cave lit by a doorway.

And near the middle, standing up out of the floor of all that light, there are three dark columns.

They are the reason we came.

Stop here for a moment, at a distance, and simply take in the shape of them.

Three towers of dark cloud, rising into the light. Rounded at the top. Wider at the base. Their edges are bright where the light catches them, and their insides are black.

They lean slightly, all in a similar direction, like grass combed by a current.

Now the scale, and this is the part that is hard to feel. The tallest of the pillars is about four light years from base to tip. Four light years. The distance from our sun to its nearest neighbouring star is a little over four light years.

So set our sun at the base of that column. Set its nearest neighbour star at the tip. The column would still be there between them, filling the whole gap.

That is one pillar. There are three.

Let that sit for a second, and then let it go. You are not required to hold it.

And the pillars are only a corner of the place.

Pull back, in your mind, and look at the whole nebula. The columns become a small dark detail near the middle of something far larger. The cloud they belong to runs on for tens of light years in every direction.

There is another feature off to one side, away from the famous three. A single spire of gas and dust, standing alone, reaching out of the cloud wall. That one is about nine and a half light years long. More than twice the height of the tallest pillar.

It is less photographed, and just as old, and it is doing exactly the same thing.

The whole nebula covers a patch of our sky a little wider than the full moon, and about as tall. You would never guess it. To the eye it is a faint grey smudge, if you can find it at all.

The cluster of stars and the glowing cloud even carry separate catalogue numbers, because they were logged separately, long before anyone knew they were two parts of one thing. The stars are NGC 6611. The cloud is IC 4703.

One is the child of the other. It took a couple of centuries to notice.

So when you look at the pillars, remember that you are looking at a detail. A small dark feature, in one corner, of something very large and very quiet.

Now let us come in close, and find out what they are actually made of.

From far away they look solid. They look like rock, or like clay pulled up by a hand. They are not.

They are gas and dust, and they are extraordinarily thin. Colder than almost anything you have ever touched. Mostly hydrogen, the simplest and most common thing in the universe, in the form of molecules. Mixed through it there is dust, which is not dust in the household sense, but fine dark grains of carbon and silicate. Soot and sand, in effect, scattered very sparsely.

If you could stand a jar of this material on your kitchen table, it would look empty. It would seem like nothing at all.

They look solid to us for the same reason a distant fog bank looks solid. Depth. You are not looking through a jar of it. You are looking through light years of it. Enough thin fog, stacked deep enough, will stop the light behind it.

And this is exactly what a newer telescope showed. Seen in infrared, which passes through dust more easily than visible light does, the pillars go partly see-through. Stars appear inside them. The walls turn to mist.

Solid from here. Almost nothing up close. Both of those are true at once.

Let us stay with the dust for a moment, because it is stranger than it sounds.

The grains out here are tiny. A typical one is around a tenth of a micrometre across. That is far smaller than anything you could see. Hundreds of times finer than a human hair. Smaller than most of the particles in a wisp of smoke.

They are made of two main things, in roughly equal measure. Silicates, which is to say the stuff of sand and common rock. And carbon, in various forms. Some of the grains appear to have a mineral core with an organic coating grown over it, like frost forming on a stone.

And they are cold. Warmed only by the starlight falling on them, they sit at around eighteen degrees above absolute zero. Which is to say: about as cold as things get.

Now, here is the useful part. Grains that small do not block all light equally. They are very good at stopping short wavelengths, which we see as blue. They are less good at stopping longer ones, which we see as red.

So starlight that passes through dust comes out looking redder than it went in. Astronomers call this reddening, and for a long time it was a nuisance, because it made distant things look wrong.

Then it became a tool. If you know how much a thing has been reddened, you know how much dust is between you and it.

And if you go further along, past red, into the infrared, the grains stop mattering much at all. The light simply goes through. Which is how we came to see inside these columns.

So what is doing the lighting?

Above the pillars, out of the frame of the most famous picture, there is a cluster of young stars. It is called NGC 6611. It holds something like eight thousand stars, and by the standards of stars they are all newborns. A few million years old, at most. Our sun is about four and a half thousand million.

A handful of them are the giants. The kind astronomers label O type, which is the hottest and most massive class there is. Fifty to eighty times the mass of our sun. Surface temperatures above thirty thousand degrees.

The brightest of them is a pair, orbiting one another, catalogued as HD 168076. Together they shine with something like a million times the light of our sun.

Now, they are not gentle neighbours, but they are not doing anything sudden either. They simply pour out light. Enormous amounts of it, much of it ultraviolet, in every direction, continuously, for millions of years.

And that light is what carved the pillars.

Here is the mechanism, and it is slow and quiet and rather beautiful.

Ultraviolet light carries enough energy to break gas apart. When it lands on the surface of the cloud, it strips electrons from the hydrogen atoms there. The gas at the surface heats. It stops being bound to the cloud. And it drifts away, off the surface, into the space around it.

Astronomers call this photoevaporation. Evaporation by light.

It is not an event. There is no moment when it happens. It is happening now. It has been happening steadily for a very long time. Rather like a bar of soap getting smaller in a shower, with nobody watching.

The whole surface of the cloud is quietly boiling away into the light. The material lifts off in a thin steady flow, and that flow is what makes the bright rims. Those glowing edges around each pillar are not the pillar. They are the cloud leaving it.

The pillars are shrinking. Slowly. Steadily. Losing themselves into the light that shows them to us.

Which raises a reasonable question. If the light is wearing the cloud away, why is there anything left standing at all?

Because the cloud is not evenly packed.

Here and there inside it are knots of much denser material. Thick enough to resist. The light hits those knots and wears them down far more slowly than it wears down the loose gas around them.

And each dense knot casts a shadow.

Everything directly behind it is shielded. The light never reaches it, so it is never boiled off. So while the open cloud retreats, the shielded material stays. And what is left, when enough has gone, is a long column of surviving cloud. It has a dense cap at its top. It points back at the star doing the wearing.

This is why they are pillars, and this is why they lean the same way. They are not structures. They are shadows made solid. They are what remains of a much larger cloud, in the places where something happened to be in the way.

Wind does this to sandstone in deserts, and leaves standing rock. Water does it to a riverbed. Here the wearing agent is light, and the leftovers are four light years tall.

Let us go right up to one of those dense caps and look at it properly.

They are small, by the standards of everything else out here. Roughly a hundred times the distance from the Earth to the sun, across. Which is to say: wider than our entire family of planets, and small enough to be a detail.

When these were first properly seen, astronomers gave them a name that was partly a joke and partly accurate. Evaporating gaseous globules. Which shortens, of course, to EGGs.

And the name is fair, because things are forming inside them.

Under its own weight, a knot like this can pull inward on itself. As it draws in, the middle grows denser, and warmer. Slowly, over a very long stretch of time, the middle of that knot can become hot enough and dense enough to start fusing hydrogen.

And when that happens, it is a star. A new one. Made in the dark, inside a shell of dust, in a pocket of shadow that survived only because something was in the way.

The gas gives way to the light. Except where it does not. And where it does not, occasionally, a star.

That collapse is worth following slowly, because it has a shape you already know.

Start with a knot of cold gas, drifting. Every part of it pulls, faintly, on every other part. If it is dense enough, and cold enough, that pull wins. The knot begins to fall inward, toward its own middle.

As it falls, it gets warmer. That is simply what happens when material is squeezed. The heat has to come from somewhere, and it comes from the falling itself.

But the knot was never perfectly still. It had some small turn to it, the way everything drifting in a cloud does. And as it draws inward, that small turn speeds up.

You have seen this. A skater pulling their arms in spins faster. Water leaving a basin gathers into a swirl. It is the same rule, and it does not care about scale.

So the knot spins. And spinning material cannot fall straight inward from every direction at once. It flattens. It settles into a disc, turning, with a thickening ball at the centre.

The middle becomes a star. The disc around it becomes, in time, whatever the disc becomes. Grains, then pebbles, then worlds.

That is why the planets in our sky all lie in very nearly one plane. Not by arrangement. Because everything here fell out of one flat spinning disc, a long time ago.

The pattern is the same in every one of those eggs, tonight.

There is a wrinkle here, and it is worth sitting with. It is the kind of thing you can turn over slowly. No part of it ever becomes urgent.

The shielding does not last.

Eventually the cloud around an egg is worn back far enough that the egg is left exposed, standing alone on a stalk. And once it is exposed, it begins to evaporate too. That stage is short. Something like ten thousand years, which out here counts as an eyeblink.

So there is a race, of a sort, though it is the slowest and least dramatic race imaginable. Either the knot collapses into a star before the light finds it, or the light gets there first and takes the material away.

Both happen. Some eggs become stars. Some do not. Nobody is keeping score, and nothing is lost when a knot disperses. The material simply returns to the general cloud, and drifts, and may gather again somewhere else, at some other time, in some other shadow.

There is no waste out there. There is only rearranging.

Now come back out a little, and look along the outside edges of the pillars.

There are streaks there. Wavy, and reddish, and rather like something poured. For a long time nobody could see them well. A telescope launched a few years ago, which sees in infrared, brought them into focus.

They come from the young stars inside.

A star being born does not simply switch on. As it gathers material inward, it also throws some of it back out, in two narrow jets, one from each pole. The jets travel fast. Faster than sound would travel in that gas, if that gas could carry sound.

When a jet like that runs into the surrounding cloud, it piles the material up in front of it. That pile-up is called a bow shock. It is the same shape a boat makes at the front of a hull. A slow spreading V.

The red colour is hydrogen. Molecules of it, energised by the collision, giving that energy back out as light of one particular shade.

So those poured red streaks are announcements. Each one means a star somewhere in there has started. Only a few hundred thousand years old. Just beginning.

Let us leave the physics for a while, and think about the people who have looked at this.

The nebula is not hard to find, as these things go. It sits at about magnitude six. That is right at the edge of what good binoculars will show you from a dark garden. Not the pillars. Those need a telescope in space. But the glow, yes.

It was first written down in the seventeen forties, by a Swiss astronomer called Jean-Philippe Loys de Chéseaux, working from Lausanne. He was compiling a list of faint fuzzy objects and he included this one.

Then, on a June night in seventeen sixty-four, a Frenchman came across it independently. His name was Charles Messier. He wrote it into his own catalogue. He was hunting comets, and he was tired of being fooled by smudges that turned out not to be comets. So he made a list of the smudges to ignore.

That list is now one of the most loved documents in astronomy, and this object is the sixteenth entry in it.

A list of things not to be distracted by. Which turned out to be a list of some of the most beautiful things in the sky.

Then, in nineteen ninety-five, two astronomers named Jeff Hester and Paul Scowen pointed a telescope in orbit at it.

What came back was the picture. You have almost certainly seen it. Three dark columns, gold and green and rust, standing against a lit background.

It was not made to be beautiful. They were studying photoevaporation. They wanted to see whether star formation really was being driven at the edges of these clouds by the light of massive stars nearby. The image was evidence, and the eggs were the finding.

But it left the journals almost immediately. It went onto posters, and postage stamps, and the sides of buildings, and the walls of a great many bedrooms.

Something about the shape of it. Three dark towers, lit from one side, with a name that turned out to be exactly right.

They went back nearly twenty years later with a better camera and took it again. Sharper, wider, and in more colours. Same three columns. Slightly different, in a way we will come to.

Now, there is an argument about these pillars, and I want to tell it to you carefully, because it is often told badly.

Some years ago, an infrared telescope found a patch of hot dust near the pillars. One explanation offered was this. A massive star nearby had reached the end of its life, some thousands of years ago. And the wave from that might already be moving through the region.

If that were right, and if the distance were the larger estimate, the shape we see could have already changed. And the news of it would still be in transit.

It is a striking idea. It also may well be wrong.

Other astronomers pointed something out. If that had happened, we should be picking up much stronger radio and X-ray signals. We do not. The evidence for the event is thin. And when the pillars were re-photographed in more detail, no sign of any such wave was found in them.

The current answer, from the people who look most closely, is that the pillars are standing. That they are being worn away by light, gently, as they have been all along. And that on that reckoning they have at least another hundred thousand years of standing to do.

A hundred thousand years. Longer than there have been cities. They will outlast this argument, and everyone having it.

Still, the delay is real, and it is worth holding for a moment. Not as a worry. As a fact with a pleasant shape.

Everything you can see out there is old news. The light left six thousand years ago and has been in flight ever since, and nothing can hurry it.

Six thousand years ago on Earth, people were beginning to press marks into wet clay to keep count of grain. The first cities were small. Nobody had made glass. Nobody had thought of a wheel with spokes.

That light set out then. It crossed the space between the stars the whole time anyone was doing any of that. It arrived tonight.

So the picture is a letter. Sent long before there was anyone to read it. Delivered to a species that grew up while it was on the way.

There is no way to see the pillars as they are right now. There never will be. What we get is what they were, and it arrives faithfully, at the only speed the universe offers.

You do not need to do anything about that. It is simply the arrangement.

And there is one more thing to say about the place, which makes it less far away than it seems.

We came from one.

Not from this cloud. This one is far too young. But from a cloud like it, and from a cluster like that one, four and a half thousand million years ago.

We can tell, and the way we can tell is lovely. Certain meteorites carry the traces of a form of aluminium that decays quickly, in astronomical terms. Its half-life is about seven hundred thousand years. So it cannot have been sitting around. It had to be made shortly before the solar system formed, and folded straight in.

And ordinary stars do not make that. Only very massive ones do. Which means there was a massive star close by when our own disc was forming. Close enough to seed it.

Massive stars are rare. Roughly one for every few hundred ordinary ones. So for us to have had one as a neighbour, our sun must have been born in company. The estimates run from a few hundred stars to a few thousand.

Those siblings are gone now. Not lost, only scattered. Clusters loosen over time, and their stars drift apart, and after a few thousand million years they are spread right around the galaxy.

They are still out there somewhere. Unmarked, and unidentifiable, and going about their business.

So the pillars are not a foreign country. They are a picture of the neighbourhood you were made in, still in progress, six thousand light years away.

Come back in close now. One last look, and then we will settle.

Float just off the flank of the tallest column, near the top, where the bright rim is.

Nothing here is moving quickly. The gas lifting off the surface is going somewhere, but you would not see it go. It would take a human lifetime of watching to notice any change at all.

The light comes steadily from above and behind you. The dark bulk of the pillar is on one side, cold and quiet. Open space is on the other. And here and there, deep inside, a faint reddish point where something has recently begun.

There is no sound. There is no weather. There is nothing coming.

The columns simply stand in the light, giving themselves up to it slowly, making a few stars on the way. They were doing this before anyone knew. They will do it long after anyone remembers.

And they are not waiting for anything. That is the restful part. Nothing out here is waiting.

Feel the weight of your body where it rests. Feel that you do not have to hold it. Let the last of the day's holding go out of your shoulders, out of your hands, out of your jaw.

Your breathing has already slowed. You did not have to do that. It happened on its own, while you were looking at something else.

Out there, the light keeps arriving. The cloud keeps giving way. Somewhere inside a pocket of shadow, a knot of cold gas is drawing quietly inward, and will go on drawing inward long after tonight.

None of it needs watching. None of it needs you.

You do not need to keep the distances. You do not need to keep the names, or the numbers, or the shape of the columns. You do not need to remember any of this in the morning. It was only something to rest your attention on while your attention was letting go.
