Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been… you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to spend a summer evening in a garden. A walled garden, behind an old abbey, in a town in the middle of Europe. A quiet man in a black habit is working there among his pea plants. His work was patient work. Done one flower at a time, for eight summers, in rows. Nobody hurried him. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin on a street of pale houses, on a warm evening. A little more than a hundred and sixty years ago.

The town is Brünn, in Moravia. Today it is called Brno, and it lies in the Czech Republic. Then it was a town of the Austrian Empire, with tall church spires, and mills along the river. The air smells of warm dust, and cut hay from somewhere beyond the walls. Swallows are going over the rooftops, high up, in the last of the light.

At the edge of the old town stands an abbey. It belongs to the Augustinian friars, and it is dedicated to Saint Thomas. Long low buildings, the color of cream. A church with a tall roof. A square of cloister inside, with a covered walk around it. The stone is still warm from the day. You can put your hand flat on it, and feel that.

A bell rings for the evening office. One bell, slow and even. It hangs in the air, and then it goes.

Under the cloister walk the air is a little cooler. The flagstones are worn in a soft path down the middle. Somewhere a door closes, quietly. Somewhere a plate is set down on a wooden table. The friars are at supper. The building is settling into its evening, the way it has for hundreds of years.

Behind the buildings there is a wall, and in the wall there is a gate. Wooden, painted green, a little faded. It opens with a soft complaint from the hinge. And on the other side is the garden.

§

Step through, and let the gate swing to behind you.

The garden is large, and it is mostly ordinary. There are fruit trees along one wall, pears and apples. Some of them carry grafts that a careful hand has bound up in cloth. There are beds of vegetables for the kitchen. There is a well, with a wooden cover. There are gravel paths between the beds, and the gravel is warm under your feet.

Off to one side, beneath the long wall of the building, is a strip of ground that is different. It is narrow, and it is neat, and it is all peas. Row after row of them, climbing on sticks and twine, a little taller than a man's waist. The leaves are a soft grey-green. The tendrils have wound themselves round the twine like small springs.

Beside this strip stands a glasshouse. The abbot had it built a few years ago, in 1854, for the friar who works here. Its panes are catching the last light and giving it back as pale gold. Through the glass you can see pots, and shelves, and the shapes of plants.

And in the pea rows, bent over a little, is the friar himself. A broad, quiet man with spectacles and a kind face. He is in his thirties. His name is Gregor Mendel. He is not doing anything dramatic. He is looking at a flower.

§

Let us stay by the gate a moment, and think about how he came to be here.

He was born in 1822, in a small village in Silesia, in the hills to the north. His family were farmers. His father kept an orchard of apples and pears and plums, and he kept bees. As a boy the friar helped with all of it. He learned to graft, and to prune. He learned to move a hive, and to follow a swarm. He learned to notice what a tree did from one year to the next. He was good at school. So he was sent to a bigger school in a bigger town, and then further, to study.

The abbey took him in when he was twenty-one. It was that kind of abbey. The abbot, a man named Napp, liked science. He sat on the board of the local society of sheep breeders. Years earlier, at one of their meetings, he had set the members a plain question. What is inherited, he asked, and how. Nobody could answer it. It was still waiting when the young friar arrived.

Napp sent his friars out to teach. And he sent this one to the University of Vienna, from 1851 to 1853.

In Vienna the young friar studied physics under Christian Doppler. Doppler is the man whose name is on the way a train whistle drops as it passes. He taught his students to measure, and to count, and to trust the counting. The friar also learned plant anatomy, and the microscope, under a botanist named Unger.

Then he came home, to the abbey, and to this garden. And he began to teach physics and natural history to boys at the town's new secondary school. He did that for fourteen years. In the daytime he taught. In the long evenings, he came out here.

§

Come closer to the rows now. The air is cooler near the plants, and it smells green.

He chose peas on purpose, and the reasons are worth sitting with, because they are simple and true. Peas grow easily. They grow quickly. A whole generation fits inside one summer. You can buy many kinds from a seed merchant, and they are cheap. And they do a thing that most flowers do not do.

They are a kitchen plant, too. The abbey ate peas, like every house in the town. Nobody thought it odd that a friar should grow a great many of them. That was a quiet advantage. His experiment looked, from the path, like a vegetable bed.

Look at one of the flowers, if you like. It is shaped a little like a small white butterfly. Two petals stand up like wings. Two more fold down and together, into a little boat, that the old botanists called the keel. Inside the keel, closed away, are the parts that matter. The stamens, which carry the pollen. And the single pistil, which receives it.

The keel closes over both of them before the flower ever opens. So the pollen falls on its own pistil, inside the boat, in the dark. The pea fertilizes itself. No bee needs to come. Nothing from the next plant along can drift in. Every pea plant, left alone, breeds true to itself.

That was the gift of it. If you plant a round pea, you get round peas. Year after year. If you plant a tall one, you get tall ones. The plant is a closed book. It only opens if someone opens it.

He bought thirty-four kinds of peas from the merchants. He grew each one for two years, and watched. Some of them did not breed true, and he set them aside. Twenty-two kinds did, without a single exception. Those twenty-two were the ones he kept.

§

Now let us see what he was looking for.

He was not interested in the whole plant at once. That was too much. He chose seven small things, seven pairs, and he followed each of them alone. One at a time. Seven is a good number. Let me say them slowly, and you can let each one go as it passes.

The shape of the seed. Round, or wrinkled.

The color of the seed inside its coat. Yellow, or green.

The color of the flower. Purple, or white. In his day they spoke of the seed coat instead, grey or white. It comes to the same thing, because the two go together.

The shape of the pod. Smoothly swollen, or pinched in between the peas.

The color of the pod, before it ripens. Green, or yellow.

Where the flowers sit. Spaced along the stem, or bunched up at the top.

And the height of the whole plant. Tall, or short.

Round or wrinkled. Yellow or green. Purple or white. That is all. No blending, no shades. Each pair is a clean either-or. The kind of thing you can see by lamplight, and put in one pile or the other, and count.

§

Watch his hands now. This is the heart of it, and it is very small work.

He wants to cross a round pea with a wrinkled one. But the round pea flower will only ever pollinate itself. So he must get there first. He finds a bud on the round-pea plant that has not opened yet. Still closed, still pale, still soft. He holds it between his finger and thumb.

With the other hand he eases open the little keel. Inside are the stamens, ten of them, not yet ripe, on their fine stalks. He takes a small pair of tweezers. Forceps, he would have called them. And one by one, he lifts the stamens out. Gently, so as not to bruise the pistil beside them. When he is done, there is no pollen left in that flower at all.

Then he goes to the wrinkled-pea plant, and finds a flower that is open and ripe. He collects a little of its pollen, on the tip of a fine brush, or on the tweezers themselves. And he carries it back, and touches it to the waiting pistil.

That is the cross. A minute or two of stillness, and it is done.

Then he closes the flower again, and covers it, so no wandering insect can bring in pollen from somewhere else. A small bag, tied on. And a label, on a thread, in his neat hand, saying what was done and when.

There is one small beetle in this garden that likes peas. It creeps into the flower to lay its eggs, and it can carry a grain of pollen in with it. He knew about the beetle. He watched for it. He looked closely at more than ten thousand plants. In only a few of them had a stray grain got in. He wrote that down too. A careful man counts his mistakes with the same pencil as everything else.

He did this many times over, each summer. In the evenings, after school. Bent over, one bud, and then the next, while the light went.

§

Let us walk slowly down the row while he works, and notice the season passing.

The little bags hang in the leaves like pale fruit. Under each one a flower is quietly finishing. The petals dry and fall away. The pistil swells. Slowly it becomes a pod. Green, and flat at first. Then plump, as the peas inside it grow.

The plants are tied to stakes, with twine, so they stand up straight and do not tangle with the next row. He wanted each plant to be its own plant, and to know which was which. In the afternoons the bees work the other flowers in the garden, and leave the bagged ones alone.

By late summer the pods are full. Then they begin to turn. Some of the plants have pods that go yellow. Some stay green until the very end. And he writes that down too, because that is one of his seven.

Then the harvest. Each plant is pulled, and its pods are picked. They go into a paper packet, with the same label the flower wore. The packets go indoors, to a dry room. The peas rattle a little inside them. It is a good sound. Small and dry and finished.

And the strip of garden is turned over, and left to rest under the autumn rain. The season's work is not out here anymore. It is indoors now, in the packets.

§

Come inside, then, for a while. It is winter now, and the garden is asleep under a little snow.

There is a room in the abbey with a plain wooden table, and a lamp. There is a stove that ticks as it warms. On the table are the packets, and a row of small dishes, and a sheet of ruled paper. The friar sits with his back to the stove. He opens one packet and pours it out.

Peas. Perhaps a few hundred, from one plant. He turns them in the lamplight. Some are smooth and round. Some are wrinkled, with little creases in the skin, like a dried apple. He does not guess. He picks them up, and he puts each one in its dish, and he makes a mark on the paper.

Round. Round. Wrinkled. Round.

Then the next packet. And the next. It took him whole winters, this counting. There is no shortcut through it. The stove ticks. The lamp burns down a little. The marks fill the page in neat groups of five.

And here is what he found, and it is the thing the whole garden was for. Let us take it slowly, because it is simpler than it sounds.

In the first year, he crossed a round pea with a wrinkled one. The seeds that came from that cross were all round. Every one. The wrinkled shape had vanished, as though it had never been there.

But he did not stop. He planted those round seeds the next spring, and this time he let them do what peas do. He let them pollinate themselves, inside their closed little boats. And in the autumn he harvested them, and in the winter he counted.

Five thousand four hundred and seventy-four round. One thousand eight hundred and fifty wrinkled.

The wrinkled shape had come back. It had been hiding for a whole generation, and now it returned. And not just anyhow. Almost exactly one in four of the seeds was wrinkled. Three to one.

He did the same with yellow and green. Six thousand and twenty-two yellow. Two thousand and one green. Three to one again.

He did it with all seven of his pairs. Eight hundred and eighty-two swollen pods, to two hundred and ninety-nine pinched ones. Six hundred and fifty-one plants with flowers along the stem, to two hundred and seven with flowers at the top. Seven hundred and eighty-seven tall, to two hundred and seventy-seven short. When he averaged all seven together, it came to two point nine eight to one. Three of one. One of the other. As steady as a bell.

§

He gave the two kinds of trait a pair of names, and we still use them.

The one that shows in the first generation he called dominant. Round, yellow, purple, tall. The one that hides, and then returns, he called recessive. Wrinkled, green, white, short. Recessive only means that it steps back for a while. It does not vanish. It waits.

And then he did the quiet, careful thing that made him different. He thought about what the numbers meant.

Suppose, he said, that each plant carries two small tokens for each trait. One from each parent. Suppose a plant with two round tokens is round. And a plant with two wrinkled tokens is wrinkled. And a plant with one of each is round, because round is the one that shows.

Now cross a round plant with a wrinkled one. Every child gets one round token and one wrinkled. So every child looks round. Just as he saw.

Now let those children breed among themselves. Each gives one token, chosen at random. Round and round. Round and wrinkled. Wrinkled and round. Wrinkled and wrinkled. Four ways it can fall. Three of them show round. One shows wrinkled.

Three to one. Out of nothing but a coin toss, done twice. He had found the shape of the thing by counting peas at a table in winter. He did not know what the tokens were made of. Nobody would, for a very long time. He only knew that they had to be there, and that they came in pairs. And that they parted, and met again.

§

Now let the winter go, and the spring come back, and the strip fill again with peas.

He did this for eight summers. From 1856 to 1863. Something like twenty-eight thousand plants passed through that narrow strip of garden in those years. More than ten thousand of them, he wrote, he examined closely, one by one. Hundreds of flowers were opened with the tweezers and closed again.

Think of the patience of it, without any strain. Not one big experiment. Thousands of small ones, exactly alike, each one a little more evidence. He was not looking for a surprise. He was looking for the same thing again, and again, until he could trust it.

Then he tried two traits at once. He crossed a plant with round yellow seeds against one with wrinkled green seeds. The children were all round and yellow, as he expected. He let them seed themselves, and he counted again.

Out of every sixteen, about nine were round and yellow. About three were round and green. About three were wrinkled and yellow. And one was wrinkled and green. Nine, three, three, and one. It is only three to one, twice over, laid across each other. The shape of the seed had parted and met on its own. The color had done the same, on its own. Neither one had leaned on the other.

He tried three traits at once, and the numbers still came out right. The same pairs, parting and meeting, only more of them.

The seasons went round. The pods swelled and yellowed. The packets came indoors. The lamp and the stove and the marks on the paper. He had, in the end, a garden full of arithmetic. And it was all lying quietly in the rows, waiting for evening, when he came out with his tweezers again.

§

Let us go into the glasshouse now, while the light lasts. Mind the step.

It is warm in here. Warmer than the garden, and closer, and the air is wet and green. It smells of damp clay pots, and leaf mould, and something faintly sweet. The panes above you have gone the color of weak tea as the sun drops. A few drops of moisture run slowly down the inside of the glass.

Glass does a simple, generous thing. It lets the sunlight in all day, and then it holds the warmth after the sun has gone. The bricks of the low wall are warm to lean on. The soil in the pots is warm. Long after the garden outside has cooled, this small room is still giving the day back, slowly.

On the benches are pea plants in pots. These are the ones he keeps under glass on purpose, during flowering, where no beetle or bee can reach them. If the plants indoors and the plants outdoors come out the same, then the garden crosses were clean. It was his way of checking himself. He did not like to trust a thing he had not tested twice.

And there are other plants too. Fuchsias, in a row along the sunny side, with their small hanging lanterns of flowers, red and purple and white. He crossed those as well, for pleasure. There was a fuchsia named after him, for a while, sold in the shops. In the old photographs of the friars, he is holding one more than once, and looking at it closely.

Stay a minute in the warm. Listen to the drip from the bench. The plants here are breathing out, slowly, into the closed air.

§

Step back outside, into the cool, and look along the wall.

There is a small wooden hut here, painted white, with slatted sides, on a post. Inside it are instruments. A thermometer. A barometer for the air pressure. A gauge for the rain. He began keeping weather records in this garden in 1857, and he never really stopped.

Three times a day. In the morning at seven. At two in the afternoon. And at nine at night, just about now. He walks out, and opens the little door, and reads each instrument in turn. He writes the numbers in a book. Temperature. Pressure. Rain. He notes the clouds, and the wind. He measures the ozone in the air with a strip of treated paper that changes color. He goes to the well, and measures how far down the water stands.

On clear days he looks at the sun, through a darkened glass, and counts the spots on it. He wonders, in his careful way, whether the spots have anything to do with the weather. And on some nights he notes a faint glow in the north, and writes that down as well. He does not claim to know what it means. He only keeps the record, so that someone, someday, can look.

The numbers go to Vienna, to the central weather office. Later he helps found a society for the study of weather, in 1865. He writes about how a forecast might be gathered up and sent out to the farmers. In his own lifetime, in this town, he was better known for this than for the peas.

It is the same habit, you see. The same hand. Read the instrument. Write it down. Do not guess. Come back tomorrow and read it again.

§

Now let the years run forward a little, gently, and listen for a low hum near the far wall.

That is the bee house. He built it later, in 1871, when he was abbot himself. A long brick shelter with the hives set into it in rows. In the evening the bees have all come home, and the hives are warm to the touch. A low, contented sound comes from inside each one. Thousands of small lives, settling.

He had kept bees since he was a boy, in his father's orchard. Here he kept many colonies. Sometimes fifty at once. He joined the town's beekeeping society, and he gave talks there. They printed some of them in their little magazine. He tried different kinds of bees. Italian bees, with their golden bands. Bees from Egypt. Grey bees from Carniola, in the mountains to the south. He tried to cross them, the way he crossed the peas, to see what the children would be like.

Bees are harder than peas. A queen goes up into the open air to mate, and nobody can follow her with tweezers. He knew that, and he tried anyway, and he wrote down what he saw.

At the mouth of each hive, a few bees are standing in a row, facing inward. Their wings are going, though they are not flying. They are fanning. They are drawing the warm air out of the hive, and the cool evening air in, so the colony can rest. They will do it for hours, in shifts, without anyone telling them to.

Put your hand near the hive, if you like. Not on it. Just near. The warmth comes off the wood. The hum comes up through your palm. It is one of the oldest safe sounds there is.

§

Now come back to the abbey, and think about the daytime, and the boys.

For fourteen years he walked into town in the mornings, to the new secondary school, and taught. Physics, and natural history. He was, by every account that has come down to us, a good teacher. Patient. Clear. Fond of an experiment the boys could do with their own hands. He never quite finished the examination that would have made him a full professor of it. So he simply went on teaching anyway, as a substitute, year after year, and nobody minded.

He carried the garden into the classroom, in a way. A barometer on the wall. A thermometer by the window. A jar of seeds. He liked the boys to measure a thing for themselves, and write the number down. Then compare it with the boy beside them. It was the only kind of knowing he really trusted.

In the afternoons he came back through the gate. Took off his hat. Rolled up his sleeves. And went to the peas.

Think of the shape of those years, if you like. A morning of chalk and lenses and boys. An afternoon of buds and tweezers. A night of weather readings by lantern. A winter of counting by the stove. It was a very full life, and a very quiet one. Not one part of it was hurried, and not one part was wasted.

The stone of the cloister is cooling now. The swallows have gone to roost under the eaves. Somewhere in the kitchen garden, a hoe is being leaned against the wall.

§

And then, one winter, he told people what he had found.

The town had a Natural Science Society. Doctors, teachers, a few gentlemen with microscopes. They met in the evenings to hear papers. On the eighth of February, 1865, the friar walked in through the snow and read them the first half. On the eighth of March, he came back and read them the rest.

Picture that room. Gaslight, or oil. A roomful of people, in their coats, on hard chairs. A man in a black habit at a table, with his sheets of ruled paper, reading out numbers. Five thousand four hundred and seventy-four. One thousand eight hundred and fifty. The newspaper said afterward that the first evening went well, and that the audience took a lively interest.

The second evening was quieter. The numbers were harder, and there were more of them. But when he had finished, a professor stood up. He said that what he had seen through his own microscope agreed with what the friar had counted. It was a small thing to say. It was kindly meant. And then everyone put on their coats and went out into the March dark.

The next year, 1866, the society printed his paper in its yearly volume. Some forty-five pages. Experiments on Plant Hybrids, he called it. The volume was bound up with the other papers, on beetles, and rocks, and rainfall. And it was sent out to libraries and societies in other towns, as such volumes were.

And then, quite gently, it sat on the shelves. Nobody argued with it. Nobody needed it yet. The friar was made abbot in 1868, and had an abbey to run. He kept up the weather, and the bees. The pea strip was planted with other things. The paper waited, in libraries, in a dozen cities, like a seed in a packet.

§

Let us follow it, because the ending is a warm one.

Thirty-four years passed. Then, in the spring of 1900, three botanists, in three different countries, each found the same thing in their own gardens.

One was a Dutchman, named de Vries. One was a German, named Correns. One was an Austrian, named Tschermak, who had, as it happens, been working with peas. Each of them had crossed plants, and let the children self-pollinate, and counted. And each of them had found the same pattern coming out of the numbers. Three to one. The hidden trait stepping back, and then returning.

And each of them went to the library, to see who had gone before. And each of them took down that old bound volume from Brünn. And there it all was. The seven traits. The tweezers. The counting. Five thousand four hundred and seventy-four. The whole thing, worked out in a garden, thirty-four years earlier, by a friar who taught physics to boys.

They said so, in print, all three of them, that year. That is the sort of honesty that science is built out of. And from that spring on, the friar's name was on the idea. It has stayed there ever since.

The tokens he had imagined were real. We call them genes now. He had counted his way to them, without ever seeing one.

§

Now let the years come back, and settle, and let it be that summer evening again.

The garden has gone blue with dusk. The pea rows are only shapes. The friar has gone in. On the study table, upstairs, the lamp is lit, and the window stands open to the warm air.

Small moths have found the lamp. They come in over the sill, one and then another. Soft grey ones, and a pale one with fringed wings. They circle the glass, and rest on the shade, and set off again. Their wings make no sound at all. The lamp is steady. The moths are patient with it.

One of them settles on the open page of the weather book, on the day's numbers, and stays. Its wings are the color of the paper. If you did not know it was there, you would not see it. The friar does not brush it away. He writes around it.

And outside, very softly, it has begun to rain. You can hear it on the glasshouse first. A light, even patter on the panes. Hundreds of small taps a minute, each one too quiet to notice on its own. Then on the leaves of the pea rows. Then on the gravel paths. The whole garden is being watered without anyone lifting a can.

The smell of it comes in at the window. Warm earth, wet stone, and the green of the peas. The little bags in the rows are getting damp, and the labels curl a little on their threads. Nothing is harmed. Everything is being looked after.

§

And the garden sleeps.

The rain goes on, soft and steady, over the whole strip. The tendrils hold the twine. Under the bags, the flowers go on quietly finishing what they started, pollen to pistil, in the dark. The pods are swelling a fraction, tonight, as they do every night. The peas inside them are already round, or wrinkled, already yellow, or green. That was decided weeks ago. Nothing about it needs deciding now.

In the glasshouse the fuchsias hang their small lanterns in the dark. In the white hut the thermometer is going slowly down, a degree, and another. In the bee house every hive is humming very low. It is the sound of ten thousand creatures being warm together.

The well holds its water at the level he measured this evening. The gravel paths drink the rain and go dark. The fruit trees stand along the wall with their grafts bound up, healing a little more in the night. Everything here is doing exactly what it was doing before you came, and will go on doing it after you sleep.

Upstairs the lamp is turned down. The moths leave the way they came. The window is pulled to, but not shut.

None of this is in a hurry. It never was. It was made by a man who understood that finding things out takes years of evenings. He was content to open one flower, and then another. And then to put down the tweezers, and read the weather, and go to sleep.

§

So let it be quiet now.

Let the rain go on falling, on the glass and the leaves and the gravel. Let the bees go on humming in the wall. Let the little bags hang in the rows, keeping what they keep. Let the numbers rest on the ruled paper, in the dark room, in their neat groups of five. Whether anyone reads them or not.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the seven traits, not the tweezers, not the three to one. It will all keep itself. It has been keeping itself, in every garden, in every seed, since long before anyone counted. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, like peas poured out into a dish. Let the lamplight fade. Let the cool of the rain come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
