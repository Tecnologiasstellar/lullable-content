# Research sources — The Gardens of Persia and the Hidden Water

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qanat — qanat definition and gravity flow; vertical shafts spaced 20–35 m; gradient 1:1000 to 1:1500 in shorter qanats; kariz / kahrez etymology; muqannis' equipment (leather bags, ropes, reels at the shaft head, hatchets and shovels, lights, spirit levels, plumb bobs and string); trade handed from father to son; spoil mounds that from the air look like a string of craters; water clocks used for just distribution of qanat water among shareholders.
https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1506 — The Persian Qanat, inscribed 2016; eleven qanats; workers' rest areas and watermills included in the listing. (Direct fetch returned 403; figures corroborated by the two sources below.)
https://irandoostan.com/persian-qanat-became-20th-site-iran-registered-unesco-world-heritage-list/ — UNESCO inscription of eleven Persian qanats on 15 July 2016 at the Istanbul session; provinces Kerman, Khorasan Razavi, Yazd, Markazi (Arak) and Isfahan.
https://www.worldheritagesite.org/list/the-persian-qanat/ — the eleven inscribed qanats, their provinces, and the associated rest areas and watermills.
https://ifpnews.com/persian-qanats-inscribed-world-cultural-heritage-list/ — the named eleven: Qasabeh (Gonabad) and Baladeh (Ferdows), Zarch and Hasan Abad-e Moshir (Yazd), Gohar-Riz, Akbarabad and Ghassemabad (Kerman), Moun, Vazvan and Mozdabad (Isfahan), Ebrahimabad (Markazi).
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40068-015-0039-9 — the deepest qanat mother well, over 300 m, belongs to the roughly 2,700-year-old Gonabad qanat in Khorasan Razavi; its discharge has been stable over long periods.
https://surfiran.com/qasabeh-qanat-iran/ — Qasabeh Qanat of Gonabad: total length about 33,113 m, 427 wells, mother well about 300 m deep, built between 700 and 500 BC.
https://www.worldhistory.org/qanat/ — mother well sunk into an alluvial fan below the water table; ventilation shafts along the charted course; gradient chosen so flow is steady without stirring sediment or damaging the tunnel; qanats at least 3,000 years old.
https://earthsci.org/mineral/energy/qanats_iran/qanats.html — tunnel dimensions about 1.5 m high and 0.75 m wide, dug just large enough for one digger; shafts sunk every 20–30 m for spoil, ventilation and repair; the longest Iranian qanat about 70 km.
https://www.livius.org/articles/misc/qanat/ — construction as teamwork headed by a muqanni who cuts with a small pick and shovel while an apprentice packs loose earth into a leather bucket; two labourers at the surface haul it with a hoist called a charkh.
https://www.thearchaeologist.org/blog/ancient-persian-engineering-the-qanats-that-conquered-the-desert — muqannis holding a gradient of less than 1:1000 over long distances using only simple levels, strings and oil lamps.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_clock — the Persian fenjan: a bowl with a small hole set floating in a larger pot, which sinks after a fixed interval; the keeper empties and refloats it and drops a pebble in a jar to count; used to fix how long each farmer may divert qanat water; the khaneh fenjan room with east- and west-facing windows; a fair elder chosen as manager, with at least two keepers needed.
https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/object-of-intrigue-ancient-persian-water-clocks — the fenjaan as a hemispherical bowl with a hole, used to mark 15- or 20-minute shares of irrigation water; Callisthenes records Persian use in the early 4th century BC.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charbagh — the quadrilateral garden divided by crossing waterways, with a small square basin at the centre; origin in the Achaemenid paradise gardens as suggested by excavation at Pasargadae; the four parts read as the four gardens and four rivers of paradise.
https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/caharbag-lit/ — the square or rectangular garden divided into four by narrow canals crossing at right angles, with the pool or fountain at the intersection; the Pasargadae stone water canals defining the plan of a garden founded in the later years of Cyrus the Great (559–530 BC).
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasargadae — the royal garden as the earliest known example of the Persian chahar bagh; the garden pattern stretching between the Tomb of Cyrus and the Tall-i Takht; 6th century BC.
https://persianheritage.org/persian-gardens — the Pasargadae garden of c. 540 BC, excavated in the 1960s, with rectilinear water channels lined with cut limestone, shaded colonnades and plantings of cypress, myrtle and pomegranate; the Zoroastrian fourfold division into cardinal points, seasons and elements.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise — pairidaeza, "walled enclosure", from pairi- "around" and -daeza "to make or form a wall"; borrowed by Xenophon as paradeisos for Persian royal parks in the early 4th century BC.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_gardens — qanats carrying water from aquifers to gardens; cypress, plane trees, pomegranates, roses and fruit trees; pavilions; the nine Persian gardens inscribed by UNESCO in 2011 (Pasargadae, Eram, Chehel Sotoun, Fin, Abbasabad, Shazdeh, Dolatabad, Pahlevanpour, Akbarieh).
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fin_Garden — Fin completed in 1590 under Abbas the Great; fed by the Soleymanieh qanat whose mother well is two kilometres upstream with 14 service wells; 2.3 hectares; yard surrounded by ramparts with four circular towers; central kushak pavilion; turquoise-tiled pools and fountains; UNESCO 18 July 2012; oldest extant garden in Iran.
https://rashintravel.com/fin-garden/ — 579 cypresses and 11 plane trees at Fin; channels and fountains working without pumps, by the weight of the water alone.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shazdeh_Garden — Shazdeh Garden 6 km from Mahan, Kerman; 5.5 hectares, rectangular, walled; gate at the lower end, two-floor residence at the upper end, the distance between them lined with water; UNESCO 2011.
https://irandoostan.com/shazdeh-garden-green-oasis-lying-heart-desert/ — Shazdeh fed by the Tigran qanat and mountain spring; water appearing at the topmost terrace behind the pavilion and dropping through twelve tiers of stepped waterfalls and pools; Qajar, reign of Naser al-Din Shah (1848–1896); desert setting.
https://irantourismer.com/eram-garden-shiraz-bagh-e-eram/ — Eram Garden in Shiraz; name from an old word for heaven; the sarv-e naz cypresses, the tallest about 35 m; over 300 rose varieties; cypress and orange trees along the paths; Qajar pavilion; now part of Shiraz University's botanical garden; UNESCO 2011.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windcatcher — the badgir catching wind and forcing it down the windward side and out the leeward side; evaporative cooling as incoming air passes over qanats or pools; four or eight openings; the Dowlatabad Garden windcatcher in Yazd among the tallest existing.
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakhch%C4%81l — yakhchal meaning "ice pit"; conical dome and walls at least two metres thick; sarooj mortar of sand, clay, egg white, lime, goat hair and ash; ice formed in shallow pools on cold desert nights and layered with straw in the pit; shade walls; connections to qanats and windcatchers; records dating to about 400 BC.
https://www.missedhistory.com/article/yakhchal-persian-ice-house-refrigeration-2500-years-desert — ice made in shallow pools on the shaded north side by radiative cooling to the night sky, harvested before sunrise and moved into the underground pit; interiors of 5–10 °C in summer.
https://surfiran.com/mag/windcatcher-an-ancient-engineering-feat-that-harnessed-the-wind/ — badgir as a brick tower with four or eight openings, set to catch a breeze and carry cool air down into the rooms; the Dowlat Abad badgir in Yazd rising about 33 m.
