Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to sit, very slowly, in a walled garden in Persia, at the end of a hot day. Water is running there in stone channels, between old cypresses. And then we are going to follow that water back, underground, to where it comes from. Nobody hurried the water. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

We begin at a gate, in the last of the daylight.

The gate is set in a long wall of mud brick. The wall is taller than a person, and warm to the touch. It has been holding the sun all day. Now it is giving the heat back, slowly, into the evening. Outside the wall is the plain. Dry, and pale, and wide. It is going gold now, and then gray, as the light goes.

Inside the wall is something else entirely.

Step through. The air changes at once. It is cooler by several degrees. It is faintly damp, the way air is beside a river. And it smells of roses, and wet stone, and the dust that has settled on the leaves all day. Somewhere ahead of you, water is moving. Not loudly. A steady, low, continuous sound. Like a person breathing in the next room.

The path under your feet is packed earth, then brick, then stone. On either side, tall dark shapes rise up into the dusk. Cypresses. They stand very straight and very still. Their tops are lost in the darkening sky.

The whole garden lies in front of you. Long, and narrow, and sloping gently away. And down the middle of it, catching the last light, runs a narrow channel of water.

Stand there a moment. The gate is behind you. The desert is behind the gate. Everything in front of you is green, and cool, and quiet.

Let us stay by the gate a moment longer, and look at the shape of the place.

The garden is a rectangle. A wall runs all the way around it. Two channels of water cross it, one running its length, one running its width. They meet in the middle at right angles. And so the garden is divided into four equal parts.

The Persians called this a chahar bagh. Four gardens. It is a very old idea. The four parts were planted with trees. Between them the water ran. And where the water crossed, there was a pool, or a pavilion, or both.

The wall is not there to keep anything out. It is there to keep the garden in. To hold the cool, and the damp, and the shade together in one place, with the desert on the other side.

There is an old word for a walled place like this. In the language of the first Persian kings it was pairidaeza. Pairi means around. Daeza means a wall. A wall, all the way around. Greek travelers borrowed the word for the royal parks they saw in Persia. From Greek it passed into other languages. It became our word paradise.

So when someone says paradise, they are saying, without knowing it, a garden with a wall around it. Cool air. Moving water. A gate that closes softly behind you.

There is a reason the number is four. Four is the old Persian way of counting the world. Four directions on the horizon. Four seasons in the year. Earth, and wind, and water, and fire. Later, when the gardens were made under Islam, the four channels were read as the four rivers of paradise. The plan stayed exactly the same. Only the reading of it changed.

Walk in now, along the main channel, toward the place where the two channels meet.

The channel is cut from stone. It is not wide. You could step across it easily. The water in it is only a hand deep. It is moving at the pace of a slow walk. Every so often it goes over a small stone step, and where it does, it makes a soft sound. Not a splash. More like a sigh.

Where the channels cross, there is a pool. It is square, and lined with tiles, and the tiles are turquoise. Blue green. The color of a shallow sea. In the daytime the water over them looks lit from below. Now, in the dusk, they hold the last of the light a little longer than anything else in the garden.

Beside the pool stands the pavilion. It is open on all sides. Wooden columns hold up a roof, and the roof throws a square of shade on the tiles. Under it there are carpets, and cushions, and nothing else. It was built for one purpose. To sit at the meeting of the waters, in the cool, and do nothing.

Sit there now, if you like. The stone is cool through the carpet. Water runs in on two sides, and out on two sides. A small fountain in the middle of the pool lifts a little water and lets it fall. Drop, and drop, and drop.

Look up, from the pavilion, at the trees.

The cypresses stand along every channel, in a line. They are tall, and narrow, and very dark. They do not move much, even in wind. In Persia the cypress is the tree of long life, because it stays green through the winter. Some of the ones in the old gardens are hundreds of years old. They have been standing beside the same water, in the same line, for longer than anyone can remember.

Between them, here and there, is a plane tree. Broad, and pale barked, with wide leaves the size of your hand. The plane tree does what the cypress does not. It spreads. It makes a roof of shade fifteen or twenty paces wide. In the heat of the day, people sit under it and do not move.

In the four beds between the channels grow the fruit trees. Pomegranate, with its small hard fruit going red now, at the end of summer. Orange trees, their leaves glossy and dark. And under the trees, the low things. Roses. Jasmine, sweeter in the evening than at noon. Mint, and basil, and other herbs, close to the ground, where the water can reach them.

Nothing here is planted for the eye alone. It is all planted for smell, and shade, and fruit. And for the way it sounds when the wind comes through. The whole garden is arranged around the water. Every tree is where it is because the channel passes by.

And each thing here has its own season. The roses come in spring, all at once, and are gone in a few weeks. The orange blossom comes with them, and scents the whole enclosure. The pomegranates ripen late, and hang on the branch into autumn. The plane tree drops its wide leaves in winter and lets the low sun through. Only the cypress stays the same, all year, beside the water.

There is one more thing to notice before we leave the pavilion. Look down.

The carpet under you is a garden too. Woven into it, in dark red and blue and green, is the same plan you have been looking at. A border, for the wall. Two bands of blue, crossing in the middle, for the channels. A square at the crossing, for the pool. And in each of the four quarters, small stiff trees, and flowers, and birds.

Weavers in Persia have been making carpets like this for a very long time. They are called garden carpets. In winter the real garden was bare. A family could unroll one of these on the floor, and sit in the middle of it. In the middle of the pool, so to speak. Warm, indoors, with the four quarters spread out around them in wool.

It is a lovely thought. The plan mattered so much that people wanted it underfoot even when the water was not running. A garden you could roll up and carry. A garden that never needed watering.

Run your hand across it now. The wool is soft, and a little warm, and it smells faintly of dust and lanolin. Under your palm, the blue channel runs to the edge of the carpet, and stops.

And now let us ask, gently, where the water comes from.

It does not rain much on this plain. In summer, it does not rain at all. There is no river here. And yet the channel runs, at the same slow pace, all through the dry months. Day and night. Year after year.

Look east, over the wall, in the direction the water is coming from. Far off, on the horizon, is a line of mountains. In winter they are white. Snow falls on them, and lies there. In spring it melts, slowly, and sinks into the ground. Underneath the plain, at the foot of the mountains, the earth is full of that water. It lies in the gravel, deep down. Cool, and dark, and out of sight.

Long ago, someone here worked out how to bring it to the garden. Not with a pump. There were no pumps. Not by lifting it. Only by letting it fall, very gently, along a tunnel they dug for it underground.

The tunnel is called a qanat. In the old Persian tongue, a kariz. It begins at the foot of the mountains, deep in the wet gravel. It runs for kilometers under the plain. And it comes out into the light, at last, at the garden wall.

Gravity does all the work. Nothing else. The water walks downhill in the dark, at its own pace, and arrives.

Let us go to where it starts, at the head of the tunnel.

Up near the foot of the mountains there is a long gentle slope of gravel. The streams have laid it down over thousands of years. And on that slope there is a well. It is called the mother well. It is the first thing the diggers made, and the deepest.

They chose the place with care. They looked for the green plants that grow where water is not far below. They dug a trial shaft, and waited, and watched the bottom of it slowly fill. When it filled, and kept on filling, they knew.

The mother well goes straight down. Through dry earth. Through damp earth. Into the layer where the water is. Many are fifty meters deep. Some are a hundred. The deepest go far beyond that. At the bottom, the water seeps in through the walls, from every side, slow and cold, and gathers.

Imagine standing at its mouth. It is a round dark opening in the ground, no wider than a doorway. The air that rises from it is cool, even at noon. If you drop a small stone, there is a long pause. Then, from very far below, a soft plunk.

That is the beginning of the garden. That sound, at the bottom of a well, at the edge of the desert.

Down there the water does not sit still. It moves through the gravel toward the shaft, slowly, from every direction at once. A good mother well is not a hole full of water. It is a place where the ground is quietly handing water over. The men who dig them talk about the ground being generous, or being tight. And they learn to tell the difference by listening, and by waiting, and by how the walls feel under a palm.

The men who dig these tunnels are called muqannis.

It is a family trade. A father teaches his son, and that son teaches his. The knowledge passes down through the generations, the way the water passes down through the tunnel. How to read the ground. Where the wet layer runs. How steep to make the slope so the water moves, but does not hurry.

Watch them work, for a while. Two men go down. Two stay at the top. At the top there is a windlass. A wooden drum with a rope wound around it, set on a frame over the shaft. The rope goes down. On its end is a bucket made of leather.

Below, the muqanni digs with a short pick. The handle is not much longer than his forearm, because there is no room to swing anything bigger. He works in a crouch. The tunnel is only about a meter and a half high, and less than a meter wide. Just enough for one man, on his knees or bent double, to cut the earth in front of him.

His apprentice, behind him, fills the leather bucket with what he has loosened. He tugs the rope. Up at the surface the drum creaks, and turns, and the bucket rises into the daylight. It is tipped out. It goes back down. Over and over, all day. A few buckets to the meter. A few meters to the week.

The only light is a small oil lamp. A clay bowl with a wick. He sets it in a niche in the wall beside him. It gives a warm round glow to about an arm's length of tunnel, and no more. Beyond that, the dark. Behind him, faintly, the gray circle of the shaft above.

They dig from the garden end toward the mother well. Uphill, in the dark.

The tunnel does not run in the open. So every twenty or thirty meters, they sink a new shaft from the surface down to meet it. The shaft is for the spoil. It is for the air. And it lets the diggers check that the tunnel is still running true, and still sloping the way it should.

The earth they bring up is piled around the mouth of each shaft. A low ring of it, like the rim of a crater. Over the years the wind rounds the rings off, and the sun bakes them pale.

From the top of a hill, or from an aircraft, you can see them. A line of pale rings, one after another, walking across the plain in a gentle curve from the mountains to the village. Like a string of beads laid down on the sand. Hundreds of them, for a long qanat. Each one a place where a bucket came up into the light.

To keep the tunnel straight, they use the simplest of tools. A plumb line, which is a weight on a string. A level, which is a length of string pulled tight between two posts. And sight. Looking back down the tunnel toward the last lamp, and adjusting a hand's width this way, or that.

Nothing about it is fast. That is rather the point. A qanat is dug at the pace of a person crouching with a pick. And it lasts for centuries.

Let us think for a moment about the slope. Because the slope is the whole secret.

The tunnel has to go downhill, from the mother well to the garden, or the water will not move. But it must go downhill so gently that the water does not race. If it races, it cuts into the floor and carries the earth away with it. If the floor is too flat, the water pools, and stops.

So the muqannis aim for a fall of about one in a thousand. Sometimes one in fifteen hundred. One meter down, for every thousand or fifteen hundred meters along.

Sit with that number. If you walked a kilometer along the tunnel floor, you would come down by about the height of a table. You would not feel it. You could not see it. Your feet would tell you the ground was level. And yet the water would know. It would keep on walking, slowly, toward the light.

The land above slopes more steeply than the tunnel does. So as the tunnel runs toward the garden, it comes closer and closer to the surface. Deep at the mountain end. Shallow at the village end. Until at last the floor of the tunnel and the surface of the ground meet. And the water comes out in the open.

A slope you could not feel with your feet. Held true, in the dark, for kilometers, by men with a string and a lamp. That is what the water in the channel is resting on.

Now follow the water on its slow walk underground.

It leaves the mother well and enters the tunnel. It is cold, and clear, and very quiet. There is no wind down here. No sun. The temperature is the same at midnight as at noon. The same in January as in July. It is the temperature of the deep ground, which does not change.

The water moves at the pace of a slow walk, or slower. A qanat ten kilometers long might take the water most of a day to travel. Some are much longer. In the town of Gonabad, in the east, there is a qanat that runs for some thirty three kilometers under the ground. Its mother well is about three hundred meters deep. Deeper than most buildings are tall. It was dug, people think, about two thousand seven hundred years ago. And it still runs. The same water, the same tunnel, the same slow arrival.

Along the way, in some of the longer qanats, the diggers made small rooms. Wider places in the tunnel, where a man could sit upright, and rest, and eat, and let the lamp burn a while. Some of those rooms are still there.

And at the end, the water comes out. Into a stone channel in the open air. Into sunlight, for the first time in its life. Bright, and cold, and moving, exactly as it did in the dark.

The tunnel needs looking after, and the looking after is gentle work.

Every year, a little silt settles on the floor. Fine sand, carried down from the gravel. A little earth comes loose from the walls. Left alone for long enough, the water would slow. Then it would pool. Then it would find its own way somewhere else.

So every year, in the cool months, the muqannis go down again. The same families, in the same tunnels their grandfathers dug. They lower the lamp, and the leather bucket, and a short shovel. They walk the whole length, stooping, from the outlet to the mother well. Where the floor has silted, they clear it. Where the roof has softened, they set a ring of baked clay to hold it. In the wet gravel near the top, they dig the walls a little wider, so the water can seep in more freely.

It takes weeks. Nobody minds. The tunnel is theirs. The water in it is their village's water.

When they are done, they climb out of the last shaft into the daylight. They wash the earth off their hands in the channel. Then they go home for the evening. And the water goes on, a little clearer than before, at exactly the same pace as always.

And the water is put to work on the way. Where the ground falls away near the outlet, a village might set a mill there. The flow is led into a narrow chute, and dropped onto a wooden wheel, and the wheel turns a stone. Grain goes in dry and comes out as warm flour. Then the water gathers itself again below the mill, and carries on toward the fields, no smaller than before. It has only lent its weight for a moment, on the way past.

Now that the water is in the open, it has to be shared.

A qanat does not belong to one person. It belongs to a village. Every family that helped dig it, or paid for a share of it, has a right to a part of the flow. Not a part of the water. A part of the time. For so many minutes, the whole flow turns into your field. Then it turns into your neighbor's.

To measure the time, they used a bowl. A shallow copper bowl, with a tiny hole in the bottom. It was set floating on the surface of a large pot of water. Slowly, the water crept in through the hole. The bowl grew heavy. It settled lower and lower. And then, after a certain number of minutes, always the same number, it sank.

That was one measure. The keeper lifted the bowl out, emptied it, and set it floating again. To keep count, he dropped a small pebble into a jar. Each pebble a bowl. Each bowl a certain number of minutes. When your pebbles were used up, your turn was over.

The keeper of the bowl was chosen by the village. A fair person. Usually an older one. He sat in a small room built for the purpose, and he watched the bowl, day and night. Two men shared the work, in turns. The room had a window facing east and a window facing west. So they could see the sun rise, and the sun set, and keep the count true.

A bowl, a pot, a jar of pebbles. And on that, the peace of a whole village rested, for hundreds of years. Everyone's turn came. The water sank the bowl at the same slow rate for everyone.

The oldest garden of this kind that anyone has found is at a place called Pasargadae.

It lies in the south of Persia, in a high green valley between dry hills. Cyrus the Great built his capital there, in the sixth century before Christ. Some two thousand five hundred years ago. The palaces are mostly gone. Columns, and doorways, and the outlines of walls remain, pale against the grass.

But the water channels are still there.

They were cut from white limestone. Narrow stone troughs, laid end to end, running in straight lines across what was once the royal garden. Every so often there is a square stone basin, where the water paused. Archaeologists uncovered the channels in the nineteen sixties, and traced where they ran. And when they traced them, the lines crossed. Two channels at right angles. Four quarters between them. A pavilion beside the water.

It is the same plan as the garden you are sitting in tonight. The very same. Twenty five centuries between the two, and the water still runs in the same shape.

We do not know exactly what grew there. Cypress, probably. Fruit trees, likely. The Greek soldiers who came through Persia a century or two later wrote about the royal parks with something like wonder. Straight rows of trees. Water everywhere. Cool shade in a hot country. They borrowed the word for it, and took it home.

Let us visit a few of these gardens now, one after another, in the evening light. We will not stay long in any of them.

The first is called Fin. It lies near the town of Kashan, on the edge of the great central desert. It was finished around the year 1590, in the time of the Safavid kings. It is the oldest of the great Persian gardens that still stands with its water running.

A wall goes all the way around it, with a round tower at each corner. Inside, the water comes from a spring called Soleymanieh, a little way up the slope. A short qanat brings it in. Its mother well is only two kilometers off, with fourteen small shafts along the way.

And once it arrives, it runs everywhere. Through channels lined with blue green tiles. Into pools, and out of them. Through fountains that rise and fall by the weight of the water alone, with no pump anywhere in the garden. The water is a little warm from the ground, and very clear. Over the turquoise tiles it looks like something lit from beneath.

There are five hundred and seventy nine cypresses in Fin. Someone counted. And eleven plane trees. Some of the cypresses are said to be centuries old. They stand in their rows along the channels, very tall, and very dark. The water goes past their feet all night.

In the middle, where the channels meet, is a pavilion. Two stories, open on the sides, with a pool under its floor. You sit above the water, and hear it, and feel the cool rise up through the boards.

The second garden is farther south, near a village called Mahan, in the province of Kerman.

It is called Shazdeh, which means the prince's garden. It is a young garden, as these things go. It was made in the late eighteen hundreds, in the time of the Qajar kings. And it is built on a hillside, on the very edge of the desert. Bare brown mountains behind it. Bare pale sand in front. And in between, inside a wall, a long green rectangle.

Because the ground slopes, they did something lovely with the water. It enters at the top, behind the main house, from a qanat that begins in the mountains. Then it steps down. A long narrow pool. Then a low stone lip, and a fall of a hand's height. Then another pool. Then another lip. Twelve steps in all, each one a small white cascade, all the way down to the gate at the bottom.

The sound of it fills the whole garden. Not one sound. Twelve small sounds, one above another, from the house to the gate. Fountains in the pools. Cypress and pine on either side. And beyond the wall, in every direction, the silence of the desert.

The third garden is Eram, in the city of Shiraz. Its name comes from an old word for heaven. Its cypresses are famous. One of them is said to be nearly thirty five meters tall, the tallest in the country. It has stood beside the same channel for two hundred years. There are orange trees along the paths. There are hundreds of kinds of roses. Tonight it belongs to the university, and students walk under the trees, and read.

Nine gardens like these were listed by UNESCO in the year 2011. And in the year 2016, eleven of the qanats that feed such gardens were listed too.

The water does one more thing here, before we let it rest. It cools the houses.

Look over the garden wall, toward the town. Above the flat roofs, here and there, stand tall square towers of brick. They are taller than the houses. They have openings at the top, on every side. They are called badgirs. Wind catchers.

A wind catcher works like this. Any breeze that moves over the town enters the openings at the top. It is pushed down the tower, down a shaft, into the rooms below. On the far side of the tower, the air is drawn back up, and out. So the house breathes. Air comes in, moves through, and leaves.

And here is the clever part. In the best houses, the shaft comes down over water. A pool in the cellar. Or the qanat itself, running under the house on its way to the garden. The dry air passes over the cold water. It gives up some of its heat. It comes into the room cooler, and a little damp, and smelling faintly of stone.

There is no machine in it. No moving parts. Only a tower, and a shaft, and the water walking past underneath. In the city of Yazd, on the edge of the desert, the roofs are crowded with them. One, in a garden there, rises more than thirty meters. The tallest of them all. On a hot afternoon, in a room beneath a wind catcher, the air moves gently across your face.

And the water keeps something else, that you would not expect to find at the edge of a desert. Ice.

Outside some of the old towns there stands a building shaped like a great clay egg. A dome, twenty meters high, thick walled and pale. It is called a yakhchal. The word means ice pit.

This is how it works. In winter, the desert nights are very cold. Water from the qanat is let into shallow pools in the shadow of a long, high wall. Under the clear sky, the water loses its heat to the stars, and by morning there is ice on it. Before the sun comes up, the ice is broken and carried into the dome, and down into the pit below it.

Layer on layer, through the winter. Ice, then straw, then ice. The walls of the dome are two meters thick. They are made with a mortar of sand and clay and lime, and egg white, and goat hair, and ash. It does not let water through. The dome throws its own shade. A small opening at the top lets the warm air out.

And so the ice keeps. All through spring. Into summer. In the hottest months of the year, someone goes down into the dark under the dome. They come up with a block of winter, wrapped in straw, sweating in the sun.

Water from the mountains, walked underground, laid out to freeze under the stars, kept in the dark. Every part of it patient. Every part of it done by hand.

Come back to the garden now. It is fully dark.

The last of the light has gone off the tiles. The pool is black, with one pale star in it. The cypresses are only shapes, darker than the sky. The air has cooled right down. The wall has stopped giving back its heat.

Somewhere in the plane tree, a nightingale begins. One phrase. Then a pause. Then another phrase, a little different. It is not singing for you. It is singing because it is evening, and this is a garden, and there is water.

Under the song, the channel goes on. The same low sound it made when you came through the gate. It has not changed all evening. It will not change all night. Somewhere under the plain, in the dark, the tunnel is delivering it. A hand's depth at a time, all the way from the foot of the mountains.

The jasmine is stronger now. The roses have gone quiet, but the jasmine has opened, and the whole garden smells of it. Cool water. Warm brick. Jasmine, and dust.

Sit a while longer. The fountain lifts its little water and lets it fall. The nightingale sings, and stops, and sings.

Nothing in this garden is in a hurry. It never was.

The water took most of a day to walk here from the mountains. The tunnel took years to dig, at a crouch, by lamplight. The cypresses took centuries to grow. The plan of the place took longer still. The four quarters and the crossing. Two and a half thousand years, to arrive at this wall, unchanged.

The muqannis have gone home. The keeper of the bowl has counted his last pebble for the day. The wind catcher breathes, slowly, over the sleeping house. The ice waits in its dark pit. The tunnel runs.

There is nothing here to watch for. The water will arrive whether you watch it or not. It has been arriving for a very long time.

Let the nightingale fall silent, when it wants to. Let the last star settle in the pool. Let the garden close its gate softly behind you.

So let it be quiet now.

Let the water go on running in the stone channels, between the dark trees. Let the fountain lift and fall. Let the wind move through the towers of the town. Let the ice lie still in its dome, and the mountains hold their snow.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the tunnel, not the bowl, not a single fact from the whole quiet evening. It will all keep itself, as it has for thousands of years. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts sink, gently, the way the bowl sinks in the pot. Let the warmth of the brick fade. Let the cool of the water come up around you, soft and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
