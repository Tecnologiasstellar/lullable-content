Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to walk, very slowly, beside some moving water. From a spring in the hills of southern France, along a stone channel, to the fountains of an old Roman town. The water falls only a few centimeters in every kilometer. It is in no hurry at all. Neither are we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

We begin at a spring, at dusk, almost two thousand years ago.

The place is called the Fontaine d'Eure. It lies in a fold of the hills near the town of Uzès. The light is going soft and gray. The heat of the day is leaving the stones. And out of the foot of a limestone slope, water is rising. Quietly. Without any fuss. As it has done for longer than anyone can say.

Put your hand in it, if you like. It is cool. It is clear as glass. It tastes faintly of stone. The rock it comes through was once the floor of a warm sea. Over ages it was pressed into pale limestone. Rain sinks into that limestone and travels through it, in the dark, for a long time. Then it comes out here.

Because it has been moving through limestone, the water carries a little of the rock with it. Dissolved, invisible, held in the water like sugar in tea. Remember that. It matters later, in a gentle way.

The limestone here has a name and an age. Geologists call it Barremian. It was laid down roughly a hundred and twenty-five million years ago, grain by grain, on the floor of a shallow sea. Rain that falls on these hills sinks straight into it. It travels through joints and small hidden channels, out of the light. Everything it touches down there is calcium. So that is what it brings up with it.

The air smells of wet stone and wild thyme. Somewhere a frog settles. The evening is very still.

The spring stands about seventy-two meters above the level of the sea. Fifty kilometers away, the town of Nîmes stands a little lower. And that small difference is the whole story tonight.

Let us stay by the water a moment, and think about slope.

Water only moves if it has somewhere lower to go. That is all a river is. A path that leans. The Romans knew this as well as anyone. They had no pumps that could push water across a countryside. They had gravity, and patience, and stone.

So the channel from this spring to the town must lean, the whole way. But it must lean very, very gently. Too steep, and the water races, and tears at the walls. Too flat, and it stalls, and goes green and still.

Here is the fact that sounds wrong. Over the whole fifty kilometers, the channel falls somewhere between twelve and seventeen meters. Scholars measure it a little differently. Call it the height of a tall house. Spread over a walk that takes two days.

That works out to about thirty centimeters in every kilometer. The length of your forearm. Over a thousand paces. If you stood in the channel, you could not see the slope. You could not feel it. Only the water knows it is there.

Vitruvius, who wrote a long book on building, suggested a fall of about one part in five thousand. Or so the reading goes. The channels that survive agree with him closely.

And the water does move. Slowly, steadily, day and night, a soft pull toward the town. Enough, at its best, for forty thousand cubic meters a day.

Forty thousand cubic meters is hard to hold in the mind. Think of it instead as a small river. Arriving quietly, all day and all night, with nobody turning anything on.

And the channel is not brim-full. A Roman channel normally ran about two-thirds full. There is air above the water the whole way. Room for a wet winter. Room for the flow to rise a little, and settle again, without ever touching the roof. The builders left that space on purpose, and then left it alone.

Now picture the people who worked that slope out.

They are surveyors. Gangs of workers and engineers, with poles and cords and a few careful instruments. They walk the hills for weeks before a single stone is cut.

Their favorite tool is a long wooden bench called a chorobates. It is about six meters long. Twenty feet of straight timber, on two legs. At each end hangs a plumb line. A weight on a string. When the string sits exactly over a notch cut in the leg, the bench is level.

On the top of the bench runs a shallow groove. On a windy day, when the plumb lines swing, the surveyor pours water into the groove. Water always lies flat. If it touches the rim evenly at both ends, the bench is level. He sights along the water to a pole held far off. Then he moves the bench, and does it again.

For straight lines and right angles they use a groma. A pole, with a cross on top, and a plumb line hanging from each of the four arms. Sight through two lines, and you have a straight edge across a field.

And for angles there is the dioptra. A flat plate on a stand, that turns. A sighting bar across it. Careful men, in the evening light, lining things up. Then writing them down.

The bench is six meters long. The road ahead of it is fifty kilometers. You can do that arithmetic if you like, though you do not have to tonight. It comes to many thousands of careful placements. Set the bench down. Read the plumb lines. Sight along to the pole. Write the number. Then lift the bench, and walk on.

Picture them at the end of a day much like this one. Dust on their legs. The poles laid down in the long grass. Someone checking a figure by the last of the light, and then folding the tablet shut. Tomorrow they will start again from the mark they left.

Bench by bench. Field by field. Slowly, across fifty kilometers, they draw a line in the air that leans by the width of a hand.

Once the line is drawn, the digging begins.

Most of the channel is cut into the ground. A trench, a little deeper than a person is tall, and a little wider than a doorway. Picks and shovels and baskets. The earth carried away by hand. The smell of it, turned and damp.

Then the channel itself is built inside the trench. Stone walls. A stone floor. At Nîmes it is about a meter and a bit wide, and nearly two meters high. A person can walk along it, stooping a little.

Elsewhere the channels are smaller. A common size is about seventy centimeters wide, and a meter and a half high. Narrow enough that you would turn your shoulders to pass. And buried shallow. Half a meter under the field, or a meter. No deeper than the roots of a vine.

And then it is lined. This is the part to sit with tonight.

The lining is a mortar the Romans called opus signinum. Lime, and sand, and crushed tile. Old pots, broken bricks, roof tiles, ground down small and mixed in. It sets a soft pink-red color. And it has one lovely property. It hardens even when wet. It does not mind water at all. Water is what it is for.

The workers trowel it on in layers. Floor, then walls. And where the wall meets the floor, they do not leave a sharp corner. They build a rounded bead of mortar, like a quarter of a circle, all along the joint. A corner is where a crack begins. A curve has no corner. So the water finds no way out.

It is mixed on the spot. Lime, burned from the same limestone as the hills. Sand from the riverbed. And the crushed tile, carried in baskets, red as brick dust. Somebody grinds the old pots small. Somebody fetches the water. Somebody kneels in the channel all day with a trowel, moving slowly backward, smoothing as he goes.

Smooth, red, curved, and waterproof. Run your hand along it in your mind. Cool and faintly rough, like the inside of a clay jar.

Over the channel goes a roof.

Sometimes flat slabs of stone. More often a low vault, a little arch of stone or concrete, curving over the water. The channel is closed. The water runs in the dark. It stays cool that way. Nothing falls in. Nothing grows.

Then the earth goes back over the top. Half a meter of it, or a meter. The field is planted again. Sheep graze over it. Within a year, you would not know it was there. Only a line of slightly greener grass, in a dry summer, gives it away.

But people need to get in. To clean it, to check it, to clear the silt. So at regular intervals a stone shaft rises from the vault to the surface. A square hole, with a lid. A stone's throw apart, all along the route.

The idea of those shafts is older than Rome. Persian well-diggers had used it for centuries, sinking a line of shafts along a hidden channel. The Romans took the idea and kept it. On some routes the shafts stand about seventy meters apart. Close enough that a worker down there is never far from a ladder and a square of sky.

From above, on a summer afternoon, you would see a line of small stone lids crossing a field. Warm to the touch. A little moss on the shaded side of each one. And under every one, cool air, and water moving.

Imagine a worker lifting the lid, on a morning much like this evening. Cool air comes up out of the dark. The sound of water, very soft, moving underground. He climbs down a ladder into the channel. Wades a little. Runs his lamp along the red walls. Then climbs out, and sets the lid back, and walks to the next one.

Sometimes the line the surveyors drew runs straight into a hill.

Then the workers go through it. Near a village called Sernhac, the channel passes through the rock in tunnels. Not long ones. The longest is about sixty-six meters. But every meter of it was cut by hand, with picks, by lamplight.

You can still see the marks of the picks on the walls. Short curved strokes, one beside another. And small niches cut into the rock at shoulder height, where the lamps could sit. Little pockets of shadow in the stone.

They dug from both ends, and sometimes down from shafts in between. And they met in the middle. The water does not care how long it took. It slides through the cool rock now, in the dark, as if the hill were not there.

Working in there had one quiet advantage. The temperature underground hardly changes. It is much the same in August as in February. No sun. No wind. Only the lamp, and the rock, and the tapping of picks somewhere ahead.

Stand in the tunnel mouth a moment. The air is a few degrees cooler than the evening outside. It smells of damp stone. The sound of the water is close and round, like a voice in a small room.

It is worth saying plainly, because most people picture it the other way.

Almost all of an aqueduct is underground, or close to the ground. At Nîmes, about thirty-five of the fifty kilometers run buried. The bridges and arches that everyone photographs are the exception. They are what the builders did only when they had to.

The same is true at Rome. Of some eight hundred kilometers of channel feeding the city, only about forty-seven ran above ground on arches. The rest was a hidden line under fields and vineyards.

An arch was a last resort. It was expensive, and it was slow. You built one only when the line met a valley too deep to go around and too wide to go under.

So most of this great work was invisible, and always was meant to be. A field with something quiet running under it. That was the plan, not an accident. Stone above ground needs looking after. Stone under a meter of soil is left alone by the frost, and the sun, and the years.

So think of the aqueduct as a long quiet thread laid under the countryside. Following the contour of the hills. Bending where they bend. Taking the long way when the long way is gentler. The route from Uzès to Nîmes is fifty kilometers. The two towns are only twenty apart. The water goes the long way, and takes its time.

But there is a valley on this road. And here the thread has to leave the ground.

The river is called the Gardon. It runs through a gorge of pale rock, between wooded hills. In the evening the water down there is green and slow, and the cliffs turn the color of honey.

The channel arrives at the top of one side, some fifty meters above the river. It must reach the other side without losing more than the width of a finger. There is no way round. So the Romans built across.

Walk to the edge and look. The bridge stands in the last of the light. Three rows of arches, one on top of another. Golden stone. Long shadows under every arch. Swallows turning in the air above the river.

The arch in the middle, over the deepest water, spans twenty-four and a half meters. In the first century, nothing wider had been built anywhere. The river slides under it without touching it.

It is very large, and very still, and completely quiet.

Let us look at how it was made.

The bridge is about forty-nine meters high. The lower row has six arches. The middle row eleven. The top row, which carries the water, has thirty-five now, and once had forty-seven.

The stone is a soft, shelly limestone, full of the shapes of tiny sea creatures. It came from a quarry just seven hundred meters down the river. Blocks were cut there and hauled up to the site. Some of them weigh six tons. There are fifty thousand tons of stone in the bridge, more or less.

And here is the thing to hold in your mind. The lower rows are built without mortar. Each block was cut to fit its neighbor exactly. Then set in place, and held there by nothing but its own weight and the weight above. Stone on stone, dry, for two thousand years.

Sit with what no mortar means. It means no margin. A block a finger too wide will not go into its place. So each one was trimmed on the ground, and lifted, and tried, and lowered, and trimmed again. Fifty thousand tons of stone, brought to a fit by hand, one block at a time.

Look closely at the piers. Blocks stick out here and there, unfinished, like the stubs of branches. The builders left them on purpose. They held the wooden scaffolding. When the work was done, nobody bothered to trim them. They are still there, holding nothing, in the evening light.

The whole bridge was put up somewhere between the years 40 and 60. And across its full length the channel on top falls just two and a half centimeters. Less than the width of two fingers. Over four hundred and fifty meters of stone.

The water crosses the bridge in the dark, inside its lined channel, and goes back underground on the far side.

Now and then along the route it enters a wider room. A settling basin. A stone tank, where the channel opens out, and the water slows down.

That is the whole trick of it. Water carries silt, and sand, and bits of leaf. When the water slows, those things sink. They settle to the floor of the basin, in a soft brown layer. The clearer water goes on. Every so often a worker climbs in and shovels the floor clean.

The Romans built these at the spring, or partway along, or just before the town. Sometimes it was only a widening of the channel. Wider channel, slower water, cleaner water. Simple as that.

Segovia, far away in Spain, did it twice over. The water there ran first into a tank the townspeople still call the big house. Then on into a second building, to settle a second time. Coarse sand out. Then fine sand out. And only then into the town.

Picture the basin now, at dusk. A rectangle of dark water. Its surface barely moving. The sound of the channel entering at one end, and leaving at the other. Between them, a stillness where the water rests, and lets go of what it carried.

There is a small lesson buried in that, and it costs nothing to notice. Nothing was filtered. Nothing was strained or treated. The water was simply given room, and time, and allowed to slow down. And what it was carrying let go of it by itself.

Now let us go back to something we noticed at the spring.

The water carried a little of the limestone in it. Dissolved. Invisible. As the water runs along the channel, some of that stone comes back out again. It settles on the walls as a hard, pale crust. Calcium carbonate. The same thing as the scale in a kettle.

It builds up slowly. A fraction of a millimeter at a time. Year after year. In the Nîmes channel it grew as deep as your hand is long. In places, half a meter on each wall.

And here is the lovely part. It grew in layers. A light layer, then a dark one. Light, then dark. One pair for every year. The bright layers came in the wet seasons. The dark ones in the dry, hot summers. Like the rings of a tree.

The pieces that have been studied are fifteen to twenty centimeters through. The layers inside them are about a millimeter each. Thin bright bands and thin dark ones, pressed together like the pages of a book that nobody sat down to write.

People have cut through that crust and read it. Around a hundred and seventy-five years of it. From about the year 50 to about 225. They can find the dry decades, where the layers grow thin. They can find the warm wet years, where they grow thick.

There are two stretches where the bands crowd close together. One runs from about the year 90 to about 108. Another from about 125 to 145. Drier decades, most likely, with less rain soaking down to the spring. And then a long run of thicker layers, from about 175 to 210, when the seasons were warmer and wetter.

The water wrote its own diary on the walls, one thin line a season, and nobody knew it was doing so. It is still there. It is still readable. It asks nothing of you tonight.

The channel reaches the town at last, on the high ground on its northern edge.

And here the water is divided. It runs into a round stone basin, cut down into the rock. About six meters across. About a meter and a half deep. Its rim is a low wall of dressed stone. Its floor is smooth.

Around the wall of the basin, ten round openings are cut. Each one about forty centimeters wide, the width of a dinner plate. Out of each opening ran a lead pipe. And each pipe went to a different quarter of the town.

The basin is cut down into the living rock, not built up out of blocks. Somebody stood on this hillside with a pick and made a circle in it. The tool marks are still there. The ten holes are as round as a dinner plate, and as smooth, and evenly spaced around the wall.

The Romans called this the castellum. A little castle for water. Really it is a quiet roundabout. Water in from one side. Water out through ten doors. Some to the fountains. Some to the public buildings. Some to the houses of those who paid for it.

Imagine standing at its rim at dusk. The water arrives in a smooth glassy sheet, and spreads, and turns slowly in the basin. The surface catches the last pale light of the sky. Ten small currents pull toward ten dark mouths. Nobody is watching. It sorts itself.

Let us follow one of the pipes down into the streets.

Nîmes in the first century is a handsome town. Straight streets. Pale stone. A great amphitheater, and a temple with clean lines. And on the corners of the streets, fountains.

They are simple things. A stone basin, waist high. A carved spout, with the water coming out of it in a steady arc. Water into the basin, day and night. There is no tap. Nothing turns it off. It runs, and runs, and the basin brims, and the surplus spills over the lip into a gutter in the street.

That overflow is on purpose. It keeps the water fresh. It washes the gutters. It carries on down the slope of the town, and out.

In one Roman town whose streets still stand, more than thirty of these corner fountains have been counted. Nobody there lived far from one. You could carry a full jar home without once setting it down.

The stone around a spout wears into a hollow, where hands have rested on it for a hundred years. The basin stays cool all day. There is a little green weed at the waterline, waving slightly. And the overflow keeps one corner of the street permanently dark, and wet, and a few degrees cooler than the rest.

So a Roman street sounds like this. A soft steady splash at every corner. A trickle in the gutters. A sheen of wet stone where the overflow runs. People come with jars, and fill them, and go home. Children put their hands in. A dog drinks. The water goes on arriving.

Some of the pipes lead on to the baths, and we can hear that water too, from the street, without going in. A deeper sound, behind a wall. Warmth, and echo, and the smell of oil. We will leave it there tonight, and stay out here in the cool, by the fountain.

Now let us widen the view, gently, and think of Rome itself.

Rome was fed by more of these channels than any city in the world. The first, the Aqua Appia, was begun in the year 312 before Christ. It ran almost entirely underground. Nobody would have seen it.

Others followed, over the centuries. The Aqua Claudia and the Anio Novus were begun together in the year 38 and finished together in the year 52. They came from the hills to the east, and their arches marched across the plain. By the third century the city had eleven aqueducts in all. Eleven separate rivers, arriving quietly, every day.

Between them, those eleven channels brought the city an enormous quantity of water. Something like half a million cubic meters a day. Some counts put it closer to a million. For roughly a million people, arriving by gravity alone, hour after hour, out of the hills.

In the year 97, the emperor appointed a man named Frontinus to look after them. He had been a soldier and a governor. Now he was the water commissioner. And he did something very sensible. He walked the lines. He measured them. And he wrote a book about them, in two parts.

He wrote down where each channel started, and how long it was, and how much water it gave. He wrote down the rules for who could take water and who could not. He wrote down where the pipes were leaking. He noted, with some satisfaction, that his measurements were most reliable at the settling tanks, where the water was calm.

It is a plain, careful, unhurried book. A list of channels, written by a man who liked them. It is still read.

There is something restful about a person like that. He did not build any of it. He went and looked at all of it, and counted, and set it down clearly. Some of what we know about this water we know because one tidy official walked the length of it.

Not every valley could be crossed with a bridge.

Sometimes the valley was too deep. Then the Romans did something that sounds impossible, and is simply true. They ran the water downhill into the valley, and then back up the other side.

It works because water seeks its own level. If you pour water into one end of a U-shaped pipe, it rises at the other end to nearly the same height. So the channel enters a sealed pipe at the top of one slope. The pipe runs down the hill, across the valley floor, and up the far slope. And the water comes out again, only a little lower than it went in.

The pipes were lead, made in lengths and soldered together. At Lyon, in France, the longest channel was about eighty-five kilometers, and it crossed four valleys this way. Several pipes side by side, cased in concrete, running down and across and up.

At Aspendos, in what is now Turkey, the pipe was stone. It crossed a valley more than a kilometer and a half wide. And at two points along the way it rose on tall towers, some forty meters high, to let the pressure ease before going on.

Lead was chosen because it can be worked. It bends. It can be joined. It takes the weight of a deep valley without splitting, if the walls are made thick enough. At Lyon they laid several pipes side by side rather than one great one, and cased them in concrete, and let the hillside hold them.

It is a calm idea, when you sit with it. The water goes down into the dark, and comes back up into the light, and carries on.

There is one more aqueduct worth seeing tonight, far to the west, in Spain.

The town is Segovia, high on a rock. The water came from a mountain river, the Río Frío, the cold river, about seventeen kilometers away. It ran through hills, and into a settling tank at the edge of the town, where the sand fell out. Then through a second house, to settle again. And then across a wide dip in the ground, on arches.

The arches are granite. Dark gray Guadarrama granite, from the mountains. Some twenty-four thousand blocks of it. And not one drop of mortar between them. The blocks simply sit on one another, cut to fit. At the deepest point of the dip there are two rows of arches, one on the other, nearly twenty-nine meters high.

Something like a hundred and sixty-five arches, marching across the town. Built, most likely, in the time of the emperor Trajan or just after, early in the second century.

The channel along the top is only a small trough cut in the stone. A shallow U, about half a meter deep, and about as wide. All that granite, all those arches, to carry something you could step across.

And the pillars are thicker at the foot than at the head, the way a tree is. About three meters across down at the bottom. Nearer two at the top. It is a shape that sheds weight downward, quietly, without needing to be told.

Put your hand on the granite in your mind. It is cool and coarse, with a glitter of mica in it. It has been holding itself up, without help, for nearly nineteen hundred years. The town has grown around its feet. People walk under it on their way home. Its shadow moves across the square all day, like the hand of a clock.

Let us go back now, to the bridge over the Gardon, because the light has gone.

It is night. The moon is up, and the stone has turned from gold to silver-gray. The river below is a soft dark ribbon, with moonlight lying on it in broken pieces that move, and reform, and move again.

High in the middle row of arches, something stirs. An owl. It has been sitting in the shadow of the stone, quite still, all this time. Now it turns its head. Then it drops from the arch, and opens its wings, and glides down the valley. Not a sound. Not one. A pale shape going away over the water.

The arches stand behind it in the moonlight. Nothing else moves.

The stone has been holding the day's heat, and is giving it back now, slowly, into the night air. If you laid your palm flat on it you would feel that. Warm on top. Cool underneath. The swallows have gone to bed in the joints of the upper row.

And over the top of them, in the dark, inside its red-lined channel, the water goes on. Two and a half centimeters, across the whole bridge. Cool, and clear, and quiet. Carrying a little limestone with it, as always. Writing one more thin bright line on the wall.

And down in the town, the fountains go on running.

The streets are empty now. The jars are all filled and taken home. But the water does not know that. It arrives at the corner, and pours from the spout, and brims the basin, and spills into the gutter. The same steady splash it made all day. A little louder now, in the quiet.

Up on the hill, in the round basin, the water turns slowly and finds its ten doors. Out in the fields, under the vines, it slides along in the dark, under lids no one has lifted since morning. In the tunnel at Sernhac it moves past the pick marks and the lamp niches. On the bridge it crosses the valley without a sound.

And back at the spring, out of the foot of the limestone, it goes on rising. Cool. Clear. Faintly tasting of stone. Enough for a whole town, and never in a hurry. It falls the width of a forearm in every kilometer, and that is enough.

Nothing in this whole long line is doing anything difficult. It is only leaning, very slightly, toward the sea. And letting the water find the way.

Fifty kilometers of stone, and not one moving part in it. Nothing to wind. Nothing to switch on. Nothing that can be forgotten at the end of a long day. Only a slope, held steady, and something patient running down it.

So let it be quiet now.

Let the owl go on down the valley. Let the moon go on lying on the river. Let the fountains splash at their empty corners, and the gutters run, and the basin turn its slow circle on the hill.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the numbers, not the names, not the slope of the channel, not a single stone of the bridge. It will all keep itself. It has been keeping itself for two thousand years, without any help from you.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let your thoughts settle, the way silt settles in a still basin. Softly, to the floor, out of the way. Let the cool of the water come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
