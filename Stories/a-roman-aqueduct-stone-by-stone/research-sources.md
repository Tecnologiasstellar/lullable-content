# Research sources — A Roman Aqueduct, Stone by Stone

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pont_du_Gard — aqueduct length ~50 km Uzès to Nîmes; total drop 17 m; average gradient 1 in 3,000; bridge 48.8 m high, 274 m long; gradient across the bridge 2.5 cm in 456 m; tiers of 6, 11 and 35 (originally 47) arches; blocks up to 6 tons, ~50,400 tons total; built largely without mortar or clamps; blocks left protruding to carry scaffolding; specus ~1.8 m high and 1.2 m wide lined with stucco containing crushed pottery and tile; 35 km of the route underground; Sernhac tunnels up to 66 m long; mineral deposits up to 50 cm thick on each wall; construction between AD 40 and 60.

https://archaeology-travel.com/france/nimes-aqueduct/ — drop of about 17 m over 50 km, average ~35 cm per km; Uzès and Nîmes only 20 km apart in a straight line while the channel runs 50 km; peak delivery ~40,000 m³/day; castellum basin 5.90 m diameter, ~1.40 m deep, ten openings ~40 cm across feeding lead pipes.

https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/13/1/54 — hydraulic study of the Pont du Gard aqueduct and the Nemausus (Nîmes) castellum; gradient precision and distribution at the castellum.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6435730/ — Fontaine d'Eure spring at 72 m asl in a Barremian limestone massif, Nîmes at 59 m asl; karst water enriched in dissolved calcium and bicarbonate; concretions 15–20 cm thick; millimetric bright/dark laminae couplets, one pair per year; record spans ~50–225 AD, about 175 years; reduced growth ~90–108 AD and ~125–145 AD (drier), higher growth ~175–210 AD (warmer and wetter).

https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-41620-4 — same study (abstract): laminated carbonate deposits of the Nîmes aqueduct as a seasonal-resolution paleoenvironmental record of the first centuries AD.

http://www.romanaqueducts.info/technicalintro/sinter.htm — sinter deposits in Roman aqueducts generally; clear massive sinter indicates clean flowing water, roughly AD 100–250 at Nîmes.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_aqueduct — most conduits ran underground; typical channel ~0.7 m wide and 1.5 m high, 0.5–1 m below the surface; channels ran about two-thirds full; of Rome's ~780–800 km of conduit only ~47 km ran above ground on masonry; waterproof smooth linings; inspection and access points at regular intervals; sedimentation tanks; Vitruvius's recommended gradient of not less than 1 in 4,800; Pont du Gard capacity up to 40,000 m³/day at 34 cm per km; siphons of soldered lead, nine pipes in parallel cased in concrete.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chorobates — chorobates: a wooden beam about 6 m long on two legs, plumb lines with matching notches at each end, and a groove along the top filled with water when wind made the plumb lines unreliable; described by Vitruvius in Book VIII of De architectura.

https://www.aveva.com/en/our-industrial-life/type/article/how-the-romans-built-the-aqueducts/ — the three surveying instruments: groma (pole with a cross and four plumb lines, for straight lines and right angles), dioptra (rotating plate for angles and levels), chorobates (praised by Vitruvius as the most reliable, a ~20-foot table with plumb lines and a water level, set up in the channel itself).

http://www.romanaqueducts.info/technicalintro/surveyingtools.htm — Roman surveying tools for aqueducts; limits of the chorobates over long distances.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_signinum — opus signinum: lime mortar with crushed pottery, tile and amphora fragments as aggregate; waterproof, hence its use in baths, aqueducts and cisterns; hardens under water.

https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/year-7/roman-aqueducts/ — the specus covered with a roof or vault; opus signinum lining of crushed tiles and amphorae; access shafts and entry holes for cleaning; shafts (putei) at intervals of around 230 feet, following the Persian qanat technique; removal of algae, gravel and travertine deposits.

https://www.worldatlas.com/ancient-world/how-the-romans-built-aqueducts-that-still-carry-water.html — specus roughly 0.7 m wide by 1.5 m high, lined with opus signinum plaster; repair practice.

https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-02144-5 — mortar analysis of a Roman aqueduct: cocciopesto/opus signinum used systematically as waterproof coating in water conduits; quarter-round fillets sealing the joint between wall and floor.

https://www.archaeoreporter.com/en/2021/01/03/the-roman-cocciopesto/ — cocciopesto: crushed ceramic reacting with lime and water to set even in wet conditions.

https://engineeringrome.org/aqueducts-equity-and-public-health-perspective/ — piscina limaria (settling tanks): slowing the water so particles settle out; located at the source, part way along, or at the terminus; the simplest were only a widening of the channel; Frontinus's remark that his measurements were most reliable at a settling tank.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_aqueducts_in_the_city_of_Rome — Aqua Appia begun 312 BC; Aqua Claudia and Anio Novus begun AD 38 and completed AD 52; 11 aqueducts by AD 226; combined delivery of roughly 520,000 to 1.1 million m³ per day for about a million people; Frontinus as curator aquarum and principal source.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_aquaeductu — Frontinus's two-book official report on the aqueducts of Rome: history, description, capacity, and the laws on use and maintenance.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curator_Aquarum — Frontinus appointed curator aquarum by Nerva in AD 97.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_of_Segovia — unmortared granite blocks; 167 arches (Britannica: some 165); maximum height 28.5 m; water from the Río Frío about 17 km away; settling tank El Caserón and the Casa de Aguas for a second decantation; U-shaped channel 0.55 m by 0.46 m; pillars 2.4 × 3 m at the base tapering to 1.8 × 2.5 m at the top; completion most likely after AD 112, under Trajan or early Hadrian.

https://www.britannica.com/topic/Segovia-aqueduct — some 24,000 Guadarrama granite blocks laid without mortar; 728 m of above-ground arcade; some 165 arches; two tiers at the central dip, 28.5 m above ground; built under Trajan.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_of_the_Gier — the Gier aqueduct at Lyon, about 85 km long, the longest known Roman aqueduct, with four inverted siphons crossing deep valleys on pipe bridges.

https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-archaeology/article/abs/aqueduct-at-aspendos-and-its-inverted-siphon/45E23D62C46EEFC8E2A7059FB5E07858 — the 17 km Aspendos aqueduct and its 1,670 m siphon with two hydraulic towers about 40 m high.

http://www.romanaqueducts.info/picturedictionary/pd_onderwerpen/siphon.htm — inverted siphons: water descending into sealed lead or stone pipe and rising the far side under hydrostatic pressure; header tanks at each end.

https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X22004321 — public street fountains (lacus): a stone basin and spout fed by one or two pipes, running constantly, with surplus drained away through an overflow into the gutter; 35 street fountains surveyed in one Roman town.

https://www.britannica.com/topic/Pont-du-Gard — Pont du Gard construction, three tiers, and the central span of 24.5 m, the widest of the first century AD.

https://nimes-tourisme.com/en/explorer/nimes/monuments-et-sites/le-castellum/ — the castellum divisorium at Nîmes: circular basin cut into the rock, ten pipe openings arranged around the wall, feeding the quarters of the city; mid first century AD.
