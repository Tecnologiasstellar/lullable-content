Good evening.

The day is over, and you have come through it, and now there is nothing left to steer. Whatever seas you crossed today, you crossed them. The harbor has you now. Let the bed hold you the way still water holds a boat. Nothing to do but float.

Let your hands give up their grip. Let your face go smooth. Let your breathing lengthen out, slow as swells in deep water.

Tonight we are going out to an island. A small one, far off a cold green coast, where the wind has opinions and the rain visits often. On its highest shoulder stands a lighthouse, white as a gull, and in the lighthouse lives a keeper, and the keeper is about to begin the night watch. We will keep it with him. Which sounds like work, but a lighthouse watch is mostly this. Warmth, and routine, and rain on glass, and one great light turning slowly all night, taking care of everyone else.

If the wind takes some of the words away, let it have them. The light does not need words to keep turning. Take one slow breath in, salt and cold and clean. Let it out slow and warm. And there is the island now, off the bow, with the tower standing up from it like a raised white finger saying, gently, here.

§

The island is a mile of grass and granite, and the sea owns everything around it to every horizon.

No trees grow here. The wind saw to that long ago. What grows is tough springy turf, cropped short by a dozen opinionated sheep, and cushions of sea-pink that bloom in the lee of every boulder. Stone walls cross the slopes, holding nothing in particular, built by keepers of other centuries who needed something to do with their afternoons.

Seabirds own the cliffs. Fulmars ride the updrafts stiff-winged, around and around, going nowhere beautifully. Down in the coves, grey seals haul out on the weeded rocks and lie about like luggage, sighing. Their evening song drifts up the cliff sometimes, long mournful vowels that are not mournful at all, just seals, talking as seals do.

And above it all, the tower. Forty meters of granite, ringed with one red band, built by engineers who came out here a century and a half ago with numbers and stubbornness. Every stone was cut to lock into its neighbors, dovetailed like a carpenter's drawer, so the whole tower grips itself tighter the harder anything pushes. Waves have broken over its base ten thousand times. The tower has never once been impressed. Tonight the sea is only murmuring, and the tower stands over its island the way a lighthouse always stands. Perfectly still, perfectly awake.

§

The keeper's day ends where everyone else's does, except his ending is a beginning.

He has spent the afternoon at the small maintenance of a small kingdom. Brass polished. Windows wiped of salt. The log written up in careful copperplate. Weather, wind, shipping sighted, seals counted, nothing amiss. Lighthouse logs are the dullest books ever written, and that is their glory. A hundred years of pages, and the best entry any keeper can write is the one that says all well.

Now the sun is down in the western water, and he climbs the stair to light up. Ninety-nine steps, spiraling up inside the granite, his hand on the same cold rail his predecessors wore smooth. He does not count the steps. His legs count them for him. Past the oil room, past the watch room with its armchair and its little stove, up into the lantern room at the top, the glass crown of the whole island. And there, waiting in the last of the daylight, stands the lens.The lens is the treasure of the tower, and there is nothing else like it in the made world.

It stands taller than the keeper, a great beehive of glass, hundreds of prisms fitted in gleaming rings around one small lamp at its heart. A Frenchman named Fresnel worked it out, two centuries ago. How to take a modest flame and, with ring upon ring of angled glass, gather every stray scrap of its light and fold it into one single level beam that can reach over the curve of the sea. Before his lens, a lighthouse glowed like a lamp in a window. After it, a lighthouse could lay a finger of light on a ship twenty miles out.

In daylight the lens just stands there being beautiful, throwing little rainbows around the lantern room like a chandelier with time on its hands. The keeper lights the lamp at its center, and trims it, and steps back. And the whole hive wakes. Every prism fills. The glass goes from cold to golden, floor to crown.

Then he starts the turning. The whole floating tonnage of glass rests on a bath of mercury, friction's final surrender, so perfectly balanced that one hand can set it moving. Below it hangs the clockwork, a drum of cable and a heavy weight in the tower's throat. The keeper cranks the weight to the top, the way you wind a grandfather clock the size of a building. And as the weight eases itself down the shaft, hour by hour, foot by foot, it turns the lens above. Slow, and level, and silent. The light does not flash, whatever sailors say. The light only turns. It is the turning that makes the flash, sweeping its one long beam past every point of the horizon in its own good time.

Every lighthouse on the coast turns to its own rhythm, its character, printed on the charts. This one says: white, three flashes, every fifteen seconds. Out in the dark, a skipper counts the beats against his watch and knows exactly which friend he is looking at. The lights are a language, and every sentence in it means the same thing. You are here, and we are watching, and the rocks are there, and go safely by.

§

With the light turning, the keeper's night settles into the watch room, one floor down, and the watch room is the coziest room on any chart.

Round as a drum, snug as a ship's cabin. The little stove ticking with heat. The armchair, shaped by forty years of keepers, that receives him like an opinion he agrees with. The kettle goes on. The logbook lies open to tonight's page, with its first entry already made. Lamp lit at sunset. All well.

Above his head, through the ceiling, comes the sound he will hear all night and has long stopped hearing. The soft rumble of the lens going round, regular as a sleeping heart. Every few hours he will climb up, crank the weight back to the top, note the weather, and come down again. Between rounds there is tea, and a book, and the wind reading along over his shoulder. That is the whole of a keeper's night. It has been called the loneliest job in the world by people who never did it. The ones who did it mostly missed it all their lives.Below the watch room, down two turns of the stair, are the living quarters, and they are a masterclass in small comfort.

Everything is round, because the tower is. The bunk curves with the wall, and lies in it like a nut in its shell. The table is a crescent, scrubbed white with a century of salt and elbows. The dresser is built into the granite itself, and holds thick china that has survived a hundred gales by being too heavy to bother falling. On the shelf, a row of books swollen with damp and rereading. A pack of cards. A tin of good tea, and a tin of better tea for Sundays.

The lamp here is small and yellow, and the stove keeps the whole curved room at the temperature of drowsiness. Keepers of the old service grew famous for their baking, out of sheer time and a good oven. Bread on Mondays, a fruit cake when the boat brought currants. The smell of it would climb the stairs and fill the tower to the lantern, and men off watch would wake for it, and come down in their socks.

Once a fortnight, weather willing, the supply boat comes out from the mainland. Flour, oil, letters, news already a week old and none the worse for it. The keeper trades a mailbag of his own. Letters home, the log's fair copy, a list of small wants. More tea. Wick trimmers. Blackcurrant jam, if there is any to be had. Then the boat dwindles back toward the coast, and the island closes gently around its keeper again, and he watches it go with no sorrow whatsoever. Some people are built for towers, the way some trees are built for cliffs. He winds the clock. He feeds the stove. He is exactly where he means to be.

§

The old keepers left their stories in the tower too, and on rain nights they keep the new keeper company.

Scratched on the oil room doorframe, a line of initials and years, going back past anyone's memory. Every man who kept this light left his letters there, small and neat, like signing the last page of a long calm book. One of them kept a garden in the lee of the wall, and his rhubarb outlived him, and comes up still, every spring, stubborn as an anniversary. One taught himself the fiddle across twenty winters, badly, happily, and the seals are said to have gathered for it, which says more about seals than fiddling. One wrote in the log, on a night of terrible weather long ago, the finest sentence the tower owns. Sea very heavy, tower trembling, light turning true, all well.

§

That is the inheritance of this place. Not heroics. Steadiness. A hundred years of ordinary men getting up in the small hours to wind a weight, so that strangers they would never meet could pass a rock they would never see. The kindness of it is almost invisible, which is the best kind. The whole tower is built of it, stone on stone, night on night, all well upon all well.

§

Near midnight, the rain arrives, and a lighthouse is the finest building on Earth to hear rain from.

It comes in off the sea in a long soft curtain, and you hear it cross the water before it lands. A hush, growing. Then the first drops find the lantern glass high above, and the watch room windows, and the granite shoulders of the tower, and the whole building begins to murmur.

Tower rain is different from house rain. The tower is round, so the rain never rattles. It streams. It wraps the granite in moving water and slides down all forty meters in one continuous whisper, and the tower stands in it like a man in a warm shower, entirely unbothered. On the glass, the drops bead and run, bead and run. And every fifteen seconds the great beam sweeps through the falling rain and turns a million drops to silver, all at once, a curtain of sparks, gone, and back, and gone. The keeper looks up from his book to watch it, the way you look up at a good fire. Not because anything needs him. Because it is lovely.

§

The wind rises a little with the rain, and the island gives its nightly concert. Wind in the wire fences, a low hum. Wind over the cliff edge, a note like breath over a bottle. The swells arrive a little taller now, and boom softly in the sea caves under the north cliff, a sound felt in the floor as much as heard. None of it is weather to worry a keeper. He has a word for a night like this in the log. Fresh. Fresh, with rain. All well.

And out in that fresh dark, the light is doing its whole silent work. Somewhere beyond the rain, a coaster is thumping north with her cargo, and on her bridge a mate glances out, counts three flashes, checks his watch, and marks his position with a pencil. He does not think about the tower. He does not have to. That is the entire point of it. The most watched-over people in the world are the ones free to think of something else. Tonight, in this bed, that includes you.

§

Deep in the small hours, the keeper makes his last round, and it is a kind of lullaby performed in granite.

Up the spiral once more, boots quiet on the stone. Crank the weight to the top of its shaft, sixty smooth turns, the clockwork taking tomorrow morning into its hands. A wipe of the inner glass. A glance at the lamp, burning steady in its crystal hive. Outside, the rain is easing, and far off through the last of it, he can see the answering pulse of the next lighthouse down the coast, and beyond it, the faint loom of the one after that. The whole coastline, blinking gently to itself, all night, every night. A necklace of patient fires, each one tended by someone warm and unhurried.

He writes the last entry of the watch in the old copperplate. Three a.m. Wind easing. Rain passing. Light turning true. All well. All well. A hundred years of nights in that logbook, and how many of its pages say only that. Storms came, and were outlasted, and even their pages end the same way. It is the most reassuring literature ever written, and tonight you are a line in it.

He banks the stove. He settles into the armchair with the blanket kept for exactly this hour. Above him the lens rumbles round, and round, needing nothing. The weight will fall till dawn. The beam will turn till the sun relieves it. The rain washes the tower, the sea rocks the island, the seals are long asleep in the coves, and every ship on the dark water knows exactly where home is.

Let the beam sweep past you one more time, slow and silver, and let it carry the last of the words away with it. Keep nothing. The light will keep it all for you.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
