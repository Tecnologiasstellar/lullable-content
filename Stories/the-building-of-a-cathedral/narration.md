Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is over now. It is done with. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing find its own slower pace. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are going to a small city in the middle of the thirteenth century. In it, the largest thing anyone has ever seen is slowly being built.

It is a cathedral. It has been rising for sixty years already. It will not be finished for another eighty, at the very least, and everyone working on it knows that.

Nobody minds. That is the heart of this story, and it is a very restful heart. We are going to spend an evening among people who are entirely at peace with not finishing.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

It is late afternoon, in autumn, and you are coming into the city through its western gate.

The streets are narrow and crooked, and busy with the end of the day. Geese being driven home. A water-carrier with his yoke. Woodsmoke drifting flat over the rooftops, and under it the smell of supper starting in a hundred kitchens.

The houses lean toward each other overhead, two and three storeys of timber and plaster, so the streets run like tunnels of shadow.

And then a street opens out, and there it is.

It rises out of the middle of the little city the way a mountain rises out of foothills. The houses come up to about the height of its doorways. Everything above that belongs to another order of things entirely.

Half of it is whole. The eastern end stands complete, roofed, its windows glowing faintly from the lamps inside.

And half of it is scaffolding. The great towers at the west end stop abruptly in mid-air. They are wrapped in timber platforms, and ladders, and hoisting wheels, open to the sky. The ropes of the cranes sway gently in the evening breeze.

It is the largest unfinished thing in the world, and it is beautiful precisely the way an unfinished thing is beautiful. You can see how it is made.

§

Stand here at the edge of the close and take in the time of it, because the time is the marvel.

The men who laid the first stones of this building are dead. So are most of their sons. The masons working today inherited the work from fathers and grandfathers. They will hand it on to sons and grandsons who have not been born.

A great cathedral took a century easily. Many took far longer. The grandest of them all, in Germany, was begun in twelve forty-eight. It stood unfinished for so long that the crane on its stump of a tower became the emblem of the city. It was finally completed six hundred and thirty-two years after it was begun.

Think about what that means about the people. A man spends forty years cutting stone for a building he has never seen whole, and never will. His name is nowhere. His work is three courses of the north tower, and a doorway, and part of a vault.

And he goes home in the evening content. Not resigned. Content.

We have almost lost the knack of that. These people had it the way they had hands. The work was the point. The finishing was for whoever God granted the finishing to.

§

Cross the close, between the stacked timber and the piles of sand, toward a long low building leaning against the cathedral's flank.

This is the lodge. It is the heart of the whole enterprise, and it is simply a workshop. A wooden hall with wide doors, benches down both sides, and a floor deep in stone chips.

The working day is ending. A dozen masons are at their benches still, finishing their cuts in the last good light. The sound is lovely. Not hammering, nothing violent. A light, ringing tapping, many mallets on many chisels, like a conversation of patient woodpeckers.

Stone dust hangs in the doorway light and turns gold.

In winter, when frost stops the building work — mortar cannot set in a frost — the lodge stays busy. The masons spend the cold months in here, cutting the hundreds of stones the walls will need in spring. Each one is cut to its template, and stacked ready. The building rises in summer. It is imagined, and prepared, in winter.

The lodge has its own customs, its own rules, its own quiet pride. A man trained in this lodge can walk to a building site five hundred miles away and be known by how he works.

§

The stone itself comes from twenty miles away, and its journey is a story of its own.

The quarry was chosen before the first foundation was dug, and chosen with enormous care. Masters travelled to look at stone the way wine men travel to look at slopes. They wanted a limestone that cuts sweetly when it is fresh, and hardens honestly in the weather. They knew the good beds by name.

At the quarry, the stone is not blasted. It is persuaded. The quarrymen find the natural seams and work along them with wedges. They tap in lines of iron and wood, patient as dentists, until the block agrees to part from its bed.

And here is a lovely economy. The blocks are rough-shaped at the quarry, before they travel. Every pound of waste stone trimmed off there is a pound the oxen do not have to haul. The quarry floor is deep in chips, and the carts leave carrying stones already half way to being architecture.

Then the river does the heavy work. Barges bring the stone down, a few great blocks at a time, to the wharf below the city. Hauling by water costs a fraction of hauling by road. Cathedral cities are river cities, almost every one, and this is a large part of why.

Freshly quarried stone is soft, full of its own quarry water. The masons say it is green, like timber. It cuts best then. So the carving is done while the stone is young. The stone hardens afterward, up on the wall, curing in the weather like everything else in this place.

§

At the far end of the lodge there is a plain door. Behind it is the most important room in the whole city. It does not look like much.

It is a long attic room with a smooth plaster floor. And scratched into that floor, line over line, is the cathedral.

This is the tracing floor. There are no drawings on parchment here — parchment is for treaties and gospels, far too dear to cover with sketches. When the master mason wants to work out a window, or an arch, or the curve of a vault rib, he comes here. He draws it. On the floor, with compasses and a straightedge and a scriber. At full size.

A window ten feet tall is drawn ten feet tall. The carpenters lay their timber directly on the lines to make the templates. The templates go down to the benches, and the stone is cut to the templates, and the stone goes up onto the wall.

The floor is a palimpsest now, after sixty years. Window over arch over gable, faint lines under fresh ones, the thinking of three generations of masters scratched into one white surface.

Nobody has ever drawn this whole building. It has only ever been drawn one problem at a time, at full size, on this floor. The cathedral exists complete in one place only. In the master mason's head. And it is handed from head to head, like a tune.

§

The master mason himself is worth a moment. He is standing in the doorway of the lodge now, talking quietly with the carpenter about tomorrow.

He is not a priest, and not a lord. He is a craftsman who rose. He served his apprenticeship like everyone else, and cut stone like everyone else. Over the years he showed the thing that cannot quite be taught. The feel for how forces move through a building.

Because that is his real work. Every pound of this enormous building is trying to reach the ground. The whole art is giving all that weight a good road to travel.

He carries the geometry in his fingers. Squares, and compasses, and a knotted cord, and a handful of proportions held as closely as recipes. Twice the base gives the height. The diagonal of the square sets the spire. Simple rules, compounded into towers.

When he dies, another master will step up, usually from this lodge, sometimes summoned from another city. The new man will stand on the tracing floor, and read his predecessor's lines, and carry on.

The building does not notice the change. That is the design.

§

Two more people belong to this evening, before we go inside.

The first is the smith. His forge stands at the end of the lodge. His fire is the one fire on this site that never quite goes out.

He shoes no horses and makes no swords. His whole trade is edges. Stone eats iron. A mason's chisel dulls in an hour of cutting. So all day, every day, the masons' boys trot to the forge with baskets of blunted tools, and trot back with sharp ones.

He is drawing his fire down for the night now, banking the coals. Tomorrow, before the first mason reaches his bench, there will be two hundred fresh edges waiting, laid out in rows. The whole ringing music of the lodge depends on this one quiet man with his wheel and his water trough.

The second is the newest apprentice, who is sweeping the forge floor, because that is what the newest apprentice does.

He is perhaps thirteen. He will sweep, and fetch, and turn the grindstone, and mix mortar, for a year or two. Then they will give him his first block of stone, and a square. They will set him the first task every mason is ever set. Make this face flat. Then make this face flat, and square, to that one.

It sounds like nothing. It takes months to do well. It is the whole craft in miniature. Patience, and truth to a line. And the humility of checking your work against a straightedge that does not flatter.

Seven years from now they will give him his mark. Fifty years from now, if God is kind, he will be the old man in the lodge whom everyone asks about the north tower. He will be the only one left who remembers building it.

§

Now come inside the finished half, through the small door in the great door, and stand still.

The first thing that happens is the ceiling takes your eyes with it. You look up because the whole building is arranged to make you look up, and your gaze goes a hundred feet before it lands.

Up there is the vault — a ceiling of stone, curved like sails, ribbed like leaves, floating over the whole vast room.

Stone should not float. Stone is the heaviest thing these people handle. And that is precisely the point. The miracle is arranged, and it is arranged with two inventions, and they are both quietly visible from where you stand.

Look at the arches. They are not round. They are pointed — two curves leaning together, meeting at a tip, like hands touching in prayer.

A round arch pushes its weight outward, hard, at its haunches. It wants to burst the walls apart, and it can only go so high before it must be squat. But a pointed arch sends its weight more steeply downward. It presses instead of shoving. It can rise, and rise, and carry its load down the columns like rain down a spout.

The pointed arch is why this room is tall. It is the whole secret, and it is drawn on the tracing floor upstairs a hundred times.

§

It is worth knowing how a stone ceiling is built at all, because stone cannot float while you are laying it.

The answer is that the vault is built twice. First in wood, then in stone.

The carpenters build the shape of the ceiling in timber, up on the scaffolding. A whole curved framework, called the centering, standing on props. It holds its arcs up under the sky, exactly where the stone arcs are going to be.

Then the masons lay the ribs over it, wedge-shaped stone after wedge-shaped stone, resting on the wood. The ribs meet at the crown, and a carved boss locks them like a knot. Then the webbing between the ribs, thin courses of light stone, leaning together.

And then comes a quiet, careful morning, months later, when the mortar has had its first season to firm. The carpenters go under the vault and, a little at a time, ease the wedges under the props. The centering sinks away from the stone by the width of a coin.

And the vault stands. It takes its own weight, every stone pressing on its neighbours. The whole curved ceiling holds itself, for the first time, and forever after.

The masons call it striking the centering. There is no drama to it when it is done right. The wood comes down, the stone stays up, and the carpenters carry the timbers along to the next bay, and begin again.

A cathedral vault is a hundred small mornings like that, in a row.

§

The second invention is outside. Look out through the glass, at the flanks of the building, where rows of stone arms leap from the walls.

They spring from tall outer piers, and arc through the open air. They land against the building's sides, high up, exactly where the vault pushes hardest.

They are called flying buttresses, and the name is honest. They are bridges for weight. The vault shoves its remaining outward push into them, and they carry it. Through the air, over the roofs of the side aisles. Out, and down, into the great piers, and so into the earth.

The building is leaning on crutches, and has turned the crutches into wings.

And here is the consequence, and it is the whole reason for everything. Because the weight travels down the columns and out through the flying arms, the walls between the columns are carrying almost nothing.

A wall that carries nothing does not need to be wall.

So they made it glass.

§

The glass is the reward for all that engineering. At this hour of the evening, it is doing the thing it was made for.

The western light is coming in low, through windows forty feet tall, and the windows are not pictures of light. They are walls of it. Blue, above all — a deep, ringing, sapphire blue that this century is famous for and later centuries will struggle to reproduce. And with it, ruby, and green, and a gold like cider.

The colours are not painted on. They are in the glass — metals melted into it while it was liquid. Cobalt makes that blue. Copper the greens, and the reds. The glaziers cut the coloured sheets into thousands of pieces and lead them together, picture by picture, panel by panel.

The stories in the windows are for daytime. What matters now, at dusk, is what the light itself does. It comes through those thousands of pieces and lies along the stone floor in slow pools of colour. Blue, then rose, then blue again. The pools lengthen, and slide, imperceptibly, as the sun goes down.

The whole room is a lantern being turned, very slowly, by the turning of the earth.

A cathedral, someone here will tell you, is built of three things. Stone, light, and time. The stone is merely the part you can touch.

§

There is one more world above the vault, and almost nobody ever sees it.

Climb, in your mind, up the spiral stair in the wall. Two hundred steps, round and round, past arrow-slit windows. Come out into the dark space between the vault below and the roof above.

It is like standing inside a wooden hill. Great oak trusses march away down the length of the building. Rafter after rafter. Tie-beam after tie-beam. They hold up the hundreds of tonnes of the lead roof.

The carpenters call a roof like this a forest. It is the right word. It took a genuine forest to build — hundreds of great oaks, chosen standing, some of them already two centuries old when they were felled. The carpenters walked the woods with the master, years ahead of need, marking trees. This curve for that brace. This straight giant for a tie-beam.

The timber up here still smells faintly of oak, decades on. The air is warm and dusty and utterly still. Below your feet, through the stone, you can just hear the evening office beginning. The chant comes up through the vault, softened to a murmur. It is like singing heard through snow.

§

Underneath everything, holding everything, there is a quiet substance that deserves a word of its own. The mortar.

It is humble stuff. Lime, burned from limestone in a kiln, slaked in pits of water where it seethes and settles, then mixed with sharp sand. The masons spread it in thin grey beds, and lay the stones upon it, course by course.

But lime mortar has one property that shapes this entire enterprise. It cures slowly.

Not in hours, like modern cement. In months, and years, and decades. It hardens by drinking the air — taking back, breath by breath, the very gas the kiln once drove out of the limestone. It is, quite literally, turning back into stone. Slowly, in place, inside the walls.

The masons know this in their hands. You do not rush a lime building. You do not stack too much height in one season, because the lower courses are still soft, still settling, still becoming themselves. The building must firm up under its own weight, year by year, like bread proving.

So the pace of the work is not set by impatience, or by money, or even by the bishop. It is set by the chemistry of lime. The cathedral rises at the speed at which it can become solid.

The building teaches everyone patience. It will not accept anything else.

And when the frosts come, the building is put to bed. Literally.

The tops of the unfinished walls, where the mortar is newest and wettest, are the tender part. A hard frost would freeze the water in the fresh beds and burst them, crumbling a summer's work.

So every autumn, before the cold arrives, the masons cap the walls. They lay on thick blankets of straw and reeds, weighted down, sometimes sealed over with turf. It runs all along the wall tops, the way a gardener earths up his beds before winter.

The cathedral spends every winter of its long unfinished life like that. Wrapped at the top, tucked in against the cold. Its newest work sleeps under straw, while the lodge rings all winter with the cutting of next year's stones.

An entire building, put to bed each year for a hundred years. It is hard to think of it as anything but a living thing, once you have seen it wrapped.

§

Look closely at the stones themselves, in this low evening light, and you will find the men.

Cut into face after face, small and neat, are marks. A crossed circle. A fish. A little windmill. Three notches like a bird's foot.

Every mason has his own mark, given to him when he finished his apprenticeship, and he cuts it into the stones he dresses. Partly it is the tally — many are paid by the stone, and the marks are counted at the week's end. Partly it is plainer than that. It says: mine. I made this one.

The marks are everywhere once you begin to see them. Hundreds of hands, all over the walls, at every height, from every decade of the work.

And they travel. Scholars, centuries from now, will trace a single mason's mark from this cathedral to another, and then to a third. A man walking across Europe with his tools and his mark. A few years here, a few years there. His small signature left at every stop.

No portraits of these men exist. No biographies. Just the marks, and the building. It is, when you think about it, not a bad way to be remembered. Better than most.

§

And who pays for all of this? The answer is gentler than you might guess. More or less everybody.

There is a fund. Every cathedral has one — the fabric fund, they call it, the money that belongs to the building itself. It has been fed, for sixty years, from a hundred small directions at once.

The bishop gives, and the canons give. The wool merchants give handsomely, and get their marks in a window for it. Pilgrims leave pennies at the shrine. Farmers leave a fleece at shearing time.

And all across the diocese, people remember the building in their wills. A widow leaves it her second-best kettle. A ploughman leaves fourpence. The clerks write every bequest into the fabric rolls, kettle and fourpence alike, year after year, and the rolls survive, and you can read them still.

Some years the fund runs full, and the walls climb. Some years it runs dry, and the work slows to a walk, and the crane stands idle on the tower, and everyone waits without much alarm.

The building has no single patron, which is why no single fortune's failure can stop it. It is being paid for the way it is being built. By many hands, slowly, each giving what it has.

§

The working day is over now. Listen — the site is putting itself to bed.

The masons rack their tools, each to its place. Chisels wiped, mallets hung. An apprentice sweeps the day's chips into the corner, gathering the good-sized bits for the rubble core of tomorrow's walling. Nothing is wasted here. Even the dust goes into the mortar.

Up on the unfinished tower, the last men come down the ladders, unhurried, one after another. It is the way men come down who do this every evening of their lives.

The great treadmill crane on the tower top is chocked and tied. Its rope is made fast. It will stand there against the sky all night, and all winter. If the money runs short, as it sometimes does, it may stand there for years, waiting. The city will simply get used to the sight of it. A crane on a tower is not a failure. It is a promise.

The watchman lights his brazier by the lodge door. The canons' voices drift from the finished choir. Swifts, who nest in the tower holes the masons leave for their scaffolding, go screaming around the heights once, twice, and settle.

The cathedral, whole and unwhole, breathes out.

§

And now it is night, and this is the part I want you to keep, if you keep anything.

The building is still working.

In the dark, in ten thousand joints, the mortar is drinking the night air, molecule by molecule, turning to stone. The walls are settling into themselves by hair's breadths, finding their final bearing, growing very slightly stronger tonight than they were last night.

The new stones cut this winter are waiting in the lodge, stacked in straw, already numbered for their places. The oaks for next summer's roof section are seasoning in the yard. On the tracing floor, the master's fresh lines wait in the dark for morning.

Nothing about this enterprise sleeps, exactly. It just works at different speeds. The men work by day. The lime works by decade. The building as a whole works by century, and it is never in a hurry, because it is not going anywhere. It is only becoming.

Sixty years done. Eighty to go. Every single person involved entirely at peace with that arithmetic.

There is nothing in your life, tonight, that needs to be finished by morning either.

§

One last picture, from outside the city, before we let the night have it.

Travellers on the road tomorrow morning, still two hours' walk away, will see the cathedral before they see anything else. The city itself lies hidden in its river valley. The cathedral stands up over the rim of the land like a grey ship seen over a hilltop.

For people walking in from the villages, that first sight is the journey's true beginning. Children ask what it is, every day, on every road into town. Parents answer without breaking stride, because the answer is part of the road now.

And here is the detail I find gentlest of all. To every one of those generations, the unfinished state is simply how it looks. Nobody alive has seen it any other way. The broken-off towers, the crane against the sky — that is the cathedral, as far as anyone walking that road has ever known.

A thing can be half-built for so long that its half-builtness becomes beloved. The finishing, when it comes at last, will take some getting used to.

§

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the pointed arches, or the flying stone arms, or the blue of the glass. Not the marks of the masons, or the forest above the vault, or the lines scratched into the white floor.

It is all still there, whether anyone thinks of it or not. The great buildings are standing tonight, in their quiet closes, in the middle of their cities. Still settling. Still curing, seven and eight centuries on. The patience is still in the walls.

Let the words go. Let the picture soften, and dim, like colours on a stone floor after sunset.

There is nothing out there that needs you tonight. There is nothing here that does either.

Only stone, and light, and time, and none of them in any hurry at all.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
