Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is over now. It is finished with. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down. Let the mattress take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing slow to whatever pace it likes. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are going to sit with a very small star. It is the nearest star to our Sun. It is also, in a quiet way, the most ordinary kind of star there is — because most stars are like this one.

It is dim, and cool, and deep orange. And it is in no hurry whatsoever. It burns so slowly that nothing like it has ever finished burning. Not once. Not anywhere.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin to travel.

We go out through the dark, and we go a long way.

Past the Moon. Past Mars. Past the wide thin belt of drifting rock. Past Jupiter with its cream and amber bands, and Saturn tilted on its side.

Past the cold outer planets. Past the last of the comets.

The Sun behind you shrinks and shrinks. At some point it stops being a disc and becomes simply the brightest star in the sky. Then it is only one bright star among many.

Now cross the gap. It is a wide one. Nothing at all happens for four and a quarter years of travelling at the speed of light.

And then, ahead, something small begins to glow.

Slow down, and let it come into view.

It is a ball of deep orange light. Not white. Not yellow. Orange, with a hint of red in it, like the last of a fire that has been left to itself.

It is small. About a seventh of the width of our Sun. If you set it beside Jupiter it would only be about half again as wide.

And it is dim. Genuinely, restfully dim. It gives off less than two thousandths of the light our Sun does, and most of that is infrared — warmth rather than glow.

You could look directly at it. That is the strange part. You could stand and watch it the way you watch a stove element going down. It would not hurt your eyes at all.

Its name is Proxima Centauri, which simply means the nearest one.

Here is the thing about it that took a long time to sink in.

This is what a star normally looks like.

About three quarters of all the stars in our galaxy are like this one. Small, cool, orange, and faint. They are the common case. They are the ordinary population.

And not one of them is visible from Earth without a telescope. Not a single one.

Every star you have ever seen with your own eyes is an exception. Every one in every constellation you know. All of them unusual, oversized, and short-lived. The bright ones are the outliers.

The actual sky, the real census of it, is a very large number of small quiet orange lights that nobody can see.

Nature makes them this way on purpose, more or less.

Stars form when a cold clump inside a cloud of gas gets dense enough to fall in on itself. The cloud breaks up into clumps of every size, all through it, at once.

And it turns out that small clumps are far more common than large ones. Not slightly more common. Overwhelmingly more.

For every very heavy star a cloud produces, it makes hundreds of small ones. The big ones are the rare accidents of the process. The small ones are what it does most of the time.

Then time does the rest of the sorting. The heavy ones use themselves up quickly and stop. The small ones simply stay.

So the galaxy gets steadily more dominated by these, in two ways at once. They were the common product to begin with, and they are also the ones that last.

The nearest one was found surprisingly late, and it was found the hard way.

In October of nineteen fifteen, a Scottish astronomer named Robert Innes was working at an observatory in Johannesburg. He was comparing photographs of the same patch of southern sky, taken years apart.

He was looking for stars that had shifted. A star that shifts quickly is usually a star that is close.

He found one, near the bright pair of Alpha Centauri, that was moving with them. It was faint — far too faint to see by eye — and it turned out to be nearer than either of them.

The closest star to our Sun. The single nearest object outside our own solar system. And nobody had noticed it until nineteen fifteen, because it is so dim.

He called it Proxima. The nearest one. The name stuck.

It is also, in the loosest possible sense, in orbit.

Alpha Centauri is a close pair of bright stars, going around each other every eighty years. Proxima sits a long way off from that pair — about thirteen thousand times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.

At that separation the pull is very faint indeed. But it appears to be enough.

One lap takes something like five hundred and fifty thousand years.

So in the whole span of anatomically modern humans, that small orange star has completed less than one circuit. It has been going around the same corner for the entire history of our species.

Nothing about it is in a hurry. Not even its orbit.

The reason they are dim comes down to weight, and it is quite simple.

A star is a balance. Gravity pulls all that gas inward. The heat of fusion pushes outward. The star settles at whatever size makes those two things equal.

A heavy star squeezes its own middle very hard. Hard squeezing makes high temperature. High temperature makes fusion go quickly.

A light star squeezes gently. The middle is cooler. And fusion, which is exceptionally fussy about temperature, slows right down.

So it is not that a small star has been given less fuel and must be careful with it. It is that the small star cannot burn quickly even if it wanted to. The pressure to do so simply is not there.

Halve the mass, and the light output drops by far more than half. That is the whole reason for the dimness.

It is worth knowing what is actually happening in the middle, because it is gentler than it sounds.

Deep inside, hydrogen nuclei are being pressed together. Four of them, by a slow chain of steps, end up as one nucleus of helium.

That helium weighs very slightly less than the four things that made it. Under one percent less.

The missing fraction leaves as energy.

That is all fusion is. A very small amount of weight, turned into light, over and over, in a place under enormous pressure.

In a star like this it happens astonishingly slowly. Imagine holding a handful of the core in your hand. Gram for gram, it would give off less warmth than the compost heap at the bottom of a garden.

It is only enormous because the star is enormous. The process itself is barely simmering.

The letter these stars are filed under has a nice history behind it, and the history is mostly women at desks.

At Harvard, from the eighteen nineties onward, a group of women were employed to examine photographic plates of stellar spectra. Each plate held the smeared-out light of dozens of stars, spread into bands by a prism.

They were called computers, because at the time that was a job description rather than a machine.

One of them, Annie Jump Cannon, worked out the ordering we still use. She arranged the classes by temperature, from the hottest down to the coolest, and the sequence she landed on runs O, B, A, F, G, K, M.

Our Sun is a G. These small orange ones are at the far cool end, at M.

Over her working life she classified the spectra of more than three hundred and fifty thousand stars. By eye. One at a time.

And she sorted the coolest ones by a detail that tells you something lovely about them.

In the spectrum of a hot star you see thin sharp lines. Those come from individual atoms, and nothing more complicated survives there.

In the spectrum of these small cool ones you see broad bands instead. Wide, soft, dark stripes.

Bands mean molecules. Molecules mean actual chemistry — two or more atoms joined together and staying joined.

The strongest of them comes from titanium oxide. Titanium, bound to oxygen, floating in the outer atmosphere of a star, and not being torn apart.

That is how cool the surface is. Cool enough for compounds to hold together. Cannon graded the M stars by how heavy those titanium bands looked, and that grading is still in use.

A star with weather chemistry in it. That is a nice thing to know about.

It is also worth saying that "red dwarf" is not quite honest about the colour.

Everything warm glows, and the hotter it is, the bluer the glow. A stove ring at its lowest setting is dull red. Turn it up and it goes orange. Very hot metal in a forge goes yellow, then white.

Our Sun sits at about five and a half thousand degrees at the surface, which is where white comes out.

Proxima sits at about three thousand. That puts its peak output down in the infrared — below the colours your eye is built for.

So most of what it gives off is warmth rather than light. Of the part you could actually see, the reds and oranges dominate, and the blues barely register.

Held up next to the Sun it would look deeply orange. On its own, with nothing to compare it to, it would look like a warm and slightly amber lamp.

And now the detail that makes these stars extraordinary, which is the way they stir.

Our Sun does not mix. Its core burns hydrogen into helium, and the helium stays there, in the middle, piling up. The fresh hydrogen in the Sun's outer layers never gets down to where it could be used.

So the Sun will only ever burn about a tenth of what it has. The rest is out of reach.

A small star does not have that problem.

Below about a third of the Sun's mass, the entire star convects. The whole thing turns over — a slow, deep, rolling circulation, from the core all the way to the surface and back down again.

Hot material rises. Cool material sinks. Nothing settles anywhere.

So the helium does not pile up. It gets carried out. And fresh hydrogen from the surface gets carried down.

The star is not burning a tank of fuel. It is burning itself, all of itself, evenly, from end to end.

Let us go down and look at that surface, because it has a texture.

A convecting surface is not smooth. It is granular, in the way the top of a pan of thick soup is granular just before it simmers.

Rising columns of hot gas arrive from below and spread out at the top. They are bright. Around the edge of each one, the cooled material sinks back down, and those margins are darker.

So the whole surface is a mosaic of soft-edged cells, each one brighter in the middle and dimmer at its rim.

The cells are not fixed. Each one swells, spreads, dims and sinks, and a new one comes up somewhere near it. The pattern is always rearranging and never finishes.

It is exactly what the top of a very slow boil looks like. And it goes on, all over the star, without pause, for trillions of years.

There is also a difference in how the light actually gets out, and it is worth a minute.

In a star like our Sun, energy leaves the core as radiation. A packet of light is absorbed and re-emitted by the dense gas, over and over, in random directions, an unimaginable number of times.

It works its way outward by accident rather than intention. Getting from the middle of the Sun to the surface takes it a very long time indeed — far longer than the whole history of our species.

In a fully mixed star it is not like that.

The energy is carried. It rides upward inside the moving gas itself, like heat travelling in a pot of water rather than through a metal bar.

That is a much more direct way to move warmth. The whole body of the star is doing the carrying, physically, by moving.

The star is not so much shining as circulating, and the shining is what happens at the top of the circulation.

Now put those two things together, and the arithmetic becomes lovely.

It burns very slowly, because it is not squeezed hard. And it can burn nearly everything it has, because it keeps stirring itself.

Slow rate. Full tank.

Our Sun is expected to hold steady for about ten billion years. It has done roughly half of that already.

A star of a tenth of the Sun's mass is expected to hold steady for something like ten trillion years.

That is a thousand times longer. A thousand.

There is no useful way to picture that. Every human being who has ever lived would fit inside a rounding error at the end of it.

Which brings us to the question in the title, and to the pleasant surprise underneath it.

What is the life cycle of a red dwarf star?

Nobody knows. Not because it is difficult to work out, but because it has never once happened.

The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old. The very shortest possible red dwarf lifetime is measured in the hundreds of billions.

So not one of them has finished. Not one has aged. Not one has run out.

Every single red dwarf that has ever formed, anywhere, since the very beginning, is still there. Still going. Still doing exactly what it was doing.

There are no old ones to study. There is no example. In the entire observable universe there is not one instance of this ever having reached an end.

I find that a genuinely restful fact, and I would like to sit with it for a moment.

Astronomers are used to seeing every stage of everything. Stars being born in dust. Stars in middle age. Stars grown huge and red. Stars that have finished and settled into small white embers.

For the big showy stars, the whole sequence is visible somewhere in the sky right now, because they move through it quickly.

For these small ones, there is only the one stage. The beginning of the middle.

The universe has simply not been running long enough to have produced a single old one. It is a very young universe, still, from the point of view of a small orange star.

There is a consequence of this that makes life difficult for astronomers, and it is rather charming.

You cannot tell how old one of these stars is by looking at it.

For most stars you can. As a star ages it moves — its brightness and colour shift in a known way, so its position on the chart tells you roughly how far through it is.

A small star barely moves at all. It arrives at its steady state early and then holds it, essentially unchanged, for the entire life of the universe so far.

So a red dwarf that formed a billion years ago and one that formed twelve billion years ago look nearly the same. Same colour. Same brightness. Same everything you can measure directly.

The one handle anybody has is the spin. These stars turn, and their turning slows very gradually over billions of years, so a slow spinner is probably an old one.

It is a rough tool. It gives you an era, not a date.

Proxima Centauri may well be older than the Earth by a comfortable margin. It does not look it, and it never will.

There are models, of course. People have worked out what should eventually happen, and it is not what happens to the Sun.

The Sun will swell into a red giant, because helium collects in its centre while hydrogen keeps burning in a shell around it. That arrangement puffs the star up.

A fully mixed star cannot do that. There is no separate inert centre and no shell. There is just one well-stirred body, slowly using itself up.

So the models say it never swells. It gets gradually hotter, and slightly smaller, and its colour shifts from orange towards white and then blue.

A blue dwarf, at the end of it. Then a long cooling, into a small white ember.

Not one of these has ever been seen. Not one will be seen for an extremely long time. The wait is so much longer than the present age of everything that the comparison stops meaning anything.

It is a prediction that nobody will ever be in a position to check. It is written down anyway.

There is a floor to all of this, and the floor is interesting.

Go down in mass, and the squeeze in the middle gets gentler, and the temperature drops. Eventually you reach a point where the middle is simply not hot enough for hydrogen fusion to get going at all.

That happens at somewhere around eight percent of the Sun's mass. About eighty times the mass of Jupiter.

Above that line, an object lights, and holds, and keeps holding for trillions of years.

Below it, nothing lights. The object is warm from its own contraction, and it glows a dull infrared. And then it cools, very slowly, for as long as there is time.

Those are called brown dwarfs. They are not failures. They are simply on the other side of a line. And the line is thin — a few percent of a solar mass, either way.

Let us go and look at one that sits almost exactly at that boundary.

It is called TRAPPIST-1, about forty light years from here. It has about nine percent of the Sun's mass, which puts it just barely over the line.

It is the size of Jupiter. Not the mass of Jupiter — the size. A star you could fit inside a planet.

Its surface is around two thousand five hundred degrees, which sounds hot and is, for a star, remarkably cool. Our Sun's surface is more than twice that.

It gives off about six ten-thousandths of the Sun's light.

And it has seven planets. All of them rocky, all of them close in. They go around it in a tight and patient huddle.

Come back closer to home now, to the second-nearest star system, about six light years out.

It is another small red one. It is called Barnard's Star, after the man who noticed something odd about it in nineteen sixteen.

What he noticed was that it moves.

All stars drift across our sky, but the motion is normally far too slow to see in a lifetime. This one shifts about ten arcseconds a year.

That is still tiny. But it adds up. In a hundred and eighty years or so, it crosses a patch of sky as wide as the full Moon.

He found it by comparing photographic plates of the same patch of sky, taken years apart. He was looking for the dot that was in the wrong place.

That is how a great deal of this was done. Glass plates, and a magnifier, and someone willing to compare two nearly identical fields of stars. Night after night, looking for the one thing that had moved.

That work is still going on, though the plates have been replaced.

For most of the last century, the census of our nearby stars had gaps in it. The faint ones kept turning up late, because there was no efficient way to find something that dim.

Then a spacecraft was sent up to do nothing but measure positions. Very precisely, over and over, for years.

By watching how each star appears to shift as the Earth moves around the Sun, it worked out distances for nearly two billion of them.

And it filled the gaps in. Our own neighbourhood turned out to be almost entirely made of these small cool stars, exactly as expected, and now they are catalogued rather than estimated.

There is something satisfying about that. The most common thing in the galaxy was the last thing to get properly counted.

The planets around these small stars sit very close in, and that gives them an unusual arrangement.

The star is dim, so to be gently warmed you have to be near it. Much nearer than we are to the Sun.

And when a planet is that near, tides slow its spin down until it stops turning relative to its star.

So one face looks at the star forever, and the other face looks away forever. There is no sunrise. There is no sunset. Nothing moves in the sky at all.

Between the two sides there is a ring. It is a permanent band of low orange light, running all the way around the world. In that band the star sits forever just on the horizon.

An unending dusk, in a fixed place, going nowhere.

And the years there are short.

The nearest planet to Proxima goes around it in a little over eleven days.

Around TRAPPIST-1, the whole set is quicker still. The innermost takes about a day and a half. The outermost, about nineteen days.

All seven of those orbits would fit comfortably inside the orbit of Mercury, with room to spare.

It is a small, close, slow-lit household. Everything is near everything else. Nothing takes very long to come around again.

If you stood on the day side of one of these worlds and looked up, you would see something quite unlike our sky.

The star would be large. Two or three times wider across than our Sun looks from here, because you are so much closer to it.

But it would be dim. A great soft orange disc, sitting in one place, giving out a light more like a late afternoon in autumn than a noon.

You could look at it directly. It would not glare. It would just be there, in the same part of the sky it was in yesterday. And the day before. And a million years before that.

The light on the ground would be warm-coloured and low, and it would never change, and no shadow would ever move.

There is one more thought to leave you with, and it is a long one, so let it be long.

The bright stars do not last. The big ones burn out quickly, in a few million years. Stars like our Sun manage ten billion or so.

Over an enormous stretch of time, those all finish, one by one, and stop being replaced.

And then what is left is these. The small orange ones. The patient ones. Quietly burning through their well-stirred hydrogen at a rate that would put a candle to shame.

For trillions of years after that, the universe is a dim, warm, orange-lit, extremely quiet place. Not empty. Just calm.

And here is the part I like. That period is by far the longest chapter there is. Everything that has happened so far — every star, every galaxy, all of it — is the brief bright beginning of it.

We are in the noisy opening minutes. The long quiet part has barely started.

So let us come back to Proxima, and simply be still with it for a moment.

It sits there. Orange, small, steady. Turning itself over slowly from the inside, in no particular hurry, the way it has been for billions of years.

Nothing about it will look different tomorrow. Nothing about it will look different in a thousand years.

If someone comes to look at it a million years from now, it will be doing exactly this. Same colour. Same size. Same unhurried simmer.

It is not waiting for anything. It is not building towards anything. It is simply carrying on, and it will carry on long after every other kind of star has finished.

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the numbers. Not the trillions of years. Not the small orange disc sitting fixed on someone else's horizon.

It is all going on anyway, whether anyone is following it tonight or not. It was going on long before this evening. It will be going on long afterwards, at exactly the same gentle rate.
