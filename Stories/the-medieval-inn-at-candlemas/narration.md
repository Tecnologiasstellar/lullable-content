Good evening.

Set the day down now. It has been a long road, and you are at the end of it. Whatever business you rode out on this morning is concluded, or it will keep. Either way, it is no longer yours to carry tonight. Let the bed take you in, the way a good house takes in a traveler. No questions, warm welcome, all night.

Let your shoulders come down. Let your jaw unclench. Let your breathing slow to the pace of someone who has already arrived. Because you have.

Tonight we are riding back six and a half centuries, to the second of February, in the England of the fourteen hundreds' grandparents. The feast they called Candlemas. The exact middle of winter's back half, when the whole country lit candles against the dark, and every wayside inn kept its fire high for travelers. We are going to be one of those travelers. We will arrive at dusk, and eat by firelight, and sleep under a deep roof while snow considers falling. Nothing else happens in this story. That is its entire plot, and its entire point.

If the old world blurs as you drift, let it blur. The fire will keep burning whether you watch it or not. So take one slow breath of cold evening air. Let it out slow and warm, the way breath shows in February. And here is the road, and here is the horse beneath you, and there ahead, at last, is the inn.

§

The road has been mud since noon, and the light is nearly spent, and that is exactly when an inn looks its best.

It stands where the road bends, a long low building of timber and whitewashed clay, under a roof of thick thatch going grey at the edges like a well-liked hat. Smoke stands up from the louvre in the roof, straight as a plumb line in the still air. Above the door hangs the ale-stake, a long pole with a bundle of green branches bound at its end. That bush is the whole sign. No letters, for few on this road can read. Green bush over the door means ale within, beds within, fire within. Every traveler in England knows it, the way birds know berries.

Your horse knows it too. His ears have gone forward, and his pace has found one last freshness. Horses can smell a stable through falling dusk at half a mile. Under the arch you go, out of the wind, into the yard, and the mud of the road gives way to clean laid cobbles, and the evening changes key.

An ostler comes out of the lantern light, wiping his hands, and takes the bridle with the ease of ten thousand bridles. The horse is led away to a stall deep in straw, to hay and warmed water and a rubdown with twists of dry grass. He has earned his own inn tonight, and he has got it. You will hear him, later, through the wall. The slow grind of hay, the occasional stamp. One of the great sleeping-sounds of the old world.

Stamp the road off your boots, lift the latch, and duck under the lintel, into the light.

§

The hall is one long room, and it holds the evening the way a cupped hand holds a coal.

Down the middle, on a raised hearth of packed clay, the fire burns generous and low, and all the room's light leans out from it. There are no chimneys in a house like this. The smoke rises into the dark of the roof and finds its way out through the louvre, and it has cured the beams above to the color of old honey. The floor is strewn with rushes, fresh this week for the feast, and walking on them releases a green hay smell that mixes with woodsmoke and bread and wet wool. That mixture is what the fourteenth century smelled like, on its better evenings.

Trestle tables run along the walls, and benches, polished to a shine by generations of travelers' weariness. Tallow candles burn in iron prickets, and their small flames repeat in every pewter cup down the board. The innkeeper's wife looks up from the fire and reads you in one glance. Wet, tired, harmless, hungry. She nods you to the bench nearest the hearth, which is the only paperwork this house requires. A boy brings a cup of ale before you have asked. In an inn in winter, arrival is its own language, and everyone here speaks it.
Today was Candlemas, and the hall still carries the feast about it, like warmth carried in from church.

Forty days after Christmas, the second of February. The day the whole village walked to church in procession, every soul carrying a candle, and the priest blessed the year's whole store of them. Farmwife and franklin, carter and child, each with a small flame cupped against the wind, a river of little lights moving through the grey noon. The blessed candles went home to every house, to be lit through the rest of the year whenever comfort was wanted. Against storms. Beside sickbeds. Through long nights. A shelf of kept light, laid in like firewood.

Two of those candles burn on the mantel beam of this very hall tonight, honey-colored beeswax, sweeter smelling than the tallow, saved for the feast. Candlemas is winter's hinge. The old rhyme says if Candlemas be fair and bright, winter will have another flight, but if it be cloud and rain, winter is gone and will not come again. Today was grey as goose feather, and the old men by the fire are well pleased. Grey Candlemas, early spring. The worst is behind. The light is coming back, a cock's stride a day. You can feel it in the hall, that particular Candlemas contentment. The year has turned. Everyone in this room has wintered another winter.

§

Supper arrives without ceremony, which is the best way supper can arrive.

A thick pottage, first. Barley and peas and winter roots, simmered since morning in the great pot that never quite leaves the fire, with a ham bone somewhere in its depths doing quiet good work. It comes in a wooden bowl with a wooden spoon, and it is hot all the way down. Then a loaf of maslin bread, wheat and rye together, dense and brown, with a crust that cracks like thin ice and a crumb that steams. Butter from the crock. A wedge of hard cheese with a name nobody spells the same way twice. And ale, brewed in the yard by the innkeeper's wife, dark and mild and faintly of oats. Kings this century eat off gold and are more poorly fed.

Around the hall, a dozen travelers eat the same supper in the same firelight. A wool merchant going down to the staple, doing sums in his head and losing his place in them, pleasantly. A carter whose load of hides waits in the yard under sailcloth. Two pilgrims bound for a far shrine, walking it slowly, in no hurry to arrive since the walking is the point. A clerk with ink-stained fingers, asleep already where he sits, upright, dignified, gone.

The talk is low and unimportant, and it is the finest talk there is. The state of the roads. The price of wool. Whether the grey sky means snow. Nobody here knows anybody's name, and everybody is entirely welcome. That is the small miracle an inn performs every night of the world. Strangers, warm, fed, and easy, all facing one fire.

§

When the hall begins to yawn, the innkeeper does the thing that gave bedtime its oldest name.

He banks the great fire for the night. Rakes the embers together, and sets over them a broad clay cover, a curfew, from the French couvre-feu, cover-fire. Every town in the land rings a bell at this hour for exactly this purpose, and the word for cover-fire became the word for the hour itself. Curfew. Under its clay dome the fire will doze till morning, safe and slow, and the whole house may sleep easy above it. The candles are pinched, one by one, down the hall. Each small light becomes a red star, then a thread of smoke, then nothing. Last of all, the two blessed beeswax candles on the beam, saved out, carried carefully up the stairs ahead of the guests, lighting the way to bed like two small good deeds.
While the hall settles, spare a thought for the road itself, because the road is why any of this exists.

This inn stands a day's ride from the last market town, and a day's ride from the next. That is no accident. All across the country, inns have grown up at exactly the distance a laden horse can comfortably travel between sunrise and dusk. The whole kingdom is beaded with them, like knots in a rope, each one a day apart. Wherever evening catches a traveler, there is a green bush over a door within reach of it.

Some of the men by the fire tonight have walked this road their whole lives. They know it in all its moods. Foul in February, firm in May, dusty at harvest, and every mile of it marked by things with names. The leaning oak. The ford. The gallows hill that has no gallows, only the name and a fine view. Roads in this age are not lines on maps. They are strings of stories, told one landmark at a time.

And every story told on them ends the same way, the good way. And so I came at last to the inn. Every road story in the world ends at an inn, or wishes it did. Yours has.

§

From the village, muffled by distance and falling snow, comes the last sound of the medieval day. The church bell, ringing the hour of rest.

Eight slow strokes, each one rolling across the fields and arriving here a little softer than it left. That bell has ordered this valley's days for two hundred years. It rang for matins this morning and for the Candlemas procession at noon. Now it rings everyone to their beds, and the valley obeys it gratefully.

In the cottages, fires are being covered. In the byres, cattle are knelt down in the straw. In the mill, the great wheel stands still in its dark water. The whole landscape is performing the same small ceremony as this inn, house by house, light by light. By the ninth stroke there is not a working soul in the valley. By the last, even the bell-ringer is climbing down his tower stair, yawning, his own day rung to its close.

Six centuries from now, people will wonder how anyone slept in such a world. The truth is the old world was very good at night. It had no engines, no wires, no glow on the horizon. When it lay down, it lay all the way down. Darkness came whole, and quiet came whole, and sleep came whole along with them. You are borrowing that wholeness tonight, and it is old enough and deep enough to share.

§

The sleeping chamber runs the length of the upper floor, tucked under the great slope of the thatch.

The beds are broad and deep. A mattress of clean straw beneath, a featherbed above it for those who have paid the extra penny, and tonight, being Candlemas, the innkeeper's wife has allowed everyone the featherbed. Linen sheets, coarse but clean and smelling of the frost they dried in. Woolen blankets, and over all a coverlet heavy enough to be an argument for staying put. Travelers in this century think nothing of sharing a chamber with strangers, and there is a comfort in it modern nights have lost. The room breathes together. A cough here, a settling there, the clerk already producing a small scholarly snore. Sleep, in the old world, was company.

§

The thatch above you is an arm's length thick, and it is the best roof ever devised for listening to weather. Rain lands on it and simply disappears, hushed to nothing before it reaches your ear. Wind combs over it and comes out the other side as a long soft sigh. And snow, should it come tonight, will make no sound at all, only a deepening quiet, as if the house were slowly pulling on a white nightcap.

Through the floorboards rises the last warmth of the covered fire, and with it the sounds of a house finishing its day. The innkeeper walking his final round, trying the door bar. The dog turning three times by the hearth and dropping with a grunt. Through the wall, the horses grinding their hay in the dark, patient as millstones. Every sound is a task ending. The whole building is being folded up for the night like a garment, seam by seam.

§

People in this age sleep differently than the centuries to come, and their way is worth borrowing tonight.

They speak of first sleep and second sleep, as naturally as they speak of dinner and supper. The first sleep begins soon after dark and runs some hours. Then, in the middle of the night, it is common to surface for a while, quietly, without alarm. To lie warm in the dark, to murmur with a bedfellow, to think slow thoughts, perhaps to say a prayer by the banked light of a blessed candle. And then to slide, easily, into the second sleep, which carries everyone to morning.

No one in this chamber fears waking in the night. It is not a failure here. It is simply the watch between sleeps, an old room in the house of the night, furnished and ordinary. Should you find yourself there some hour from now, remember the inn. Turn over as the pilgrims do, unbothered, and let the second sleep come for you in its own time. It always comes. It has been coming for people, night after night, for as long as there have been beds.

§

Outside, at last, in perfect silence, it has begun to snow.

Big, slow, deliberate flakes, the kind that fall as if lowered on threads. They land on the thatch, on the yard cobbles, on the cart's sailcloth, on the ale-bush over the door, and they lie where they land. The road you rode in on is going white, and smooth, and quiet, its ruts and its mud all pardoned till morning. No one will travel it tonight. Everyone who was on it is somewhere like this now, under a roof, behind a fire, done.

The candle at the head of the stair is pinched at last, and the darkness that follows is not empty. It is full. Full of wool and feathers and breathing and banked warmth and six centuries of travelers all sleeping this same good sleep. The inn holds you as it has held them all. The snow holds the inn. The night holds the snow. And underneath everything, mild as embers, the year has turned, and the light is coming back, a cock's stride a day.

Let the old world go dark now, gently. Keep nothing of it but the warmth.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
