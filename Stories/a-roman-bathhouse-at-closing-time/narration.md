Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is over now. It is done with. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing find its own slower pace. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are going somewhere warm. Somewhere very warm, in fact, and very old.

We are going to a Roman bathhouse, in a small provincial town, on an ordinary evening about eighteen centuries ago. And we are going at the best possible hour. The last one. The hour when the crowds have gone home, and the attendants are closing up. The hour when the whole warm building is settling down for the night.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

It is dusk, and you are walking down a narrow street of flat paving stones.

The town is finishing its day. Shutters are going up over shop fronts. Somewhere a dog considers barking, and decides against it. The smell of the evening is woodsmoke, and bread, and warm dust cooling down.

Ahead of you there is a long building of brick and stone, with small high windows. It is not grand from the street. Most of these places were not. The grandness was kept for the inside.

But you can tell what it is before you reach the door, because you can feel it.

Warmth. The whole building sheds a faint warmth into the street, the way a loaf does when it is carried past you. The stones of the pavement outside are dry when everything else is going damp with evening.

A little steam drifts from a vent near the roofline, and unravels, and disappears.

This is the bathhouse. Go in.

§

The entrance hall is high and dim, and it is nearly empty.

The last few bathers are leaving as you arrive. Their hair is damp and combed. Their skin has that polished, pink, well-scrubbed look. They are moving slowly, the way people move when they are warm all the way through. They call good nights to the doorkeeper, unhurried even in their leaving.

An attendant pads past with an armful of folded linens. Another is going around the walls with a taper, lighting the evening lamps. Each lamp is a small clay boat of olive oil with a burning wick. Each one, as it catches, adds a small amber circle to the dusk.

The doorkeeper nods you through. There is no hurry here at this hour. Closing, in a place like this, takes a long and gentle while.

Listen for a moment before we go further. Under everything, there is the sound of water. Running water — a soft, continuous, unbothered sound, coming from several directions at once.

Remember that sound. We will come back to what it means.

§

The first room is the changing room, and it is worth a look around.

The walls are lined with rows of niches, like a dovecote for clothes. All empty now. During the crowded afternoon, every niche held a folded tunic and a pair of sandals, and the room rang with talk.

Now it holds only lamplight and the smell of the day just gone. Warm stone. Olive oil. A faint clean edge of scented balm — someone here could afford the good ones.

The floor is a mosaic. Small black and white tiles, laid in patterns of waves and dolphins. They are worn glossy in a broad path, by generations of bare feet. All of them walked the same route, toward the warm rooms.

Follow that worn path. It knows the way better than anyone.

§

While we walk, let me tell you how the day here worked. The rhythm of it was one of the pleasantest things any city has ever arranged.

The baths opened in the late morning, but the real hour was mid-afternoon. When the working day ended — and here it ended early — the whole town came. That was the custom, and it held for centuries.

And it truly was the whole town. The entry fee was small change, deliberately. A magistrate and a dockworker sweated in the same hot room, and swam in the same cold pool. There were very few places in that world where such a thing was true.

The afternoon crowd came for the ritual, and the ritual barely varied. First the exercise yard — a ball game, a little wrestling, enough to raise a sweat. Then oil, rubbed over the skin.

No soap. The Romans did not use soap here. Oil was the whole method, and it worked, and we will see how in a moment.

Then the warm rooms, in their proper order. Then the cold plunge. Then, scrubbed and glowing, came the best hours of the day. Talking, strolling, doing business, doing nothing. People stayed far longer than the bath itself required.

The bath was never really about being clean. Being clean was the excuse. The bath was about the hours around it.

§

Before the warm rooms, look out through this doorway, into the open court.

This is the exercise yard. A square of raked sand, open to the sky, wrapped on all four sides by a covered walk with columns.

This afternoon it was the loudest place in town. Ball games in every corner. Wrestlers, and skipping, and weights made of stone with iron handles. Old men walking laps under the colonnade, putting the world in order.

And around the edges, the sellers. Sausage men, and pastry men, and a boy with a tray of honey cakes, all crying their goods over the noise. The smell of the frying reached the street.

Now the sand lies raked and empty, holding the last grey light. The colonnade is a rectangle of quiet shadows. Somewhere under it, one abandoned ball rests against the base of a column, perfectly still, waiting for tomorrow.

A swallow crosses the open square, high up, and is gone.

§

We know exactly what this place sounded like in full swing, because one famous Roman wrote it all down.

The philosopher Seneca once took lodgings directly above a bathhouse. He wrote a letter to a friend about it, and the letter survives. It is essentially a list of noises, composed by a man trying to read.

He lists the grunting of the weight-lifters, and their loud dramatic breathing. The slap of the masseur's hands, flat and cupped, each with its own note. The splash of the swimmer who insists on diving in.

The man who loves the sound of his own voice in the echo of the pool. The one who sings in the bath — because everyone, he notes, sings in the bath. The sudden roar when a ball game reaches a disputed point.

And under all of it, the cries of the sellers. The sausage man, the pastry man, the drink man, each with his own call, each call repeated all afternoon.

He meant it as a complaint. It has aged into something else. It is the sound of an afternoon eighteen centuries gone, preserved perfectly. Every noise in it is the noise of people enjoying themselves.

The room you are standing in made all of those sounds today. It will make all of them again tomorrow.

§

One small strange thing about that daily rhythm. The hours themselves were not like ours.

The Romans divided daylight into twelve hours. Always twelve, no matter the season. So in summer, when daylight is long, each hour stretched. In winter, when the days shrink, the hours shrank with them.

An hour was not a fixed thing. It was a share of the day.

Nobody carried a watch. The town read its time from sundials, and from the light, and from habit. And the bath hour — the eighth — simply drifted through the year with the sun, arriving earlier in winter, later in summer, always at the same comfortable point of the afternoon's decline.

There is something restful in that arrangement. Time was not a grid the day was stretched over. It was cut to fit the day, whatever the day happened to be.

The bathhouse kept the only schedule that mattered here. Open with the late morning. Warmest through the afternoon. Closing at lamplight. It ran on light and warmth, and so did everyone in it.

§

The oil deserves its own moment, because the tool that went with it is a small masterpiece.

It is called a strigil. A blade of bronze, curved like a crescent moon, blunt as a spoon. Not for cutting. For scraping.

The method was this. You oiled your skin. Then you warmed yourself thoroughly, until the sweat ran and the pores opened. By then the oil had taken up the whole day's dust.

And then, slowly, you scraped. Long, patient strokes, down the arms, down the legs. The blunt curve gathered the oil and the sweat and the grime together, and carried it all away. The skin beneath was left soft, and clean, and faintly gleaming.

Doing your own back was impossible, of course. So you brought a friend, and did each other's, and talked the whole time. Even the cleaning here was social.

The scrapings went into a little pot. The strigils were rinsed and hung in sets, on rings, like keys.

There is a set hanging by the door of this room now, drying, done for the day.

§

Now the warm rooms, in their order. The order was the whole art of the place.

The first is the tepidarium. The gentle room. It is merely warm — the warmth of a kitchen where baking has been going on all afternoon. Its job was preparation. You sat here, and oiled here. You let the body come up to temperature slowly, the way a sensible cook warms a bowl before the dough goes in.

Beyond it is the caldarium. The hot room. There is a large basin of hot water at one end. The air is thick and soft with steam. The heat wraps around you like a blanket. This is where the sweat ran, and the strigils did their work, and the talk grew slow and easy.

And then, back at the far end, the frigidarium. The cold room, with its deep cold plunge pool. One gasping dive after all that heat. The skin tightening, the breath catching, and then out, tingling everywhere, laughing, more awake than you have been all day.

Heat, and then cold. Effort, and then rest. The Romans did not invent that rhythm, but nobody has ever built it in marble more thoroughly.

And the rhythm was at its very best in winter.

Picture this building in January. Outside, the town is grey. Rain on the paving stones, or a rind of old snow. Everyone's house is smoky, and draughty, and dim, because most houses then were.

And in the middle of it all stands this — the one building in town that is warm to the bones. Warm floors, warm walls, hot water, bright with lamps, loud with company.

On a winter afternoon, walking through that door was one of the finest small moments a person's day could hold. The cold peeled away in the first room. The damp cloak went in a niche. An hour later you were sitting in gentle steam, listening to the rain on the high windows, in no hurry whatsoever.

The bathhouse in winter was not a luxury. It was the warm heart of the town. Everyone from the magistrate to the dockworker kept their hands stretched toward the same fire, one floor down.

At this hour, all three rooms stand empty. The steam in the caldarium is thinning. The cold pool is a black mirror, perfectly still, holding one lamp upside down in its surface.

§

Stand in the middle of the warm room now, and notice something with your feet.

The floor is warm.

Not warm where the sun fell on it. Warm everywhere. Evenly, deeply, generously warm, like a stone that has spent all day beside a fire. Through your sandals it is a comfort. On bare skin it would almost be too much — in the hottest room, bathers wore wooden clogs to spare their soles.

There is no fire in this room. There is no fire in any of these rooms. There is no smoke, no hearth, no brazier.

The fire is underneath you.

§

Here is the secret of the whole building, and it is a lovely one.

The floor you are standing on is not resting on the ground. It is a raised floor. It stands on hundreds of little pillars of stacked brick, each about knee height. They are arranged in patient rows, like the trunks of a small square orchard.

Beneath the floor, between those pillars, is a low hidden space. And through that space, all day long, flows the heat of a furnace.

Hot air, and the breath of the fire, drifting slowly between the brick trunks. It washes the underside of the floor. It finds its way across the whole building. The floor above it is layers of tile and thick concrete. It drinks the heat, and holds it, and lets it up gently into the rooms.

The Romans called the arrangement a hypocaust. The fire below. It is underfloor heating, complete and fully worked out, two thousand years before anyone reinvented it.

An entire building, warmed from beneath, by a fire nobody sees.

§

The fire itself lives at the back of the building, in a low workroom, and we can look in on it.

This is the furnace room. It is not marble. It is brick and soot and firelight, and it smells of woodsmoke and hot iron.

Here is the mouth of the furnace — an arched opening, glowing a deep orange at this hour. Beside it, cords of firewood, stacked with real care, enough for days. A long iron rake leans against the wall.

Two stokers are finishing their shift. They have fed this fire since before dawn. Now, at closing, they are not putting it out — a bath fire was almost never put out. They are banking it. They rake the coals together into a compact glowing bed. They lay on a few dense logs. The fire is settled down to burn low and slow through the night.

The building must not go cold. Cold stone takes days to warm again, and the fuel that costs is enormous. So the fire is kept like a sourdough — never allowed to die, only rested.

All night it will breathe warmth under the floors, quietly, with nobody watching.

§

The heat does one more trick before it leaves, and this one is in the walls.

Set into the walls of the warm rooms are columns of hollow box-shaped tiles. They stand stacked one on another inside the plaster. Together they make flues, running from the floor cavity all the way up to the roofline.

The warm air under the floor rises through them. So the walls themselves are warm. The heat climbs up inside them, sheet after sheet of it. The rooms are wrapped in warmth on five sides, floor and walls together.

It is why nothing here feels like standing near a heater. There is no near and far, no hot side and cold side. The warmth is simply the condition of the room, even and calm, coming from everywhere at once.

And the smoke has been kept sealed away from the bathers this whole time. It slips up those same flues and out at the roof. It is that thin unraveling thread you saw from the street.

§

Now, that sound of running water. It has been with us in every room, and it is time to explain it.

The water here never stops. It is not drawn from a well, bucket by bucket. It arrives on its own, continuously, from hills many miles away. It has been arriving like that every hour of every day, for longer than anyone here has been alive.

It comes by aqueduct. A channel of stone and mortar leaves a spring in the high country. It falls, very, very gently, all the way to this town. The fall is the entire engine. A few fingers' width of drop for every hundred paces of distance. No pump anywhere. Gravity, patiently arranged.

When the channel reaches town, it fills a tank. The tank feeds pipes. The pipes feed fountains, and houses, and, above all, this building.

And because the water cannot be turned off — it simply keeps coming, whether wanted or not — the overflow is put to work. It runs continuously through the drains and the latrines, rinsing the whole system clean, day and night.

That is the sound you keep hearing. A supply with no tap and no end. It runs quietly through the building's veins, on its way from the hills to the sea.

§

Everything you have seen tonight is the small country version. In the capital, the same idea was built at a scale that is hard to hold in the mind.

The great baths of Rome were the size of a town district. The largest could hold more than fifteen hundred bathers at once, and thousands upon thousands passed through in a day.

Their halls rose like canyons. Their cold rooms alone could have swallowed this whole building. Behind the scenes, mile after mile of hidden corridors let the staff and the firewood move unseen. A row of cisterns the size of houses held the aqueduct's water in reserve.

And in the hot rooms, the tall openings facing the afternoon sun were glazed. Sheets of glass, cloudy and green-tinged and precious. They let the sunlight pour in and add its heat to the fire's. And they kept the steam inside.

A window built to farm the sun. In the second century.

But the marvel, truly, is not the size. It is the ordinariness. An afternoon of heated marble, hot water, cold water, exercise, company and light — and it was as ordinary as bread. It was simply part of what a town was.

§

And in one corner of the empire, far to the north, there was a bathhouse where the furnace had help.

In the west of Britain, in a green valley, hot water rises out of the ground of its own accord. It falls as rain on the hills, sinks deep, deep down into the rock, and finds heat far below. Then it spends thousands of years working its way back up.

Where it finally surfaces, it comes up steaming. Hundreds of thousands of gallons a day, hot from the earth itself, in a country where the sky is usually grey.

The Romans arrived, and found the local people already treating the spring as sacred, and could hardly believe their luck. They built one of the great bathhouses of the north right over it. They built a temple beside it. And they named the town for the waters.

The spring asked nothing of any stoker. The earth was the furnace.

And here is the quiet ending to that story. The spring has never stopped. The same water still rises there today, at the same gentle heat, through the base of the same Roman stonework. The empire went home fifteen centuries ago. The bath is still being drawn.

§

Running a building like this was a craft in itself, and it was run entirely by feel.

There were no dials anywhere. No gauges, no thermometers. The temperature of three rooms, and the mood of a furnace, all had to be read by the body.

The bath master learned his building the way you learn a person. He knew that the hot room came up slowly on damp mornings. He knew which log wood burned steady and which burned eager. He knew, walking barefoot across the warm floor, from the feel of it through his soles, whether the fire below wanted feeding.

Bathers were his instruments too. If the hot room ran too hot, he heard about it at once, loudly, from everyone. If it ran cool, the complaints were quieter but longer.

So the fires were trimmed all day, armful by armful, by men reading stone with their feet and steam with their faces.

Two thousand years before the thermostat, the temperature was held steady all afternoon, every afternoon. The instrument was attention.

§

It is worth saying how we know all this, because the knowing is its own gentle story.

Buildings like this one were too solid to vanish. When their towns faded, the bathhouses stood, and silted up slowly, and fields grew over them. They waited underground, sometimes with their floors intact. Sometimes the little brick pillars still stand in their rows under a fallen floor. Holding up nothing, patiently, for centuries.

Archaeologists find them all across the old empire. A farmer's plough clips a mosaic. A trench for a pipe finds a curved wall. And then the slow uncovering begins, with brushes and trowels and years of patience.

They find the furnace mouths, with the last firing's ash still in them. They find the strigils, and the oil flasks, and the hairpins, and the single sandals.

And on the walls, sometimes, they find writing. Scratched names. A line of a poem. Someone's opinion of someone else's singing. The same idle, friendly graffiti people have always left in the places where they were comfortable and had time.

Nothing about a bathhouse suggests fear or grandeur when it is dug up. Everything about it suggests an ordinary good afternoon. That is rare in archaeology, and the people who do the digging tend to love these buildings for it.

§

The attendants are finishing now. Watch them work for a moment. Closing time in a bathhouse has its own domestic music.

One moves along the warm rooms with a mop of rags on a pole, gathering the day's splashes from the mosaics. The floor dries behind him almost at once. It is warm, after all.

Another gathers the forgotten things. A comb, a hairpin, one sandal. Always one sandal. They go into a basket, for the shelf by the door.

The linens are counted and stacked. The oil jars are stoppered. The lamps are checked, and most are pinched out, but not all. A few will burn all night, at the corners of the pools. A dark room with water in it is nobody's friend.

The strigils hang in their rows. The doorkeeper's stool is put up on a ledge.

Nobody hurries, because nothing here needs hurry. The building does most of its own housekeeping. The water rinses. The floor dries. The fire keeps.

§

One person stays.

Every bathhouse kept a night man. His duties are light. Walk the rooms once or twice. Mind the lamps by the pools. Feed the banked fire a log toward morning, if it sinks too low.

He has done this for years, and he has the evening down to a ritual of his own. A stool by the tepidarium door, where the warmth is kindest. A little bread and cheese from a cloth. A cup of watered wine.

He sits where the lamplight and the water sounds overlap, in the warmest job in town, and he listens to the building.

He can read it by ear, the way a sailor reads a ship. The fire is drawing well tonight. The cistern is full and quietly overflowing, exactly as it should. A roof timber eases itself, high in the dark, and he knows that sound too, and lets it pass.

By the second lamp-trimming he will be nodding. Nobody minds. Half his job is simply to be here, and a sleeping watchman in a warm room is still a watchman.

§

And now the day staff are gone, and the bathhouse is closed. This is the hour that belongs to the building itself.

Stand in the doorway of the warm room one last time and feel it around you.

The floors will stay warm all night. The banked fire sees to that. It breathes its slow heat through the dark space below, between the little brick pillars. No one watches it. It has no need of anyone.

The walls hold their warmth like a kettle holds its last heat, giving it up by inches until morning.

The water runs on. It does not know the difference between open and closed. It slides through the pipes, and over the stone lips of the basins. The sound of it fills the empty rooms more gently than any voice.

Steam gathers over the hot pool, drifts, thins, and gathers again.

In eight hours the stokers will come back and wake the fire. The doorkeeper will take down his stool. The whole warm day will begin again, exactly as it did yesterday. Exactly as it will tomorrow, for another few centuries.

§

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the fire resting under the floor, or the water running from the hills, or the warm walls breathing upward in the dark. Not the strigils drying on their rings.

It is all going on anyway, whether anyone is there to feel it or not. It was going on before this evening, and it will be going on afterwards. It is tended by people who know exactly what they are doing, and are in no hurry at all.

Let the words go. Let the picture soften, and steam over, and drift apart.

There is nothing out there that needs you tonight. There is nothing here that does either.

Only a warm old building in the dark, holding its heat until morning.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
