Good evening.

The day has wound itself down, all the way to here. Whatever it asked of you, it has stopped asking. The lists are closed. The lights are low. Let the bed take your whole weight, and notice that it can.

Let your eyes rest. Let your hands lie open on the blanket, warm and idle, off duty. Let your breath find the slow rhythm it keeps when no one is asking it questions.

Tonight we are going to read the oldest clock face there is. The night sky. Long before gears, long before bells, people told the hour, the month, and the season by looking up. The sky is full of hands and dials and pendulums, all still running, all still keeping perfect time over your roof at this very moment. We will find them one by one, slowly, lying back in a dark meadow with all night to spend.

None of it needs memorizing. The stars have been keeping these appointments for longer than there have been eyes, and they will keep them whether we watch or drowse. Drowsing is allowed. Drowsing is encouraged. Take one slow breath of cool night air. Let it go, longer than it came. And lie back on the warm grass of the dark, and let the sky open above you, rim to rim.

§

Begin with the great wheel, because everything else rides on it.

Watch any bright star near the horizon for a while, patiently, and you will catch the sky moving. The whole dome of stars is turning, all together, one vast wheel of lights, so slowly that only patience can see it. Stars rise in the east, arc over, and set in the west, exactly as the sun does, and for the same reason. It is not the sky that turns. It is us, rolling eastward inside it, carried on the turning Earth like children asleep in the back seat.

The wheel has a hub. Almost due north, at a height that matches how far north you stand, there is one modest star that barely moves at all. The North Star. Polaris. Not the brightest star, whatever people say. Just the stillest. It happens to hang almost exactly over the Earth's axis, so the whole sky pivots around it, night after night, like a dance around a quiet guest.

And near that hub hangs the sky's own clock hand. The seven bright stars of the Plough, the Big Dipper, swing around the North Star once a day, like the hand of a great dial. Shepherds and sailors read the hour from it for thousands of years. Dipper low, evening young. Dipper high, midnight past. It is up there now, somewhere on its round, telling the time to nobody, beautifully. You never need to set it, and it has never once run down.

The moon keeps the sky's calendar, and it is the friendliest timepiece ever hung in a window.

It tells its time by changing shape. A thin silver eyelash low in the west, and the month is new. A perfect half, like a coin cut by a careful jeweler, and the month is a week old. The full round lamp, rising gold at sunset, and the month is at its middle. Then the slow ebb, night by night, back to darkness, and the month is done.

Twenty-nine days and a half, crest to crest, steady as tide. The oldest tally marks humans ever carved, on bones older than every city, count in twenty-nines. Someone was keeping moon-time before anyone kept anything else. Month itself is just the word moon, worn smooth by saying.

And the moon's timekeeping reaches down and moves the sea. Every tide that lifted every harbor today was the moon's doing, its gravity combing slowly through the oceans as the Earth turns beneath it. High water comes about fifty minutes later each day, because the moon has moved on a little, and the sea follows it around the world like a devoted dog. Somewhere right now a harbor is filling in the dark, obedient to a clock a quarter of a million miles away. Nothing about that has ever needed supervision.
Among the fixed stars, a few bright ones refuse to keep still, and they are clock hands too. The wanderers. The planets.

You can tell a planet from a star at a glance, once you know the trick. Stars twinkle. Planets burn steady. A star is a point so distant that our restless air makes it shiver. A planet is near enough to be a tiny disk, and it rides the turbulence calmly, like a broad boat on chop.

Each wanderer keeps its own pace across the star fields. Brilliant white Venus never strays far from the sun, showing up as the evening star for a season, then slipping around to become the morning star, on a stately cycle her watchers have known for five thousand years. Red Mars takes about two years to make his loop of the sky. Bright Jupiter strolls through one constellation per year, a kind of celestial year-hand, taking twelve years to walk the whole circle. And pale gold Saturn, slowest of the naked-eye family, spends nearly thirty years on one lap. A person could mark the decades of a whole life by three returns of Saturn, and some quietly do.

The old sky-watchers of Babylon logged the wanderers on clay tablets, night after night, for centuries. Their patience became the first astronomy, and their seven sky-lights, sun, moon, and the five wanderers, gave us something we still keep. The seven days of the week. Saturday is Saturn's day, still, after all this time. When you say the days of the week, you are reciting the visible sky of Babylon, in order, like a small prayer nobody remembers is one.

The stars even tell their ages, if you look at their colors, and that makes the sky a clock of lifetimes too.

Starlight comes in shades, easy to see once someone points them out. Steely blue-white stars are the young hotheads, burning fast and bright and briefly, as stars count briefly. Golden stars, like our own sun, are the settled middle of the family, steady providers, halfway through very long careers. And the deep orange and red stars are the elders. Vast, cool, gentle giants, swollen soft with age, glowing like coals at the end of a good fire.

Look at the winter sky and you can see a whole family album at once. The blue youngsters of the hunter's belt. The golden steady ones. And above them the old red shoulder-star, an elder statesman, calm and enormous, further along in time than everything around it. Nothing in that album hurries to the next page. A star's middle age lasts billions of years, and the elders take their leave over spans no calendar bothers to name. The sky is not only telling tonight's hour. It is telling deep time, all of it at once, in colors, quietly. And every shade of it says the same thing to a person lying in a dark meadow. There is time. There is so much more time than you ever needed to worry about.

The seasons have their own dial, and it is drawn in constellations.

Because the Earth drifts around the sun, the night side of the world faces a slightly different shelf of stars each week. The sky slides westward, four minutes a night, an hour every fortnight, until the whole star scenery has changed with the season.

Winter has its hunter, striding up the frosty east with his three-star belt, trailed by the brightest star in all the sky, glittering like ice given a voice. Spring has its lion, and the great curve of the Plough's handle arcing down to a golden star the color of late honey. Summer has its high bright triangle, and the soft river of the Milky Way running through it, shore to shore. Autumn has its flying horse, a great quiet square of four stars, like a window left open on the cooling year.

People have always read the farm year from this dial. When the seven little stars of the Pleiades rose at dusk, sailors of the old Mediterranean knew the sailing season was closing. When the hunter sank into the west, ploughing time was near. No almanac, no calendar on the wall. Just the same slow furniture, rearranging itself on schedule, forever. The star that told your great-great-grandmother when to plant is the same star, to the atom, that is crossing your roof tonight. Very few family heirlooms are kept in such good repair.

§

And beneath all the quick dials, the sky keeps one clock so slow that whole civilizations are single ticks of it.

The Earth, as it spins, wobbles. Very slightly, very grandly, like a spinning top leaning its way around a slow circle. One full wobble takes about twenty-six thousand years. And as the axis leans, the hub of the sky migrates. The North Star is only the north star for now.

When the pyramid builders looked north, their pole star was a different star altogether, a dim steady lamp called Thuban, and they aligned their greatest stones to it. In twelve thousand years, the brilliant blue star Vega will hold the post, and it will be the finest north star this planet has ever had. Polaris is simply this era's appointee. A caretaker hub, holding the position, twenty-six millennia a lap.

There is something deeply restful in a clock that slow. Nothing you will ever do, or fail to do, registers on it. It was mid-tick when the first cities rose. It will be mid-tick when your great-grandchildren's town has changed its name twice. The sky does not do deadlines. It does eras, gently.

So here is the whole nursery of clocks, all running above your bed at once, and not one of them ticking.

The great wheel, once a day, carrying every star over your roof. The Dipper, swinging its silent hour hand around the pole. The moon, counting its twenty-nine nights, towing the patient tides. The seasons, sliding their scenery four minutes a night. And under them all, the twenty-six-thousand-year wobble, the grandfather clock of the world, mid-tick, unhurried, sound.

Every one of them is running right now, in the dark above the ceiling. None of them needs winding. None of them needs watching. None of them has ever once been late. You could not stop them if you tried, and you could not be forgotten by them if you slept for a hundred years. The universe keeps its own time, and it keeps yours too, free of charge, all night, every night.

Let the sky hold the schedule now. It was always holding it. Let your own small clocks, the heart, the breath, slow to their night pace, unsupervised. Let the words drift west with the stars, four minutes at a time, and keep nothing.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
