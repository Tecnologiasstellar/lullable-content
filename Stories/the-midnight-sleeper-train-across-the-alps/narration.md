Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is over now. It is done with. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing find its own slower pace. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are taking a train. A night train, the old kind, with beds in it. It leaves a city in Austria in the late evening. It arrives in Switzerland after breakfast. In between, it crosses the Alps in the dark.

You will be asleep for most of it. That is not a problem. That is the entire design.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

It is a little after nine in the evening, and you are walking down a platform in Vienna.

The station is winding down. The commuter crowds went home hours ago. The shops in the concourse have their shutters half lowered. Someone is pushing a slow cleaning machine across the tiles, leaving a damp shining path behind it.

Out here on the platform the air is cool. It smells faintly of warm brakes and coffee.

And along the platform edge, waiting, is the train.

It is long, and dark blue, and quiet. The carriages have a deep midnight colour, and their windows are mostly curtained already, glowing a soft amber from inside.

Down the platform, small groups of travellers walk slowly with their bags, reading the numbers on the carriages. Nobody hurries. There is something about a night train that makes people walk more softly. It is the way people move in a house where someone is already asleep.

You find your carriage. The steps up are lit by one small lamp.

§

At the top of the steps, in the doorway, there is an attendant.

They have a list, and your name is on it. They welcome you aboard in two languages, glance at your ticket, and tell you where your compartment is. Third door along.

This person is one of the quiet facts of the night, so notice them for a moment.

The attendant rides the whole route. While you sleep, they stay awake. They sit at the end of the corridor, in a small compartment of their own. It holds a kettle, a folding seat, and a window on the night.

They know where the train is at every moment. They know which stations come when. And they have one particular job we will come back to, because it is my favourite piece of the whole arrangement.

Step inside now. The corridor is narrow, and carpeted. It runs along one side of the carriage. Windows on the platform side, doors on the other.

§

Your compartment is small, and it is perfect.

The bed is already made. A proper bed. A firm narrow mattress, and white sheets folded down. A duvet, and a pillow plumped by somebody who does this every night.

There is a reading lamp on a hinged arm. There is a little shelf, and a hook for your coat. There is a narrow wardrobe, just deep enough for one hanger to live a full life.

In the corner there is a small washbasin with a folding cover. Beside it, a cake of soap the size of a domino, and a fresh towel.

And on the pillow there is a small welcome bag. Inside are a bottle of water, and earplugs you will not need, and a little chocolate.

The window is wide, and the blind is up for now. Outside, the platform lamp, the last travellers, the cleaning machine finishing its patrol.

Take your shoes off. Hang up your coat. Sit on the bed, and feel the train do that thing that parked trains do. The faint, almost imperceptible tremble of a machine that is alive, but resting.

§

While the last doors thump shut down the corridor, here is where all this began.

The idea of a proper bed on a train is about a hundred and fifty years old. It was carried to Europe by a young Belgian engineer who had travelled in America. He had slept on a train there. And he had come home unable to stop thinking about it.

He founded a company to build sleeping cars, and to run them across borders, which nobody had really done. Its name was a whole sentence of French, so everyone shortened it to Wagons-Lits. The beds-on-wheels company.

Its carriages were painted a deep midnight blue, with gold lettering. For a century, that blue meant one thing all across Europe. It meant that somewhere in this train there are made beds, and clean sheets, and a person paid to guard your sleep.

The great routes ran everywhere. Paris to the Riviera. Calais to Rome. The famous one, east to Istanbul.

Your train tonight is a descendant of all that. The company changed, and the routes changed, and the carriages have been rebuilt a dozen times. But the colour survives. The train standing at this platform is still midnight blue. Some traditions know their own worth.

§

Departure, when it comes, is nothing at all.

There is no whistle. There is no lurch. There is a soft chime somewhere down the corridor, and doors closing, one after another, each with the same padded thump.

And then the platform is moving. Or rather you are, so smoothly that for a second it is genuinely hard to say which.

The station slides away. The city begins — signal lights, the backs of apartment buildings, lit kitchen windows, a tram waiting at a crossing with its one bright eye.

The train threads out through the yards, gathering speed gently, and the rhythm settles in underneath you. A low steady hum, and beneath it, a soft rolling sway.

Vienna thins. The lights spread out, then scatter. They give way to long stretches of dark. A farmhouse light sits here and there, like sparks from a fire that is settling for the night.

You are away.

§

Now, that promised favourite fact. The attendant knocks, gently, and asks for your ticket. And then they ask for something else.

Your passport.

You hand it over, and they keep it. All night. It goes into a folder in their little compartment at the end of the corridor, along with everyone else's.

Here is why. Somewhere in the small hours, this train crosses a border. In the old days, that meant officials coming aboard at two in the morning, knocking on every door, waking every passenger.

The attendants ended that. Now, if anyone needs to see documents in the night, the attendant simply hands over the folder. The officials look through the passports in the corridor, under the dim lights, while the owners of those passports sleep on, unknowing, in their bunks.

In the morning your passport comes back with your breakfast.

It is such a small arrangement. Somebody thought carefully about your sleep, decades ago. They built a system so that a national border could pass over you like weather while you dreamt. The whole night train is made of thinking like that.

§

Before settling in properly, pad down the corridor for a few minutes. Two carriages along there is a bistro car, and it is open late.

It is a small warm room with a handful of tables, lit low. The windows are black mirrors now, holding the reflection of the lamps and, faintly, the country pouring past behind it.

A steward stands behind a narrow counter, polishing glasses that are already clean. They work in the unhurried way of somebody whose shift simply lasts until it ends.

Order a herbal tea, or a hot chocolate. It arrives in real china, on a real saucer, with a small biscuit you did not ask for.

Two tables away, a pair of travellers talk quietly in a language you do not know, laughing softly at intervals, in no rush to finish. Someone else reads, one hand around a glass of red wine, swaying gently with the carriage.

The cup in front of you trembles in its saucer, very slightly, a fine ring of ripples crossing the surface of the tea. The train is telling you its speed through the china.

Drink it slowly. Nothing after this requires you.

Then carry the warmth of it back down the swaying corridor to your compartment, past the numbered doors, to the bed.

§

Turn off the main light now. Leave the little reading lamp on, for the moment. Lie back on the bed, on top of the duvet, and just listen.

The sound of a modern train is worth attending to, mostly because of what is missing.

The old sound — the one from the films, the clickety-clack — is nearly gone. And the reason is a lovely piece of engineering.

Rails used to come in short lengths, bolted end to end. A small gap was left at every joint, so the steel could expand on hot days. Every time a wheel crossed a gap, it dropped a fraction of a millimetre and struck the next rail. That strike was the click. Sixty feet later, another. Click-click. Click-click. All night, all the way.

Then the railways learned to weld. The short rails were joined into continuous ribbons — a mile long, several miles long, seamless. And the expansion problem was solved another way. The rail is stretched as it is laid, tensioned like a guitar string. It is set to the length it wants to be on an average day. Then it is anchored to the sleepers so firmly that it simply cannot move. The steel still wants to expand and contract. It is just never allowed to.

So the joints are gone, and the click went with them.

What is left is what you are hearing now. A deep, smooth, continuous hush, like a stone rolled along velvet. And underneath you, the gentle sway.

§

The sway has its own explanation, and it is a kind one.

The carriage does not sit directly on its wheels. It rests on two bogies. Small four-wheeled trolleys, one near each end. Between the carriage and the bogies there is a bed of springs and dampers.

The wheels feel every small thing the track does. The springs take all of it, and they hand upward only the largest, slowest movements. A long curve becomes a slow lean. A change of gradient becomes a gentle lift, or an easing down.

The carriage, in other words, is a cradle. It is suspended, and it rocks — not randomly, but slowly, within small limits, around and around a centre.

Your body knows this motion. Being held, and gently rocked, by something large that is going somewhere and does not need your help. It is one of the oldest feelings there is, and the train produces it, mile after mile, as a simple by-product of good suspension.

There is nothing you need to do with that. Just notice it, and let it carry on.

§

Where are we now? West of Vienna, running along the valley of the Danube in the dark.

If you lifted the blind you would see the hills of the wine country rolling past, black on deep blue. Now and then, the lights of a small town lie strung along the river. Linz will come, and go, some time before midnight. You may be asleep for it. The train does not mind either way.

The stops in the night are brief and soft. The train eases in, stands a few minutes with a faint ticking as somewhere a door opens for a boarding passenger, and eases out again.

From your bunk, a station at night is a slow drift of platform lamps across the ceiling. A voice or two, muffled, unhurried. Then darkness again, and the hush resuming.

There is a particular pleasure in half-hearing a place you will never see. Some small Austrian town, asleep, its name on a blue sign you did not quite read, already miles behind.

§

Here is a quiet thing the railways gave the world, and it suits this hour of the night.

Before trains, every town kept its own time. Noon was simply when the sun stood highest over your own church tower. Vienna's noon came a few minutes before Innsbruck's, because Innsbruck lies further west. Nobody minded. No journey was fast enough for minutes to matter.

Then the trains came, and the minutes suddenly mattered very much. A timetable cannot work if every station along the line believes something different about what time it is.

So the railways did something quietly enormous. They declared one time. Railway time, kept by the railway's own clocks, the same along the whole line, whatever the local sun was doing.

The towns grumbled, and then adjusted, and then forgot they had ever done anything else. The time zones the whole world lives in now grew out of that. Out of timetables. Out of the need for a train and a station to agree.

So the hour on your phone tonight, here in the dark, is a great-grandchild of the railways. The train is not keeping your time. You are keeping its.

§

Somewhere in the deepest part of the night, the train does something remarkable, and almost nobody ever sees it.

It comes apart.

A long night train is often several trains travelling together. The front carriages are going to one city, the middle ones to another, the rear ones to a third. They run together through the first part of the night, sharing a locomotive, like travellers sharing a road.

And then, at a junction station at two or three in the morning, they separate. Shunting staff in high-visibility jackets walk the dark platform. Couplings are undone. A locomotive backs quietly onto one portion, and draws it away down a different line, toward a different country.

The carriages part like guests leaving a dinner, each toward its own home. Inside them, hundreds of people sleep on, and their beds are sorted. This bed to Zurich. That bed to Milan. Like letters at a sorting office, without a single sleeper being woken.

If you surface at the right moment you may feel it. A soft distant clink. A pause that goes on a little long. A very gentle tug, as though the night had shifted its grip on the train. That is all.

§

By now the mountains have begun, though you would know it only by inference.

The train is climbing. The sound has changed very slightly — the locomotive working a little, far ahead, a low patient note. The curves come more often, long and slow, leaning the carriage one way, holding it, easing it back.

This line into the mountains is an old one. It was pushed through in the eighteen-eighties. The last gap in Austria's railways was the Arlberg. A high massif, standing between the Tyrol and the lands at the western end of the country.

Thousands of workers spent years on it. They laid the line up the valleys in great sweeping ramps. Where the mountain finally refused, they went through it. A tunnel over ten kilometres long, bored from both ends at once. The two headings met in the middle of the massif, with the accuracy of a handshake.

When it opened, in eighteen eighty-four, the emperor himself rode the first train through. It was the longest tunnel in the country for the following century.

Your train tonight runs on that same line, on those same curves, drawn through the same mountain. The lamps of the valley villages below the line are the great-grandchildren of the lamps the first passengers saw.

§

Lift the blind a little, just for a minute. It is worth it.

The moon is up, somewhere out of view, and the peaks have snow on them, even now. They stand back from the line, pale grey against a sky full of sharp small stars, utterly still.

Below the line, a valley. A river finds the moonlight and gives it back in places, a broken silver thread. A village is gathered around a church, and the church has a clock, lit faintly amber, and every window in the village is dark.

It is perhaps two in the morning down there. Everyone in that village is asleep. The mountains stand around their valley like furniture in a closed house.

And through it all, your train moves along a shelf cut into the mountainside a century and a half ago. It moves like a hand drawn across a map. Steady, quiet, certain of its way.

Let the blind down again. The night outside will go on without supervision.

§

Somewhere before the summit, the train makes one last stop, high up, and it is worth surfacing for.

It is a mountain village station. One platform, one long roof heavy with its winters, one lamp at each end.

The air that slips in when a door opens is different up here. Thin, and clean, and pine-edged, and noticeably colder than the air Vienna gave you.

One passenger boards, unhurried. You hear their case wheel along the corridor, and a door further down, and then nothing.

The train waits longer than it needs to. Trains do this at night. The timetable is built with slack in it, and the slack gathers at the quiet stations, and so the train stands, resting, ticking faintly, ahead of its own schedule and perfectly content.

Outside, the village is a handful of roofs below the line. A fountain runs in the little square, all night, into its wooden trough. It has run all night for centuries. Nobody is there to hear it, and it runs anyway.

Then the softest chime, and the doors, and the platform lamp slides off along the ceiling of your compartment. The climb resumes.

§

Then the tunnel.

You will know it when it comes. The sound gathers itself and comes closer — the hush becomes a fuller, rounder rush, wrapped snugly around the train. The air presses, very gently, for a moment, the way it does in a lift. Your ears notice, and then agree to it.

Inside the mountain there is nothing to see and nothing to pass. No lights slide over the ceiling. The train runs in its own sound, in a straight line, through ten kilometres of solid quiet rock.

There is something deeply restful about a tunnel, if you let there be. For these few minutes the world has no weather, no distance, no scenery, no news. There is only the warm lit carriage, and the bed, and the steady rush, and a mountain overhead holding everything still.

And then the sound opens out. It softens, and widens. You are through, and descending. The rush settles back into the hush. Somewhere below, the western valleys are waiting, with their scattered lamps.

The watershed is behind you. The rivers on this side run to a different sea.

§

While you drowse, spare one thought for the people who are awake.

The driver sits far ahead, in the lit cab. They watch the signals come up out of the dark, one green after another. Each one means the same calm thing. The way is clear. Somewhere in the night, at a division point, one driver hands the train to the next. They exchange a few quiet words on a platform. The train barely notices.

The signallers, in their control rooms, watch the whole railway as small moving lights on great dark screens. They set routes with a click, a hundred miles ahead of you.

The attendant sits at the end of your corridor, with their kettle and their folder of passports. They listen to the carriage the way a shepherd listens to a field.

And out along the line, at maintenance sites in the small hours, there are track crews in lamplight. Tightening, measuring, renewing. The night is when the railway holds still enough to be cared for.

A whole quiet profession of wakefulness, arranged in a chain across the mountains, so that several hundred strangers can sleep at seventy miles an hour.

§

And you are not alone on the line tonight. The night railway has other traffic, and most of it is freight.

The day belongs to passengers. The night belongs to goods. While the cities sleep, the long freight trains come out. Grain, timber, containers, new cars stacked two high. They thread their way across the continent, on the hours nobody else wants.

Once or twice tonight, one will pass you, going the other way.

You will half-hear it from your bunk. First a change in the sound outside, a sudden nearness. Then a long, deep, even rumble, wagon after wagon after wagon, washing past your window a few feet away.

It goes on longer than seems possible. Twenty seconds. Forty. A freight train can be half a mile of iron, and it takes its time.

And then it is gone, all at once, and the night snaps back to stillness, and your own hush resumes as though nothing had happened.

Somewhere aboard it, one driver, alone in a warm cab, is taking six hundred tonnes of timber to Italy through the small hours. They saw your lit train coming for miles. Two crews, passing at speed, each guarding a different kind of cargo. Theirs sleeps standing up.

§

There is one more piece of craft in this carriage worth knowing about, and you are lying on it.

In the daytime, on many routes, this compartment is not a bedroom at all. It is a sitting room, with a bench seat and a view.

The bed you are in folds. The backrest swings up and locks flat to become the upper berth. The seat base pulls out to become the lower. The bedding lives all day in a locker above the door, stacked and strapped, waiting for evening.

Turning a corridor of sitting rooms into a corridor of bedrooms is the attendant's evening work. They move down the carriage, compartment by compartment, and each one takes them only a few minutes. Latches, hinges, sheets, tucks. A room becomes a bed, the way a table becomes a den for a child. Except that here it is done perfectly, two hundred nights a year.

By the time the passengers board, all of it is finished, and the carriage pretends it was always this way.

In the morning, after the breakfasts, the whole trick runs in reverse. The beds fold away, and the sitting rooms return, and the only evidence is a faint warmth in the blankets folded back into their locker.

§

It is worth saying that this train very nearly did not exist.

There was a stretch of years, not long ago, when the night trains of Europe were disappearing. Budget flights had taken their passengers. One by one, the routes were closed. The sleeping cars went to the sidings. It seemed fairly clear that the whole idea was finished.

One railway disagreed. The Austrians kept their night trains when nearly everyone else had given up on them. They bought the sleeping cars the others no longer wanted. They kept the old routes warm, and added the abandoned ones, and named the service after the night itself.

And the idea turned. Slowly, and then not slowly, people remembered that a bed crossing a continent is a rather civilised thing. The routes multiplied again. New carriages were built for the first time in a generation. They have little single cabins now, and better showers. And the same folding washbasins, because some things were right the first time.

So this train is both old and new. The habit of it is a hundred and fifty years old. The carriage you are lying in is younger than some of the shoes in your wardrobe. The night outside is the same night it always was.

§

The train runs on across the high country, and the night settles into its deepest hour.

In the corridor, the lights are dimmed to a soft amber strip. The attendant has poured a tea. Behind each numbered door along the carriage, a stranger sleeps. A student. A grandmother. An engineer with a meeting in the morning. Each sways by the same small amount. Each is carried by the same hand.

In your compartment, the reading lamp can go off now.

The dark that follows is not complete. The edge of the blind glows faintly when the moon finds the right side of the train. The hush continues underneath you. So does the slow lean into curves. And the occasional soft rush of a tunnel, taking you in and letting you go.

Zurich will arrive eventually, with its lake and its bells and its breakfast. But that is hours from now, and it is entirely the train's business, and none of yours.

Your only task tonight was to lie down. It is done.

§

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the welded miles of silver rail, or the passports sleeping in their folder, or the carriages sorted in the dark toward their separate mornings. Not the snow on the quiet peaks.

It is all going on anyway, whether you attend to it or not. The signals are turning green ahead, one after another, all the way to the lake. The mountain has already closed behind you over the tunnel. The train knows the rest.

Let the words go. Let the picture soften and slide away, like a village passing at two in the morning.

There is nothing out there that needs you tonight. There is nothing here that does either.

Only a warm bed, moving gently west, in good hands, in the dark.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
