Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, let the day come to an end now. Whatever kind of day it has been. It is over. It has no more claims on you. Let your shoulders drop, away from your ears. Let the bed take your weight, all of it. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands go soft. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing slow down by itself, without any help from you. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to sit, very quietly, with a boy at a table in Paris. A little less than two hundred years ago. He is working something out with his fingertips. It is patient work, done at night, a few dots at a time. Nobody is hurrying him. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin in a village, at the end of a summer afternoon, in the year 1815 or so.

The village is Coupvray. It sits on a low rise a little more than twenty miles east of Paris. Fields, and vineyards, and a river valley. The houses are stone, with tiled roofs gone soft and brown with age. The light is long and gold. Swallows go back and forth over the lane.

Listen to the village at this hour. A bell, from the church. A cart coming back from the fields, its wheels loud on the stones, then quiet on the earth. Somebody calling a dog in. Beyond the last house the vines run in long rows down the slope. The leaves are warm. The grapes are still hard and green.

One of the houses has a workshop built onto its side. Inside it, the air is warm, and it smells of leather. Not new leather only. Old leather too, and oil, and beeswax, and the dry sweet dust of hay from the stable beyond the wall.

This is a harness maker's shop. The man who works here is called Simon-René Braille. He makes collars and reins and saddles for the horses of the village. Bridles, and traces, and the broad padded collars that let a horse lean into a load. He is good at it. People come from the villages around for his work.

And in the corner, on a low stool, sits his youngest child. A small boy. His name is Louis.

§

Let us stay in the workshop a while, and get to know it by touch.

There is a long bench, scarred and dark, polished by forearms. On it lie the tools. Round knives for cutting hide. A wooden clamp that holds the leather while it is stitched. And awls. Slender steel points set in round wooden handles, for making the holes a needle will pass through.

The work goes like this. A strip of leather is cut, and its edges are beveled smooth. Then the awl goes in, at a slant, hole after hole, in a straight even line. Then the needles follow. Two of them, one from each side, waxed thread pulled tight with a small dry creak. Hole after hole. Stitch after stitch. It is slow. It is meant to be slow.

The boy on the stool cannot see any of this. He lost his sight when he was very small. That is all there is to say about it tonight. What he has instead is the rest of the room. The hiss of the knife. The tap of the mallet. The warm animal smell of the hide, and the cooler smell of the steel.

He learns the tools by their handles. He learns the leather by its grain. He learns the difference between a hole freshly made and a hole that has taken a thread. His fingertips are already doing the work that the rest of his life will ask of them.

§

Evening comes down over the village.

His father has made him something. It is a board. Into it he has hammered rows of round-headed nails. The heads make the shapes of letters. The boy runs his hand across them. An A. A B. The letters of his own name. He learns to read them the way another child would learn to read chalk.

The nail heads are cool at first. Then they warm under his hand. He goes along the rows the way he will one day go along a page. He does not know that yet.

At the village school, he sits with the sighted children and listens. He has a good memory and a quick mind, and the schoolmaster is kind to him. He learns by ear what the others learn by eye. Three years pass this way. He is known in the village as a clever, gentle boy.

But there is a limit to what a board of nails can hold. And there is a school in Paris where blind children are taught to read from books. Letters are written on his behalf. In time an answer comes back. There is a place for him. There is money to pay for it.

He is ten years old.

Picture the road to Paris, in February of 1819.

The coach leaves early, in the cold. The boy sits wrapped against his father, with a bag of clothes at his feet. The road is rutted and long. The wheels find every stone. Outside, the fields are bare and the sky is low and gray.

He cannot see the country going by. But he can hear it change. The lane becomes a road. The road becomes a highway with other wheels on it. Then, slowly, the sound of the city comes up around the coach. Iron rims on paving stones. Voices. Bells. Water being poured out of a doorway. The smell of coal smoke and river mud and bread.

Paris, in 1819, is a city of narrow streets and tall houses. Horses everywhere. Thousands of chimneys. The river Seine running through the middle of it, brown and slow.

The coach stops. His father takes his hand. They walk together to a door in the rue Saint-Victor, not far from the river, on the left bank. And the door opens.

§

This is the Royal Institute for Blind Youth. Let us step inside.

It was founded some thirty years earlier by a man named Valentin Haüy. He was not blind himself. He was a sighted man who had been troubled by how blind people were treated in his city. He had decided that they could be taught to read. He was one of the first people anywhere to think so. His school was the first of its kind in the world.

The building is old. It was a college once, and it has the bones of one. Long corridors. Stone stairs worn into shallow dips. High rooms that hold the cold. In the dormitory, the boys sleep in rows of narrow beds under gray blankets. At night the windows rattle a little when the wind comes off the river.

There is a schedule. Prayers, lessons, meals, work. The boys learn to make slippers and to weave, and to knit, so that they can earn a living. They learn music, because music does not need eyes. And they learn to read.

In the morning the courtyard is full of the sound of feet. Boys crossing it to lessons. They know it by its echoes. The wall on the left gives a sound back quickly. The open side, toward the gate, does not. A blind child learns a courtyard the way a sighted one learns a face.

At night the house has its own sounds. The river is close, and on a still night you can hear it. Bells from the churches nearby, marking the hours. A cart on the cobbles. The creak of beds settling. The smell is of tallow candles, and soup, and old stone.

The boy from Coupvray is quiet at first. Then he begins to listen, the way he listened in his father's shop. He counts his steps from the door to the stairs. From the stairs to his bed. And he begins to learn where everything is.

§

Let us pick up one of the school's books.

It is heavy. Take it in both hands. It is the size of a large atlas. It is heavier than that. The pages are thick paper, almost card. Each page is printed on one side only, and then pasted back to back with its neighbor.

Valentin Haüy had found that if you pressed ordinary letters hard into damp heavy paper, they left a raised shape. When the paper dried, the shape stayed. You could feel it. So he had printed books that way. The letters are large, and loopy, and shaped like handwriting, with little flourishes on the capitals.

The first of these books was made in 1786, in this same school, by the pupils themselves. They set the type by hand. They pressed the damp sheets. They let them dry, and felt the letters come up, and read what they had made. It was the first book ever printed for the fingers. It was slow, and it was a beginning. A beginning is a great deal.

The trouble is that the fingertip is not the eye. A curling capital letter has to be traced all the way around before you know what it is. Then the next one. Then the next. A single line takes a long time. A page takes an age. And there are very few pages, because every book is made by hand. In the boy's first years there, the school's library holds just three of them.

The boy reads them anyway. He reads them all. He is patient, and he is hungry for what is in them. And somewhere, without saying it, a thought begins in him. A letter for the fingers should not be shaped like a letter for the eyes. Not at all.

§

Now something arrives at the school that will change everything, though nobody knows it yet.

The year is 1821. A visitor comes to the Institute. His name is Charles Barbier. He is a former artillery captain, and he has an idea. Some years ago he worked out a way for soldiers to pass messages in the dark, without a light, without speaking. He calls it night writing.

It works with dots. Raised dots, punched into paper, that you read with the finger. A cell of them is two columns wide. Each column can hold up to six dots, one above the other. So a full cell is twelve dots tall and two across.

And the dots do not stand for letters. They stand for sounds. Barbier has drawn up a table of thirty-six sounds of the French language, six rows by six. The number of dots in the left column tells you the row. The number in the right column tells you the place along that row. Count down, count across, and there is your sound.

He has made tools for it too. A ruler with grooves cut into it, to guide the point. A small sharp punch. You lay the paper on the ruler and press. The dot goes down through the paper and comes up on the other side. So you write from right to left, and turn the sheet over, and read from left to right.

The boys are shown it. They try it. And a twelve-year-old boy from Coupvray goes very still.

§

Let us sit with him for a moment and think about what he has noticed.

Dots. Not shapes traced around. Just a small firm point under the skin, there or not there. A fingertip can tell that in an instant. It does not have to search. It does not have to follow a curve. The dots are the right idea.

But twelve of them is too many. A column of six dots is taller than the pad of a fingertip. To read one cell, the finger has to slide up and down. Then it has to move across and slide again. It is slow, and it is easy to lose your place.

And there is the counting. To read one of Barbier's cells you count the dots in one column. Then you count the dots in the other. Then you remember both, and look them up in your head. Three, then five. Count, remember, count again. Counting is slow. Counting is not reading.

And sounds are the wrong idea. Barbier's table has no spelling in it. No punctuation. No numbers. You could write down how a word sounded, roughly, but you could not write the word.

So the boy begins to take it apart. Quietly. At night, when the dormitory is asleep. Over the next few years, between the ages of twelve and fifteen, he works on it in the dark. A slate, a punch, and scraps of paper. Fewer dots. Letters instead of sounds. A cell a fingertip could read at rest.

§

Here is what he arrives at.

Six dots. Two columns of three. Picture them as the six on a die, standing on end. Small enough that the whole cell sits under a single fingertip without moving. You lay the finger on it, and you know it.

Think about what a fingertip does when it rests. It does not scan. It simply feels. It can tell one dot from two, and top from bottom, and left from right, all at once, without moving. That is the whole of the trick. The cell was cut down until it fit the finger. Nobody asked the finger to fit the cell.

Six dots can each be raised or not raised. That gives sixty-four ways to arrange them, if you count the empty cell. Sixty-three ways with at least one dot. That is more than enough for an alphabet. It is enough for punctuation. It is enough for numbers, and for the marks of music.

And there is a deeper thing in it, which is worth holding for a moment. The cell has the same footprint every time. Every letter takes the same space. So a line of them is even, and a finger sliding along it meets one cell after another at a steady beat. Like walking along a fence, post by post. You never have to guess where the next letter begins.

He shows it to the director of the school. He is fifteen years old. The year is 1824.

§

Now let us watch how the dots are actually made, because it is beautiful craft.

There is a slate. It is a frame of metal, or wood, in two leaves joined by a hinge. The paper goes between the leaves. The top leaf has rows of small rectangular windows cut into it, each one the size of a single cell. The bottom leaf, under each window, has six shallow dimples in the pattern of the six dots.

The paper is heavier than ordinary paper. It has to be, or the dots would sink back. When both leaves are closed, the sheet is held firm. The rows of windows lie over it like the panes of a small window. A page of it, when it is finished, has a texture like the skin of an orange. Rough, and even, and full of small news.

A small slate holds only a few lines at a time. When they are full, you open it, and move it down the page, and close it again, and go on. Line by line, down the sheet, the way a plow goes down a field.

And there is a stylus. A short steel point set in a round wooden handle. It fits the hand the way an awl does. The way his father's awls did.

You find a window with your fingertip. You set the point of the stylus into it, and press, gently, into the dimple beneath. The paper gives with a soft tick. That is one dot. You move the point to the next dimple. Tick. That is another. When the cell is done you slide to the next window, and go on.

But the dots are being pushed down. So they rise on the other side. Which means that what you write is a mirror of what you will read. So you write from right to left, along the row, and the letters go in backwards. And when the line is done, you lift the leaf, and turn the paper over. And there it is, raised and right way round, ready for the finger.

The sound of it in a quiet room is very small. Tick. Tick. A pause. Tick. Like a clock that has forgotten to keep time.

§

Let us look at the letters he chose, because there is a pattern in them, and the pattern is calm.

Call the dots by number. One, two, three, down the left column. Four, five, six, down the right.

The first ten letters, A through J, use only the top four dots. One dot alone is A. Two dots stacked in the left column, B. The top two across, C. And so on through ten shapes, none of them reaching the bottom row.

The next ten letters, K through T, are the same ten shapes again, with dot three added. The bottom-left dot. So K is A with a foot. L is B with a foot. The pattern repeats, one step down.

The next letters, U through Z, are the first shapes again, with both bottom dots added. Three and six. The same small family, one more time, standing on two feet.

Numbers borrow the first ten letters. A small sign is set in front of them, to say that what follows is a number and not a word. So A is one, and B is two, and J is zero. Nothing new to learn. Only the same ten shapes, wearing a different hat.

It is a system a child can hold in one hand. And there is one small oddity in it that people still smile about. The letter W is out of place. It does not follow the pattern. That is because French, in the eighteen-twenties, hardly used a W at all. The boy left it out. It was added later, for other languages, and slotted in at the end.

§

Let us wonder, gently, for a moment, where the idea really came from.

Some of it came from Barbier, and the boy said so. The dots were his. The punch and the ruler were his. The trick of writing backwards, so that the page reads forwards, was his.

But look again at the workshop in Coupvray. A harness maker's whole craft is a line of small holes. Evenly spaced. Made one at a time with a steel point. The awl goes in. The awl comes out. The next hole is exactly the same distance along. A good strap is judged by how even that line is. And you can judge it with your eyes shut.

A slate and stylus is that same line of holes. A steel point, a wooden handle, an even row. The boy had held that handle, or one very like it, before he could talk. His hand knew it.

It is a lovely thing to sit with tonight. A father's tool, for making a collar for a horse. In the hands of the son, a tool for making words.

Nobody can say for certain that this is how it happened. It does not need settling. The awl is on the bench in the village. The stylus is in his hand in Paris. And they are the same shape.

§

Some years pass, and the boy becomes a young man, and stays.

At seventeen he is asked to teach at the school. History, and geometry, and algebra. He is good at it. He is patient, and clear, and the younger boys like him. He knows exactly what it is to sit where they sit.

He has a small room of his own now, with a table by the window. On the table there is a slate, and a stack of paper, and a stylus laid across it. In the evenings, when the lessons are done, he sits there and works on the system. A letter is changed. A sign is added. A shape that was hard to feel is thrown out. Nobody asks him to do this. He simply does it.

In 1829, when he is twenty, he writes his system down and has it printed. A slim book. It describes a method for writing words, and music, and plainsong, by means of dots. Some of the early shapes still use small dashes as well as dots. A few years later he thinks better of that. The second edition, in 1837, keeps only the dots. The dashes were hard to make, and harder to feel. So they go.

That is how he works. Nothing is precious. If a thing is slow under the finger, it is changed. The finger is the judge.

§

Let us climb into the organ loft, because he spent a good deal of his life there.

Before the organ, there was the cello. The Institute taught it, and he took to it. Picture the instrument between the knees, the neck against the shoulder. The strings are gut, and they smell faintly of the resin on the bow. You do not need to see a cello to play it. You need to know where the fingers go, and the fingers learn that on their own. He learned it the way he learned everything. By hand, and by ear, and slowly.

He has a fine ear. From 1834 he is the organist at a church in Paris called Saint-Nicolas-des-Champs. Later, at another, Saint-Vincent-de-Paul. He plays for services, week after week, for years.

Picture the loft. It is high up at the back of the church, cold in winter. The great pipes stand behind the bench like a forest of pewter. Somewhere a boy works the bellows. The air fills. The organist sets his hands on the keys, and feels for the stops by their knobs. And the sound comes out into the stone.

The church smells of wax, and cold stone, and faintly of incense from the morning. Below, people sit, and stand, and kneel. Up here there is only the bench, and the keys, and the air moving through the pipes.

And here is the quiet gift. At nineteen, before the book was even printed, he had worked out how to write music in his cells. The same six dots. A dot pattern for each note. A mark for its length. A mark for the octave. A blind musician could sit with a page under one hand and learn the piece a bar at a time. Then close the page, and play.

Everything he knew about music, he could now write down. And everything anyone else knew, in time, could be written for him.

§

Come down from the loft now, back to the schoolroom, and watch him teach a boy to read.

The younger boy is perhaps eight. He is new. His hands are still shy. The teacher sets a sheet in front of him. He lays the child's right hand on it. Then his own on top, very lightly.

Feel that, he says. That is a single dot. That is A.

The child's finger moves. He finds the next cell. Two dots stacked. Slowly, one letter at a time, he goes along the row. The teacher does not hurry him. When the boy loses his place, the teacher moves his hand back to the edge of the page. They begin the line again.

Outside, in the courtyard, the other boys are at their work. A loom knocks, somewhere. Someone is humming under his breath. The afternoon goes on.

It takes days. Then it takes less. Then one afternoon the child's finger is moving along a line at a steady pace, and he is reading. He does not notice that he is reading, because he is thinking about what the words say.

And then, one day, the slate. The child writes his own name. Right to left, one dot at a time, with the teacher's hand near his. He turns the paper over. He reads it back. It is his name, and he made it himself.

That is the whole thing, really. That is what the six dots were for.

§

The school itself took some years to see what the students already saw.

The boys used the dots among themselves long before they were used in lessons. They wrote notes. They copied out what they were taught. They wrote to one another, and, in time, to their families. A system that a child can learn in a week does not need much help to spread.

In 1854, France made it official. The dots became the way blind students in the country would read and write. And from France, over the decades that followed, the cell went out into the world. To other languages, one after another, each one working out which pattern would stand for which sound. The shapes were kept, wherever possible, so that a reader who learned one could guess at another.

The United States took it up, officially, in 1916, after trying a few systems of its own. Some languages kept his letters just as they were. Others took the same sixty-three shapes and gave them to their own sounds. The cell was the thing that traveled. What it stood for was decided, gently, one country at a time.

The school moved, too, in the eighteen-forties. A new building on the other side of the river, near the Invalides, with a garden. The dots went with it. The school is still there today. It still teaches them.

And the name went with it. The name of a harness maker's son from a village of vineyards. People say it now in dozens of languages, without thinking about it, the way we say the name of a thing.

In English we write it with a small letter now. Not a surname. Just braille, the way we say paper, or ink. That is what happens to a name when the thing it belongs to becomes ordinary. It is a kind of honor. The quiet kind.

§

And the dots are still here, all around you, tonight.

They are on the buttons of elevators. Small clusters beside each number, at the height of a hand. They are on the edges of stair rails in stations. On the doors of rooms. On the keys of some machines at the bank.

In Europe, since a rule made in 2004, they are on the boxes of medicines. The name of the medicine, and its strength, in the same six-dot cell that a boy worked out in a dormitory. So a person can reach into a cupboard in the dark and know what is in their hand.

There are dots on the menu in some restaurants. On the corners of playing cards. On small labels in a kitchen, so that one tin can be told from another. On the tags of clothes in a wardrobe, so that a blue shirt is known to be blue.

There are libraries of it. There are printers that punch it out at speed. There are devices with small pins that rise and fall under the finger, one line at a time, reading a screen. But under all of that, it is the same. Two columns of three. Sixty-three ways. The shape of the six on a die.

Almost every writing system in the world has been carried into it. The letters change. The cell does not.

§

Let us think for a while about the fingertip itself, because it is the other half of the story.

The pad of the finger is one of the most finely feeling places on the body. Under the skin, packed close together, are thousands of tiny nerve endings. Some of them answer to pressure. Some to a light touch. Some to the smallest edge moving across them.

A raised dot on paper is very small. About the height of a grain of sand. But a fingertip moving across it feels a clean bump. Then a gap. Then another. The gaps matter as much as the bumps. The whole system is built on the finger's gift for noticing where something is not.

A practiced reader keeps the touch light. Pressing hard flattens the dots and tells you less. The hand moves steadily from left to right along the line. The other hand often waits at the start of the next, ready to take over. Reading this way is quiet. Only a hand moving over paper, in a room with the light off.

In a cold room the finger warms the paper a little, as it goes. In a warm room the paper is already warm. A good reader hardly notices the paper at all. No more than you notice the page when you are lost in a story. The hand goes along. The words come. That is all.

§

Let us go back to the Institute now, on a night in the eighteen-twenties, and stay there.

It is late. Rain has begun. It falls into the courtyard, steadily, on the flagstones, and runs in a thin sheet from the roof tiles into a gutter. The sound of it comes in through the shutters, soft and even. The city beyond has gone quiet. A cart, once, far off. Then nothing but the rain.

The dormitory is dark. Rows of narrow beds, the shape of each sleeper under a gray blanket, the slow breathing of the other boys. At the end of the room, by the window, one of them is still awake. He sits on the edge of his bed with a slate across his knees and a sheet of paper in it.

There is no candle. He does not need one. He has never needed one. The dark is not dark to him. It is just the room, at night, with the rain outside.

His hand moves. Tick. A pause. Tick. He is trying a shape for a letter. He runs his finger over what he has made. He thinks about it. He tries it again, a little differently. There is no hurry in him at all.

The slate is cool under his hands. The paper is a little soft from the damp air. Down in the courtyard the rain finds the same hollow in the flagstones it always finds, and fills it, and runs on.

And the rest of the house sleeps. The kitchen is cold and swept. The long tables in the refectory are wiped and bare. In the workroom, half-made slippers wait in rows. In the music room, the cello stands in its corner, the strings loosened for the night.

Along the corridor, the heavy books lie closed on their shelves. Their raised letters are patient. They have said all they can say. They are not troubled that something smaller and better is being made a floor above them.

Miles to the east, in Coupvray, the workshop is shut. The bench is dark. The awls hang in a row on the wall. The smell of leather has settled into the walls, where it will stay for two hundred years. Until the house is a small museum, and people come to stand quietly in that room.

Nothing here is in a hurry. It never was.

§

So let it be quiet now.

Let the rain go on falling into the courtyard, whether anyone is listening or not. Let the boy at the window set down his slate at last. Let him lie back on his narrow bed, and pull the blanket up, and let his hands go still. He has done enough for tonight. So have you.

Let the awls hang in their row in the dark workshop. Let the heavy books rest on their shelves. Let the cello lean in its corner. Let the rain go on.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the dots, not the letters, not the dates. It will all keep itself. It has been keeping itself, on elevator buttons and in libraries and under moving fingertips, for a very long time. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let your fingertips rest, wherever they are. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, the way paper settles when it is laid down on a bench. Let the dark be just the room, at night, with the rain outside.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
