# Research sources — Six Dots: The Quiet Work of Louis Braille

- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Braille — Coupvray about twenty miles east of Paris; father Simon-René a leatherer and maker of horse tack; lost his sight at three; admitted to the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in February 1819 at age ten; Haüy books embossed on heavy paper, "uncomfortable sizes and weights", three books; Barbier's code of up to twelve dots in two columns, learned of in 1821; six-dot system presented at fifteen (1824); first published 1829; 1837 second edition dropped the dashes; taught history, geometry and algebra; cellist and organist at Saint-Nicolas-des-Champs and Saint-Vincent-de-Paul (1834–1839); adopted in France 1854; United States 1916.
- https://afb.org/about-afb/history/online-museums/life-and-legacy-louis-braille/coupvray — born 4 January 1809 in Coupvray, 25 miles east of Paris; father's harness workshop attached to the house; harnesses, reins, saddles and collars for the villagers' horses; grandfather took over the local harness maker's business.
- https://afb.org/about-afb/history/online-museums/life-and-legacy-louis-braille/braille-nvents-his-code — Barbier's sonography of twelve dots for the thirty-six sounds of French, introduced at the Institute in 1821; Braille worked on the system between thirteen and sixteen and showed it to Dr Pignier at fifteen; six-dot cell, sixty-four combinations counting the empty cell; worked with paper, slate and stylus.
- https://museelouisbraille.com/en/biographie — nailed-letter alphabet at home; three years at the village school; left for Paris in February 1819 aged ten; tried Barbier's system at twelve; adapted it between twelve and sixteen; extended it to music notation at nineteen; hired to teach at seventeen; played the organ in Paris churches; museum in the family house at Coupvray.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Night_writing — 2 × 6 dot grid; thirty-six phonetic sounds in a six-by-six table read by counting dots in each column; adopted at the Institution Royale des Jeunes Aveugles in 1821; Barbier's ruler with six grooves and a punch; written right to left and read from the other side.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slate_and_stylus — two hinged leaves, windows in the top leaf, dimples in the bottom; the writer works right to left, turns the paper over and reads left to right.
- https://www.brailleauthority.org/use-braille-slate-and-stylus — dots are embossed from the back of the sheet and read as raised dots on the front.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valentin_Ha%C3%BCy — sighted founder of the first school for the blind (1780s); devised embossing raised Latin letters on heavy paper.
- https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Catholic_Encyclopedia_(1913)/Valentin_Ha%C3%BCy — by December 1786 Haüy's pupils had embossed his Essai sur l'éducation des aveugles from movable type, the first book for the blind; letters slightly different from ordinary italic, on heavy paper.
- https://www.camillesourget.com/en/ouvrages/913-hauy-valentin-essai-sur-leducation-des-aveugles-the-first-printed-book-2/ — the 1786 Essai is a quarto (246 × 190 mm), printed on one side only with the blank sides pasted together, in an embossed italic typeface.
- https://digital-archive.perkins.org/Documents/Detail/sample-pages-from-essai-sur-leducation-des-aveugles/12383 — the embossed letters resemble cursive handwriting with ornamented, curled capitals.
- https://nfb.org/images/nfb/publications/bm/bm10/bm1003/bm100310.htm — scholarship at ten in 1819; Haüy's school and its embossed-letter method; embossed printing so slow and costly that very few books existed; the library's three books.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_for_Blind_Youth — housed on the rue Saint-Victor (former Collège des Bons-Enfants) after 1816; moved to 56 Boulevard des Invalides in 1843–44, where it remains; Braille studied there from 1819 and later taught.
- https://www.britannica.com/topic/Braille-writing-system — braille invented 1824; sixty-three characters of one to six dots in a six-position cell; a–j use the upper dots, k–t add dot 3, u–z add dots 3 and 6, w the exception.
- https://www.perkins.org/how-the-braille-alphabet-works/ — the same alphabet pattern; w does not fit because French had no w when Braille built his code; added later for other languages.
- https://sightscotland.org.uk/articles/information-and-advice/who-was-louis-braille — organist at Saint-Nicolas-des-Champs 1834–1839 and later Saint-Vincent-de-Paul; taught history, algebra and geometry; braille not on the Institute's curriculum until 1854.
- https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-01-04/louis-braille-music-200/106176564 — braille music notation built from the same six-dot cell, still in use.
- https://www.euroblind.org/newsletter/2022/march/en/security-and-safety-your-fingertips-braille-medicinal-products — EU Directive 2004/27/EC requires the medicine's name and strength in braille on outer packaging.
- https://www.access-board.gov/ada/guides/chapter-4-elevators-and-platform-lifts/ — braille required beside elevator control buttons in the United States.
- https://afb.org/blindness-and-low-vision/braille/what-braille — braille adapted to many languages worldwide; still read by touch with a light moving hand.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_uniformity_of_braille_alphabets — the same six-dot cell reused across languages, with letter shapes kept where possible.
