Good evening.

Let the day be over now. Whatever it held, it is behind you. Let your shoulders drop. Let them drop a little further than feels necessary. Let your jaw loosen. Let your hands go heavy and open.

Feel the weight of you settling into the bed. Your heels. Your hips. The back of your head. Nothing is holding you up. The bed is doing all of it.

Tonight we are going to a small stone building on the top of a mountain in Scotland. People lived there. Every hour, day and night, one of them went outside and read the weather. They did this for twenty-one years.

If your attention drifts, let it drift. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. Nothing tonight needs remembering.
So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

The mountain is called Ben Nevis. It is the highest ground in Britain, though not by very much. One thousand three hundred and forty-five metres. High enough for the air to thin a little. High enough for the weather to arrive first.

The summit is a wide plateau of broken grey stone. Not a peak. A field of rubble, tilted, with a long drop on the north side and gentler slopes elsewhere.

For most of the year, you cannot see any of it. The summit sits inside cloud. Not fog rolling past. Inside. The cloud simply is where you are standing, moving slowly, wetting everything it touches.

Picture that cloud now. It is not white. Close up it is a soft grey that dims the light without ever going dark.

It beads on wool. It settles on the backs of your hands as the finest possible rain, so fine you feel the cold before you feel the wet.

Sound behaves oddly in it. Footsteps on wet stone go small and close. The wind is somewhere above you, and somewhere below, and rarely where you are.

The stone underfoot is older than almost anything you will think about tonight.

Ben Nevis is the cold root of something that finished a very long time ago. Hundreds of millions of years ago, rock down there was soft with heat. The middle of it sank, slowly, into itself, and cooled, and set.

What you are standing on is the deep part. The mountain above it has been carried away by ice and rain, a grain at a time, for longer than there have been grasses.

Nothing about it is in a hurry. Nothing about it has moved in your lifetime, or your grandmother's, or anyone's.

It is simply there, being extremely still, holding up a small stone building and a pony track and a box of thermometers.

The building is low and long and made of stone. The walls are thick. Thick enough that the cold takes many hours to work through them, and never quite manages it.

There is a door on the leeward side, and a passage, and then rooms. A room for the instruments. A room for the ledgers. Small bedrooms. A kitchen with a stove in it.

Inside, on a winter night, it is warm in the way a ship is warm. Not hot. Warm enough. The lamps are turned low. Paraffin, and the faint smell of it, and a small steady flame behind glass.

There is a stove, and the stove is the centre of everything. Coal comes up the mountain by pony, so nobody is careless with it.

There is a kettle. There is always a kettle. Tea is made at strange hours here, because someone is always awake at strange hours here.

A cook lives in the building with the observers. That is a real detail, not a decoration. Superintendent, two or more assistants, and a cook. Somebody has to feed people who work all night.

Think about the shape of a day in that house.

Meals happen, but not at ordinary times, because the hours are shared out and somebody is always mid-shift. People eat when their turn allows. Someone is usually asleep in the afternoon.

Between readings there is a great deal of time, and it is quiet time. There are books. There is correspondence, carried down by pony and answered slowly. There are games, and long conversations, and the particular companionship of people who are not going anywhere this week.

In midwinter the daylight is thin and brief. The lamps stay lit through most of it.

It sounds lonely written down. By the accounts, it mostly was not. It was a small warm household with an unusual job and a very good view, on the days there was one.

Here is why they are there.

In the early eighteen eighties, a man named Clement Wragge walked to the summit and back, day after day, through a summer, and then through another summer, taking readings as he went. He wanted to show it could be done. He showed it.

Money was raised by public subscription. Ordinary people sent small amounts. A building was put up on the plateau, and in the winter of eighteen eighty-three the observations began.

Robert Traill Omond was the first superintendent. Later, Angus Rankin took the post. Their names sit at the top of long columns of numbers.

Before any of it could begin, a path had to exist.

A track was cut up the side of the mountain, wide enough and shallow enough for a pony to walk carrying weight. It winds. It has to. A straight line up a mountain is no use to anything with hooves.

Then the building came up that track in pieces. Stone. Timber. Glass. Lead. The instruments, wrapped carefully, walking uphill in a basket.

Every wall of that little observatory was carried to the top of Britain by something with four legs and something with two.

Now the hour.

Somewhere in the building a clock reaches the hour, and one person stands up. This happens every hour. Through the night. Through the winter. For twenty-one years.

They put on what the weather requires. Sometimes a coat. Sometimes everything they own. They open the door, and the cloud is waiting, and they step out into it with a lamp.

The reading takes a few minutes. Then they come back in, and write it down, and the next one is one hour away.

What did they read.

The pressure of the air, from a barometer kept indoors and carefully corrected. The temperature, from two thermometers. The wetness of the air. The wind, its strength and its direction. How much rain or snow had fallen. How much of the sky had cloud in it. Whether the sun had shown at all.

Six or seven quantities, once an hour, forever. That is the whole job. It sounds small written down. It was not small.

Cloud was recorded as a fraction. You look up, and you judge how much of the sky has something in it, and you write down a number out of ten. Ten is completely covered. Nought is nothing at all.

On Ben Nevis the answer was very often ten, and very often the observer was standing inside the ten.

And there was room in the book for words as well as figures. The state of the sky. What the weather was doing. Small remarks, in a small hand, at the end of a ruled line.

The two thermometers are worth staying with, because one of them is doing something clever.

The first is ordinary. Dry glass, dry bulb, and it tells you the temperature of the air.

The second has a small sleeve of muslin around its bulb, and the muslin is kept wet. Water evaporates from that wet sleeve. Evaporation takes heat away. So the wet thermometer always reads lower than the dry one.

And here is the elegant part. How much lower depends on how dry the air is.

If the air is already full of moisture, very little can evaporate, and the two thermometers read almost the same. If the air is dry, the evaporation is brisk, the cooling is strong, and the two readings pull apart.

So the gap between the two numbers is a measurement of the air's thirst. No electronics. No moving parts. Just water, cloth, glass, and the patience to read both.

On Ben Nevis, inside the cloud, the two numbers often sat very close together. The air there had no thirst at all.

The muslin has to be kept damp, which means someone has to keep it damp. There is a small reservoir, and a wick, and a bit of attention paid to it at each visit.

In hard frost the sleeve freezes, and a frozen sleeve behaves differently, and the observer has to know that and account for it.

This is the texture of the whole job. Not heroics. Small maintenance, done properly, on the hour, by someone with cold fingers and a lamp.

The barometer stays indoors, in the quiet, on a wall that does not shake.

It is a tall glass tube, closed at the top, standing in a small reservoir of mercury. The weight of the whole atmosphere presses down on that reservoir. The mercury rises in the tube until the two forces agree.

You read the height of the mercury. That is the pressure of the air. A column of metal, held up by the sky.

On the summit, the column stands lower than it does at the coast. There is simply less atmosphere above you up here. Some of it is beneath your feet.

The reading is never quite the number you write down.

Mercury expands when it is warm, so the observer takes the temperature of the barometer itself and corrects for it. Gravity varies a little with where you are standing, so there is a correction for that too.

Every hour, a small piece of arithmetic, done by lamplight, to turn a height in a tube into a fact about the air.

There is something restful in that. The instrument does not lie, but it does need to be understood.

Then the wind, which on that summit is a serious animal.

Direction first, by eye and by vane. Then strength.

The wind on Ben Nevis has been recorded at speeds that make standing an optional activity. It arrives over the plateau edge and lifts, and drops, and turns. It can be still at the door and unreasonable twenty paces away.

The observers wrote it down all the same. A number, an hour, a direction. The mountain shouting, recorded in a neat hand.

Rain is measured in a gauge, which is a cylinder with a funnel and a marked jar inside it.

But on the summit, much of what falls does not stay liquid, and much of it arrives sideways. So snow is collected, and brought inside, and melted, and measured as the water it becomes.

That is the honest way to do it. A drift is not a measurement. Water in a jar is a measurement.

Someone stands in a warm kitchen at two in the morning, waiting for snow to turn back into rain, so that a number can be true.

The sunshine has its own instrument, and it is the loveliest of them.

A solid sphere of glass, mounted in a curved brass cradle. The sphere gathers the sunlight from wherever the sun happens to be, and focuses it to a single hot point.

Beneath it sits a stiff card, marked with the hours.

When the sun is out, the point of light burns a thin brown line along the card. When cloud passes, the burning stops. At the end of the day, you hold up a card with a scorched trace on it, and you can read exactly how long the sun was there.

Sunshine, written by sunshine, in a language of small burns.

Then there is the snow.

Thermometers must be kept in a shaded, ventilated box, so the sun cannot warm them and the rain cannot wet them. A louvred wooden box on legs. It is called a Stevenson screen, and there is one outside the observatory.

The box has to sit a certain height above the ground. Between three and five feet above the surface. That is the rule.

But on Ben Nevis, the surface rises. Snow falls, and settles, and does not leave. So the screens were built to be raised, and lowered, to keep the correct distance from a ground that moved.

Think about that for a moment, gently, without any effort.

The mountain gathers snow all winter. Metre by metre, the world under the instruments comes up to meet them. And every so often somebody goes out and lifts the box a little higher, and measures the gap again, and writes down what they have done.

By late winter the snow can reach the roof. There is a tower on the building, and a hatch, and in the deep months the observers come and go through the tower, stepping out onto a white plain that used to be the sky above their door.

And there is ice here of a particular kind, which is worth seeing.

The cloud on the summit is made of water droplets, and those droplets can be colder than freezing while still staying liquid. They are waiting for something to touch.

When they touch a railing, or a post, or the corner of a wooden screen, they freeze instantly, on contact.

That is rime, and it grows in the strangest direction.

It builds towards the wind. Into it. Feather by feather, each new droplet freezing onto the tip of the last, so that every edge of the building grows a white blade pointing at the weather.

Fence posts become fins. Ropes become thick white bones. The louvres of the screen fur over, and someone has to go out and clear them, gently, before the reading.

In the morning the whole plateau is furred and pale and pointing the same way, like grass under a slow river.

The cold up there is not dramatic. It is only persistent.

The average temperature at the summit sits close to freezing, taken across a whole year. Not deeply cold. Just never reliably warm.

Frost is possible in any month. Snow lies in the shaded hollows late into the summer, grey and grainy and hard, shrinking slowly and rarely disappearing.

That is the quality of the place. It does not have seasons so much as degrees of the same season. The observers dressed for winter most of the year, and were rarely wrong.

Supplies come by pony.

There is a track up the mountain, built for this, and between June and November the pony walks it. Coal. Oil. Flour. Instruments. Post.

In the summer months, visitors come too. The mountain gets busy in its modest way. There is a small hotel near the observatory for a time, and people climb up to see the strange men who live in the clouds and write things down.

Picture the summer visitors arriving, because it is a comic and gentle scene.

They have walked up for hours. They are warm, and pleased with themselves, and out of breath. They arrive in a cloud, having been promised a view.

The observers, who live there, are very used to this. Tea is provided. The instruments are explained, again. Somebody asks whether it is always like this, and is told, kindly, that it usually is.

Then the visitors go back down before dark, and the mountain empties, and the hour comes round again as it always does.

And then autumn comes, and the track closes, and the mountain returns the building to the people inside it.

Not every day is inside a cloud. A few days each year, the summit is clear, and those days are remembered.

The plateau lies open under a very large sky. You can see west to the sea, and the islands beyond it, low and blue. You can see other mountains standing in their own weather, minding their own business.

Below you, a floor of cloud, filling the glens, level as milk in a bowl.

On such a day the observers still go out on the hour. The same six numbers. The same neat hand. The view is not one of the quantities they were asked to record.

And then there are the night hours, which are the heart of it.

Two in the morning. Three. Four. The building is asleep except for one person and one lamp.

They put down the book. They open the door. The cold comes in around their ankles, and out they go into the dark and the wet, with the lamp making a small bright room around them that moves as they move.

The instruments, read by lamplight, one after another. Then back in. The latch. The coat on the hook. The kettle, if it is that kind of night.

Then the numbers, written down carefully, while everyone else in Britain is asleep and the weather quietly continues.

Now, a quiet thing that happened here, which nobody planned.

In the late summer of eighteen ninety-four, a young physicist came up to help for a few weeks. His name was Charles Thomson Rees Wilson.

He was not there long. But while he was there, he saw something the observers saw often. The sun, low and behind him. His own shadow thrown forward onto the cloud. And around the shadow of his head, rings of soft colour.

They are called glories. The sunlight enters the tiny water droplets of the cloud, and turns inside them, and comes back towards the source, and arranges itself into faint coloured rings.

Wilson stood on the summit and looked at them, and wanted, very much, to make one indoors.

So he went back to Cambridge and tried. He built a small glass chamber. He filled it with clean, damp air, and expanded it suddenly, which cools it, and made his own cloud on a table.

And then he noticed something he was not looking for.

The droplets in his little cloud did not form at random. They formed on things. On invisible specks. On charged particles passing through the air.

Which meant that a particle far too small to see would leave, behind it, a thin visible line of water droplets. A track. A path you could photograph.

That chamber became one of the most important instruments in physics, and it began as a man on a wet mountain, admiring some coloured rings.

The glories were not the only light the observers wrote about.

Along with the numbers, the ledgers carry notes in the margins. Haloes around the moon. Rings and arcs made by ice crystals high above them. Mock suns, sitting on either side of the real one. The aurora, when it came, low and green in the north.

None of this was required. The columns did not ask for it.

But when you are outside at four in the morning every night for years, you see things. And it seems they could not help writing them down.

Meanwhile, the hourly readings continued.

From eighteen ninety onward, there was a second observatory down at sea level in Fort William, at the foot of the mountain, taking readings on the same hours.

Two sets of eyes, at two heights, on the same weather, at the same moment. Above and below. It let them see the air as a thing with layers, rather than a thing with a surface.

That pairing did something quietly useful.

Weather does not sit still on top of the ground. It is stacked. Warm below and cold above, usually. But sometimes the other way round, with a lid of warmer air resting over cold air in the glen.

With one observatory at the top and one at the bottom, you can watch the stack change through a night, hour by hour, and see the lid arrive and lift.

Two thermometers, thirteen hundred metres apart vertically, reading at the same moment. Nobody had had that before, in Britain, for years at a time.

Consider what accumulated.

Every hour. Both places. Year after year. Pressure, dry bulb, wet bulb, rain, wind. Written by hand, in ink and pencil, in ruled columns, in bound ledgers, by people in a warm room with a lamp.

More than one and a half million observations.

Nobody involved could have read them all. They were never meant to be read that way. They were meant to be kept.

In time the readings were printed, in large volumes, by the Royal Society of Edinburgh.

Picture those books. Heavy. Cloth-bound. Page after page of ruled columns, hour by hour, month by month, year by year.

There is no narrative in them. No chapter has a surprise in it. You could open one anywhere and find the same calm grid of numbers waiting.

They went onto shelves, and stood there, doing nothing at all, for a very long time.

And they were kept. That is the whole ending, and it is a soft one.

A century later, the ledgers were photographed, and put on the internet, and ordinary people were asked to help type them out. More than three and a half thousand people said yes.

They did it in under three months. Strangers, in the evenings, reading Victorian handwriting off a screen, typing in a number for the wind at two in the morning in eighteen eighty-nine.

Most of the work was done by a few hundred of them. People who found it soothing, and kept going, page after page, long after the novelty had worn off.

Which is a pleasing symmetry, when you think about it gently. The readings were made by a handful of patient people working through the night. They were recovered, a century later, by a handful of patient people working through the evening.

Nobody in either group was in a hurry. That is why it worked both times.

The old columns are now data again. Twenty-one years of weather on a mountain, waiting quietly, until somebody wanted them.

And they turned out to be worth having.

A long, unbroken run of measurements from one fixed place is a rare object. Most records have gaps. People move the instruments, or stop for a war, or lose a decade in a flood.

This one just goes, hour after hour, for twenty-one years, from a spot that has not changed and cannot be built on.

So it became a baseline. Somewhere to measure from. When you want to know what the air over Scotland is doing now, it helps enormously to know exactly what it was doing then, at two in the morning, in eighteen ninety-one.

None of them knew that. They were simply on time.

The building is still up there, in its way.

The walls are low now, and open to the sky, and softened by weather. Stone going back to being stone.

Walkers reach the summit and find them without expecting to. And because the wind is usually doing something, people sit down in the lee of those old walls to eat a sandwich and get their breath.

They are still sheltering people. That was never in the plan, and it is a fine thing for a building to end up doing.

Let us go back inside, one last time.

It is very late. The lamp is turned low. The stove has settled into its steady red. The kettle is quiet.

Someone has just come in from the reading. Their coat is on the hook, dripping slowly. Their hands are around a cup. The numbers for this hour are written down and blotted and closed.

Outside, the cloud goes on moving over the stones, without hurry, the way it did all day, the way it will all night.

The room smells of paraffin and coal and wet wool drying. The clock ticks somewhere behind you. A page turns.

Someone else is asleep down the passage, and will be woken in a few hours, gently, for their turn.

And the mountain holds all of it, the way it has held everything, without effort, without comment.

There is nothing to watch for now. Nothing on the mountain requires you.

The next reading is an hour away, and it is not yours, and someone else will take it. And an hour after that, another. All night, gently, whether you are awake or not.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the numbers. Not the names. Not the muslin, or the pony, or the coloured rings on the cloud.

Let it all go soft. Let it blur at the edges. It was only ever here to be something quiet to hold.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
