Good evening.

The day is done, and you have done enough of it. Whatever is unfinished will still be there tomorrow, patient as furniture. You can set it all down now. Let the mattress take over. Holding you up is its entire career.

Let your forehead smooth out. Let your hands uncurl. Let your breath slow down to the pace it keeps when nobody is measuring it.

Tonight we are going a very long way back. Not to another place. To another time. We are going to drift through the age of the dinosaurs, from its quiet beginning to its quiet inheritance. It is the longest story this planet has to tell, and it is told entirely in stone.

Deep time is restful, once you settle into it. Nothing in this story happens quickly. Continents move at the speed of growing fingernails. Ages last longer than imagination. Every creature you will meet tonight finished its long day millions of years ago, and nothing here asks anything of you.

If the numbers blur, let them blur. If you lose your place among the ages, it does not matter. The ages do not mind. So take one slow breath in. Let it out even more slowly. And let the floor of time open gently beneath the bed, and lower us down.

§

Begin with how we know anything at all.

Everything in tonight's story comes from stone. A bone buried in river sand, slowly exchanged, mineral for mineral, until it is rock in the shape of a bone. A footprint pressed into mud on some ordinary morning, baked by an afternoon sun, filled by a flood, and kept. An egg. A leaf. The faint stipple of skin. The earth has been keeping a diary for four billion years, and it writes in sediment.

Patient people learned to read it. They noticed that the pages lie in order, oldest at the bottom, like laundry no one ever puts away. Read upward, and time passes. Read downward, and it returns.

Tonight we read downward first. Down through the yesterday layers, the ice ages, the grasslands. Down past the first mammals the size of your thumb. Down two hundred and thirty-five million years, to a warm and thirsty world.

And here we step off, into the Triassic.

§

The world has only one continent tonight.

All the land on Earth is gathered into a single vast country, which we now call Pangaea. You could walk, in principle, from pole to pole. The middle of it is far from any sea, and dry as an oven, red rock and dust. But along the coasts and rivers there are ferns, and conifers, and slow green shade.

And in that shade, something new is walking.

The first dinosaurs are not giants. That surprises everyone. They are slender, quick, and light, many no bigger than a goose. They stand upright on two long legs, tails out behind for balance, and they move like dancers. That upright stance is their whole invention. Legs beneath the body, not sprawled out to the sides. It costs less to carry. It goes further on less. It seems a small thing. It will carry them through one hundred and sixty-five million years.

For now, they are rare. Modest neighbors in a crowded world. If you visited the Triassic, you might not notice them at all. The earth notices. The earth has time.

§

Ages pass. The one continent begins, very slowly, to let go of itself.

Pangaea opens along deep seams, the way bread opens in a warm oven. Seas pour into the gaps. Coastlines multiply. Rain reaches lands that were desert. The world turns green almost everywhere, and we have arrived in the Jurassic.

This is the age everyone pictures. Fern meadows under a warm haze. Forests of conifer and ginkgo. Shallow golden seas lying warm across the continents. And through the morning mist, moving with enormous calm, come the largest animals that ever walked.

The sauropods. The long-necks.

Picture one at dawn, at the edge of a fern plain. A body the size of a house, held steady on four legs like living pillars. A neck reaching up, unhurried, into the crown of a tree that no other mouth can touch. The largest of them, found in Argentina, stretched over thirty meters and weighed as much as ten large elephants. A herd of them crossing a plain would sound like distant, patient thunder. Nothing bothers them. Nothing could. They eat, and walk, and grow, and that is the whole of their day.

Cut one of their bones open, all these ages later, and you find rings, like a tree's. Season after season of quiet growth, written from the inside. Some kept growing for decades. As if the body itself had settled into the long rhythm of the age, and saw no reason to stop.

§

Not everything in the Jurassic is enormous. Some of it is simply unhurried.

Here, in the warm afternoon, stands a stegosaur. The size of a van, with a double row of broad flat plates rising along its back, like garden slates set on edge. Scholars have wondered about those plates for a century and a half. Perhaps they warmed the animal, catching the low morning sun the way a greenhouse does. Perhaps they were simply handsome, the way a peacock's tail is handsome. The stegosaur is not telling. It browses the low ferns, hour after hour, plates glowing faintly amber in the afternoon light. An animal built like a rooftop, at peace in the sun.

§

Time moves. The continents drift apart like guests at the end of a long party.

We are in the Cretaceous now, the last and longest age of the three. And the world is softening in a lovely way.

The first flowers are opening. Until now, the world has been green, only green, for two hundred million years. Now come whites and yellows, small at first, along riverbanks and clearings. Magnolias among the conifers. Water lilies on the slow water. And with the flowers come the first bees, working the morning shift, inventing an arrangement that has never gone out of style. The dinosaurs walk through all of it, ankle-deep in blossom, entirely unimpressed.

And on a floodplain in the north, we find the gentlest scene in the whole fossil record.

A nesting ground. Hundreds of round nests, scooped in the earth, spaced a body-length apart, like a well-planned campground. They belong to duck-billed dinosaurs, plant eaters the length of a bus. The nests hold eggs, and then hatchlings, small as rabbits, with legs too weak to wander. And the wonderful thing is this. The bones say the parents stayed. The hatchlings' teeth are worn from eating, though they could not yet leave the nest. Someone was bringing them their supper. Ferns and berries, delivery after delivery, season after season.

When scientists uncovered these nests in Montana, they named the animal Maiasaura. It means good mother lizard. Whole herds returned to the same ground, year upon year, to raise their young together. The age of the giants, it turns out, was also an age of nurseries. The plain at dusk would have murmured with them. Thousands of parents, settling beside thousands of nests. Sleep was invented long before we were.

§

Look closely at some of the smaller dinosaurs of this age, and you find something soft.

Feathers.

Not for flying. Not yet. Just a fine downy covering, for warmth, the way a chick wears fluff. We know because of a place in what is now northeast China. Whole communities of small dinosaurs were preserved there in fine volcanic ash. It was gentle as snowfall in the way it kept them. Around their bones, in the stone, there is a halo of fuzz. Some show simple filaments, some show true feathers, vaned and patterned. Careful studies of those fossils have even recovered hints of their colors. Rusty ginger. Black and white. A dark glossy sheen like a crow's wing.

So adjust the old picture, gently. Many of the small ones were not scaly and gray. They were soft, and banded, and quick, and warm. Something that would fit in your two hands, wearing stripes, dozing on a branch. Remember them. They are about to matter very much.

§

Even the famous king of this age deserves a calmer portrait than he usually gets.

Tyrannosaurus lives at the very end of the story, in the river country of western North America. Twelve meters, nose to tail. A head the size of a bathtub. The storybooks paint him forever roaring. But no animal roars all day.

Here is a truer hour. Early morning on a wide slow river. Mist on the water. The great animal walks down the sandbar, unhurried, leaving footprints that will outlast mountains. He lowers that enormous head and drinks, quietly, the way every animal drinks. Herons the size of gliders lift from the shallows and resettle. He stands there a long while, warming in the first sun, doing nothing at all. Even kings spend most of their lives at rest.

§

The dinosaurs never had the world to themselves, and their neighbors are worth an unhurried visit.

Look up, first. The sky of the Jurassic has skin-winged fliers in it, the pterosaurs, cousins of the dinosaurs though not dinosaurs themselves. Some are small as sparrows, hawking for insects over the lagoons. By the last age, some are the largest fliers there have ever been. Tall as a giraffe when they stand. Wings wider than a small airplane. Yet they launch themselves with a quiet vault and ride the warm air for hours, hardly beating a wing. Shadows the size of sailboats, drifting over the plains. The herds below never bother to look up. The shadows have always been there.

§

And look into the warm seas. Long-necked marine reptiles cruise the green light like slow gondolas. Coiled shells called ammonites hang in the water in their millions, drifting, jet-propelled, patient. When they died, their shells settled into the seafloor like coins into a fountain. Whole cliffs of England are made of that settling. You can stand today on a hillside far from any coast and pick Jurassic seashells out of the stone. The sea keeps its receipts.

§

Some of the loveliest pages in the stone diary are simply footprints.

Bones tell you what an animal was. Footprints tell you what it was doing. And there are places where whole processions are pressed into the rock. Riverbanks in Texas. Mudflats in Bolivia, now tilted up into a wall of stone, printed with thousands of steps.

Read the trackways slowly and they turn into mornings. Here a herd of long-necks crossed wet sand, all walking the same unhurried direction, like commuters who have agreed not to rush. The small prints travel in the middle of the great ones. The young walked surrounded, ringed by the elders, the way elephants still arrange themselves. No print ever shows a panic. Just walking, endless calm walking. One set of tracks simply ends where the animal waded into water and floated free. An ordinary swim on an ordinary day, one hundred and some million years ago, saved like a pressed flower.

§

The age had its own music, and we have found some of the instruments.

Among the duck-billed dinosaurs there is one with a long, hollow crest. It sweeps back from its head, like a trombone worn as a hat. Inside the crest, the breathing passages loop through more than a meter of winding tube. Scientists have mapped those tubes and modeled the sound they would make. A low, round, mellow note, deeper than a tuba, soft as a foghorn heard across water.

So give the Cretaceous evening its sound. Herds calling gently to each other across the floodplain as the light goes. Long low notes, overlapping, each animal's crest tuned a little differently by its size. The young ones piping higher, learning the family music. It would have carried for miles, the way whale song carries. The world has always sung itself to sleep.

§

Some dinosaurs lived where you might think none could, and they teach us the most about patience.

There were herds living inside the polar circles, in what is now Alaska and southern Australia. The poles were far milder then, forested and green, but the light still worked the way it works now. Months of summer sun. Then months of winter dark.

And the herds stayed. Bones of the young show they were born there and wintered there. Through the long night they moved slowly among the dim trees, browsing on bark and evergreen, waiting out the dark together. Big eyes for the starlight. Warm bodies pressed side by side. They had no calendar, but their bones kept one, laying down a bright ring for every returning spring. Whole generations practiced the art you are practicing now. Resting through the dark, unhurried, certain the light comes back.

§

Here is a small strange fact about those nights, worth keeping under the pillow.

The days themselves were shorter then. The Earth spun a little faster in the age of the dinosaurs, and the moon rode a little closer. A day in the late Cretaceous ran about twenty-three and a half hours. Which means the year held around three hundred and seventy-two days, and the extra ones were simply nights. Corals kept the count. Their skeletons grow a fine line each day, and the old corals carry more lines per year than the young ones.

Ever since, the moon has been drifting gently away, a few centimeters a year, about the pace a fingernail grows. And as it drifts, it slows us, and the nights grow longer. Every age sleeps a little longer than the one before. Tonight is, very slightly, the longest night the world has ever had. It seems only right to use all of it.

§

Much of what we know arrived through very patient hands, and they belong in the story too.

A fossil leaves the ground the way it entered. Slowly. Someone walks a dry hillside for weeks, reading the rubble. Someone kneels for a whole summer beside one shoulder blade, lifting the desert away with brushes and dental picks, a spoonful at a time. The bone comes home wrapped in plaster, like a patient, and is unwrapped over months under good lamps.

In the museum halls at night, after the visitors go, the great skeletons stand in the dark in their arrested strides. Guards say the halls feel peaceful then. Not eerie. Peaceful. Every skeleton is the end of two long journeys. Sixty-six million years in the ground, and one careful summer in someone's hands. Patience, meeting patience.

§

And once, in a limestone lagoon in what is now Germany, the two halves of tonight's story lay down in the same stone.

A creature the size of a raven settled onto the soft lagoon floor, and the fine lime mud held it perfectly. Quarry workers split that stone open a century and a half ago. Inside was a skeleton with teeth like a small dinosaur's. Clawed fingers like a small dinosaur's. A long bony tail like a small dinosaur's. And all around the bones, printed in the stone like breath on glass, feathers. Full flight feathers, on true wings.

They named it Archaeopteryx, ancient wing. Half of it belongs to the old age, half to everything that came after. It is the hinge of the whole story, small enough to hold in two hands. The museums keep those slabs like crown jewels, and they are right to. Somewhere in a quiet case tonight, the first wing is resting on its stone pillow, exactly as it has for a hundred and fifty million years.

§

The names of the three great ages are gentle things themselves, once you unfold them.

The Triassic is named for the number three. In the old quarries of Germany, that age lies in three tidy layers, red, white, and brown, like a folded blanket. The Jurassic is named for the Jura, the long soft mountains between France and Switzerland, which are made of it. Whole mountains, built from the floor of that warm sea, lifted up with the shells still in them.

And the Cretaceous is named from creta, the Latin word for chalk. The white cliffs of Dover are that age, straight through. And chalk, looked at closely, is a snowfall. It is made of the shells of sea creatures almost too small to see. They drifted down through the green water, year after year, for thirty million years. A hundred meters of white cliff is simply patience, compressed. The dinosaurs' last age is written in the softest stone there is. Children draw hopscotch squares with it.

§

The giants had quiet inventions inside them, and one of them is still in use tonight.

A long-neck the size of a house needs a great deal of air, and it cannot afford to gulp. So the sauropods breathed the way birds breathe now. Not in and out of a simple bag, the way we do. Instead, a smooth one-way circle. Air flowed through the body like a slow river, fresh the whole way round. Air sacs tucked among their bones made the great necks light, the way a swan's bones are light. It is the calmest breathing ever invented. No effort. No waste.

Every bird outside your window still uses it. And their stomachs worked just as patiently. A sauropod did not chew. It swallowed the forest whole and let time do the cooking. A warm, slow inner vat worked through the night while the animal drowsed. Even their digestion was a kind of sleep.

§

Here is something easy to forget about all three ages. There was no grass.

Not one meadow. Not one lawn. Grass had not been invented yet. It barely arrives before the story's end, and the wide grasslands we know come long after.

So when you picture the herds, clear the picture gently. No prairies. Instead, fern meadows, deep and soft, rolling to the horizon like green surf. Horsetails crowding the riverbanks, the same plants that still grow in wet ditches now, only taller then. Groves of ginkgo, whose fan-shaped leaves turn butter yellow in autumn, exactly as they still do on city streets. Cycads like great pineapples wearing crowns. Conifers up to the clouds.

Some of those plants are still with us, almost unchanged. There is probably a ginkgo within a mile of your bed. The dinosaurs' salad bar never quite closed.

§

Some mornings of that world were caught in ways stone alone cannot manage.

The conifer forests wept resin, gold and slow, down their bark. Whatever the resin touched, it kept. A midge, resting a moment too long. An ant in mid-errand. A raindrop. A tiny down feather, drifting from some small dinosaur preening overhead. The resin folded over them, and hardened, and became amber.

Hold a piece of Cretaceous amber to a lamp and you are looking into honey-colored air a hundred million years old. The insect inside still has its lace wings, its bristles, its little errand it never finished. Museums keep drawers and drawers of these golden mornings. Each one is a few seconds of the age, kept perfectly, like a note slipped inside a bottle. Nothing in them ever hurries again.

§

And the eggs. Stay a while with the eggs.

Every dinosaur that ever lived, from the sparrow-sized to the house-sized, began inside an egg. And the eggs were never large. The biggest known would fit in your two hands, smaller than a rugby ball. A mother the weight of ten elephants began as something you could carry in a jacket pocket.

Inside the shell, the long patience started early. Months in the warm dark, folded, growing, listening to the muffled world. Some nests were warmed by the sun in open sand. Some parents piled ferns over them and let the slow warmth of the composting green do the brooding. Some of the small feathered ones sat their nests directly, wings spread over the clutch, exactly the way a hen sits now. We have found one preserved that way, settled over its eggs, wings out, calm. Whatever else the ages changed, they never improved on sitting quietly in the dark, keeping something warm.

§

All of it ran on sunlight, and the sun of that world deserves one slow look.

It was the same sun. That is the wonderful part. A little dimmer, a little younger, but the same patient star, rising on the fern meadows exactly as it rose on you this morning. It lifted the mists off the Jurassic rivers. It warmed the stegosaur's plates at noon. It set, orange and enormous, behind the nesting plains, while the low crest-notes drifted across the water. Count it, if you like. The age of dinosaurs held something like sixty billion sunsets. Every single one of them ended a day in which, on the whole, very little happened. Ages of ordinary evenings, one after another, world without hurry. Tonight's is simply the next one in the series.

§

Every long afternoon comes, eventually, to its evening.

Sixty-six million years ago, there came an ordinary day with one difference. A stone from the sky came down into a shallow tropical sea, near what is now the coast of Mexico. It was about the size of a city, and it changed the weather of the whole world. Skies dimmed. Winters came out of season. And over the years that followed, the age of the giants drew quietly to its close.

The earth kept a record even of this. In the rock, all over the planet, there is a thin gray line no thicker than a coin. Below it, the age of dinosaurs. Above it, everything after. A hundred and sixty-five million years, closed like a chapter, under one gray line.

Hold the number, not the ending. A hundred and sixty-five million years. We have managed, so far, a small fraction of one percent of that. Nothing that lasted so long should be called anything but a success. The giants had the longest, greenest, warmest afternoon any family of creatures has ever enjoyed. And then the light changed, and the afternoon was over, and the world grew very quiet for a while.

§

But the story does not end at the gray line. It slips through it.

Remember the small warm ones. The feathered ones, the size of your two hands. Some of them, by then, had already learned the air. They were light, and quick, and they could live on seeds and small things the changed world still offered. They came through.

We do not call them dinosaurs anymore, though by every rule of the family tree, that is what they are. We call them birds.

There are more than ten thousand kinds of them alive tonight. Which means, counted fairly, there are more kinds of dinosaur in the world right now than there are kinds of mammal. The age of dinosaurs did not truly end. It shrank, and softened, and learned to sing.

The blackbird on the fence is carrying the oldest inheritance on Earth. The hummingbird at the flower is a sauropod's cousin, at one hundred-millionth the size. It still grows rings in its bones. It still builds nests the way the good mothers did. Tomorrow morning, just before the day properly begins, you may hear the dawn chorus start up outside. That is the sound the Cretaceous makes now. It is a much gentler roar.

§

So let the ages settle, one on top of another, like heavy blankets.

The Triassic at the bottom, warm and red, with its light-footed dancers. The Jurassic over it, green and misty, with its patient walking thunder. The Cretaceous over that, strewn with the first flowers, murmuring with nurseries. And on top, thin as a sheet, our own little while, with its blackbirds and its bedrooms.

Everything in this story has already happened. Every giant found its rest ages ago. Every nest emptied into the sky. The stone remembers it all, and stone is in no hurry, and neither are you.

Let the great herds fade back into the mist. Let the footprints fill slowly with golden sand. Let the gray line rest where it lies. Let the birds sleep in the hedges, heads under wings, keeping the old story warm until morning.

Let the words go now. Keep nothing.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
