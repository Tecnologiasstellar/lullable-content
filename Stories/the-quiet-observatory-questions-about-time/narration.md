Good evening.

The day has run out, all on its own, the way days do. You did not have to finish it. It finished itself. Whatever it left undone belongs to tomorrow now, and tomorrow is not here. Let the bed carry you. It has carried heavier things than tonight.

Let your eyes rest closed. Let your hands lie open. Let your breath slow to the pace of someone with nowhere to be. Which is, for the next several hours, exactly what you are.

Tonight we are going to climb a small hill, to a small observatory, and spend the night with the gentlest big question there is. The question is: what is time. Everyone lives inside it. Nobody has ever seen it. It cannot be bottled or weighed, and yet it can be measured more precisely than anything else humans measure. The people who measure it best have always worked at night, on hills, with telescopes. So that is where we are going.

Do not worry about following any of it. Big questions, at bedtime, are not for answering. They are for holding, loosely, the way you hold a smooth stone in your pocket. If your thoughts wander off the path, let them wander. They know the way back. Take one slow breath in. Let it out even more slowly. And let us start up the hill.

§

The path is grassy, and the evening is mild, and the climb is not steep.

The town falls away behind you, lit windows sinking into the dusk. The air smells of cut grass and coming dew. Ahead, at the top of the hill, stands a small white tower with a rounded dome, like an egg wearing a beret. An observatory. A modest one. The kind a town builds when it decides the sky is worth keeping an eye on.

Observatories are always built on hills, away from the town's light. Astronomers are gentle neighbors, but they do ask one thing of a town, and it is the same thing you are asking of the world right now. Dim the lamps. Let the night be properly dark.

The door is unlocked. Inside, the round room is cool and quiet, and it smells faintly of machine oil and old wood. In the middle, on a stone pier sunk deep into the hill, stands the telescope. Long, and patient, and pointed at nothing yet. A wooden chair waits beside it. A logbook lies open on the desk. Someone has kept records here, night after night, for a very long time.

Overhead, with a soft rumble, the dome rolls open a narrow slice, and the stars pour in.

§

Begin with the simplest clock in the room, which is the room itself.

The observatory is bolted to the hill. The hill is bolted to the Earth. And the Earth is turning. Smoothly, silently, at a pace nothing ever disturbs. You cannot feel it. No one has ever felt it. But look through the telescope at any star and hold still. Slowly, steadily, the star drifts across the eyepiece and slips away. The star is not moving. You are. The whole room is tilting eastward with the planet, carrying you past the sky.

That drift is the original clock. Before there were gears or batteries, there was the turning of the Earth. A day is not a thing that happens to us. A day is what we call one turn of the ride.

And here is a small secret astronomers keep. Measured by the stars, a turn does not take twenty-four hours. It takes twenty-three hours and fifty-six minutes. The missing four minutes come from our slow drift around the sun, which nudges the sunrise a little later against the stars each night. So the stars run ahead of the kitchen clock, four minutes every night. Which is why the constellations of winter are different from the constellations of summer. The sky is a calendar, turning four minutes at a time.

§

Now point the telescope at the moon, low and yellow over the hills, and meet the second strange thing about time.

Light is fast, but it is not instant. It crosses the room in no time worth mentioning. But the sky is not a room.

The moonlight arriving in your eye tonight left the moon a little over one second ago. You are seeing the moon as it was one second in the past. Sunlight is older. It rode through space for about eight minutes before it warmed your face this morning. If you watch a sunset, the sun you see touching the horizon slipped below it eight minutes before.

And the stars. The starlight falling through the dome tonight is older than tonight. From the nearest bright stars, it has traveled for years. From others, for centuries. There are stars in the telescope whose light set out before this observatory was built. Before the town below it. Before the language these words are in.

Look up, and you are not seeing the sky as it is. You are seeing a beautiful patient archive. Every star shining at you from its own moment in the past, all arriving together, now. The night sky is the deepest library there is, and all of it is open after dark.

§

Which raises the softest question of the night. What do we mean by now.

In this room, now is easy. The lamp, the chair, the logbook, all sharing one moment. But stretch now across the sky and it comes gently apart in your hands. Your now includes a one-second-old moon and an eight-minute-old sun and stars from a hundred different yesterdays.

There is no master clock ticking above the universe, keeping one true time for everyone. Physicists looked for it, carefully, for a long time. It is simply not there. Every place carries its own time with it, the way every place has its own weather.

That could sound unsettling, and in the daylight it sometimes does. But consider it from a bed. There is no universal now that you are falling behind. There is no cosmic schedule you are failing to keep. There is only your own time, flowing at its own pace, here, in this room, under this blanket. The universe does not require you to be anywhere in it but here.

§

The clocks people built to catch time are their own quiet story, and the observatory keeps some of them.

On the wall hangs a pendulum clock, older than anyone living. Its heart is just a weight on a rod, swinging. Left, and right, and left, each swing taking the same slice of time as the last. That steadiness was discovered, the story goes, by a young student watching a lamp sway in a cathedral, timing it against his own pulse. The lamp did not care how far it swung. Each swing took the same time. Out of that patient observation came the clocks that ruled three centuries.

Listen, and you can hear it. Tick, and tock. Each tick a small agreed-upon piece of forever.

Astronomers loved these clocks and trusted them almost completely. Almost. For the final word, they always went back to the sky. Each clear night they would watch a chosen star cross the meridian, the invisible line overhead, and set the clock by it. The stars kept the time. The clocks just carried it through the cloudy nights, like lanterns carrying fire. For centuries, the time in everyone's pocket came down from a hill like this one.

§

Then, about a hundred years ago, time turned out to be stranger and softer than any clock had suggested.

A quiet clerk in a patent office, thinking in his spare hours, worked out something remarkable. Time does not flow at the same rate everywhere. Motion changes it. Gravity changes it. A clock carried fast around the world comes home a whisper behind its twin. A clock on a mountaintop, further from the Earth's heavy heart, runs a whisper ahead of one in the valley.

These are not tricks of machinery. Time itself runs differently, place to place, journey to journey. The differences are tiny here. Fractions of a blink across a whole lifetime. But they are real, and they have been measured, with clocks so fine they could notice a staircase. Raise a good enough clock one step, and it ages differently than its twin below.

So even time, the strictest thing we know, turns out to bend. It is not a rigid rail running through the universe. It is more like a river, wide and slow, moving at different speeds along different banks. You are in the gentlest part of the current right now. Lying still, close to the warm earth, letting it carry you.

§

Before the pendulum, people caught time with gentler nets, and the observatory keeps a shelf of them.

The oldest is a bowl of water with a small hole in it. Fill it, and it empties itself at a steady dribble, and the falling water level is the hour. Egyptians used them. Greek courts used them to time speeches, which tells you lawyers have always needed watching. On cloudy days and dark nights, when sundials went blind, the water kept dripping.

There were candle clocks, marked in rings, burning down the evening hour by hour. In parts of Asia there were incense clocks, where time was a slow thread of scent, and you could smell what hour it was. There were hourglasses on ships, turned by a boy whose whole job was the turning.

Every one of them is a way of taming the same wild fact. Time only flows. It never pools, never hurries, never waits. All a clock can do is hang a bucket in the stream. The stream itself was here before every clock, and it slides on tonight, softly, underneath them all.

§

Time, it turns out, can also save lives, and one of its finest hours involved the sea.

For centuries, sailors far from land had a problem. They could find their latitude from the sun and stars easily enough. But longitude, the east and west of the world, hid from them. And the secret of longitude, strangely, was time. If you know the exact hour back at your home port, and the exact hour where your ship floats now, the difference between the two tells you how far around the world you have come. Every hour of difference is one twenty-fourth of the way around.

The whole puzzle came down to one requirement. Carry the time from home, perfectly, across an ocean. Pendulums could not do it. A rolling ship shakes a pendulum senseless.

The answer came from a quiet English carpenter's son named John Harrison, who spent most of his life building four clocks. Not four hundred. Four. Decade after decade, each one better, each one calmer at sea. The last was no bigger than a generous pocket watch, and it crossed the Atlantic losing only seconds. With time held safely in a box, ships finally knew where they were. One patient man, one long life, four clocks, and the map of the world came into focus. Slow work, done well, outlives everything.

§

All those carried clocks needed one home to agree on, and the world chose a hill very like this one.

Greenwich. A green rise above a river, with a small brick observatory on top. Astronomers there watched the stars cross their meridian for so long, and so carefully, that the world's sailors came to set their watches by Greenwich as a habit. And when the nations finally met to choose one line of longitude to count from, the habit won. The prime meridian of the whole planet runs through that courtyard. There is a strip of brass in the pavement, and visitors stand with one foot on each side, smiling, one shoe in each half of the Earth.

Every time zone on the planet is counted from that quiet hill. Noon in one place, midnight in another, teatime somewhere else, all of it agreed by everyone, all of it anchored to a garden by a river. When you set a clock tonight, anywhere on Earth, you are shaking hands with that hilltop. The world runs on many things. More of them than you would think are small, calm places on hills.

§

Today, the keeper of the second is smaller than a raindrop, and steadier than the sky itself.

Deep in a few quiet laboratories, the world's best clocks no longer watch stars or pendulums. They watch atoms. Inside a cesium atom there is a tiny natural rhythm, a hum, always the same, in every atom, everywhere, forever. Count enough beats of that hum and you have exactly one second. Not roughly. Exactly. By worldwide agreement, that hum is what a second is.

These clocks are so faithful that they would drift by less than a single second in millions of years. They are the steadiest things human hands have ever made. And they revealed a small, sweet irony. The Earth itself, the original clock, is the wobbly one. Our planet's spin slows and shivers, minutely, year to year. Tides drag on it. Even great winds lean on it. So every so often, the timekeepers of the world slip one extra second into the calendar, quietly, usually at midnight on New Year's Eve, to let the perfect clocks wait up for the drowsy planet.

Think of that. The whole world, pausing one second, to be kind to the Earth for turning a little sleepily. Even the planet is allowed to slow down. Tonight, so are you.

§

One more question deserves a place on the hill tonight. Why does time only go one way.

You can walk north and then south. You can never walk to Tuesday and back. Mornings become evenings. Never once, anywhere, has an evening become its own morning.

The physicists' answer, told gently, is about tidiness. Things, left alone, drift from ordered to comfortable. A sandcastle becomes a beach. Never the reverse. Sugar spreads through tea. It never gathers itself back into a cube. The universe began wound up tight, like a great spring, and everything that happens is the spring gently letting go. That slow letting-go is the direction we call forward.

Which means the arrow of time is, at heart, the universe relaxing. All of it, everywhere, easing outward into evening. Stars radiating their warmth into the dark. Tea cooling on ten million tables. Shoulders, all over the world, coming down from around ears. You are not resisting the universe when you unwind. You are joining it. It has been unwinding, beautifully, for close to fourteen billion years, and it intends to continue all night.

§

And your own sense of time, the one under your skin, has a kindness built into it.

You have felt how an anxious hour crawls, and a happy one is gone before the kettle cools. Clocks disapprove of this. The mind does it anyway. Inner time stretches and softens with attention, with warmth, with rest.

And at the edge of sleep, it does the kindest thing of all. It lets go entirely. Somewhere in the next while, without noticing, you will stop keeping time altogether. The hours between then and morning will not be counted by you at all. They will pass in a single soft breath. Sleep is the one country where time does not follow you in. It waits politely outside the door, with your shoes. It will all still be there in the morning, every borrowed minute of it, handed back to you with the light.

§

There is no need to keep hold of any of this, by the way.

The four minutes, the old light, the bending river. None of it will be on any test, and all of it will still be true in the morning. If the ideas are beginning to blur together into something soft and starlit, that is not failure. That is exactly the state of mind these hills are for.

Keep only the picture. A small white dome on a dark hill, open to the sky, patiently letting the past rain in. That picture is enough.

§

Before we come down the hill, spend a moment with the two ends of time, the longest and the shortest, because both are restful.

The longest first. The star at the eyepiece has been shining for billions of years, and has billions left. Nothing you did today, and nothing you did not do, registers on its schedule at all. Stars do not have deadlines. They have seasons, so long that whole civilizations fit inside a single one like a bee in a summer. It is oddly comforting, being that small in time. The pressure of the day is not visible from up there. It never was.

And the shortest. Your own body is keeping time right now, and it has never once needed your help. The heart, somewhere below your thoughts, is swinging its soft pendulum. The breath is counting its slow bars, in, and out. You are full of little clocks, all of them winding down for the night exactly as they should. Time does not need watching, from either end. It has managed the stars for billions of years, and it will manage your Tuesday.

§

The logbook on the desk keeps a humbler kind of time, and it may be the best kind.

Open it anywhere. Tuesday, clear, calm. Wednesday, cloud from the west, closed early. Thursday, observed the double star in the Swan. Friday, rain on the dome, stayed for the sound of it. Decades of nights, one plain line each.

No line says hurry. No line says behind. The sky does not give homework. It only gives nights, one at a time, and takes them gently back at dawn. Some nights the astronomer saw wonders. Many nights, only cloud. Both got their line in the book, in the same steady hand. A clouded night is not a failed night. It is simply a night, resting.

Somewhere in those pages there is a night exactly like this one. Mild, quiet, nothing urgent anywhere under the stars. It was enough then. It is enough now.

§

And if you ever wonder whether all this measuring ever touched ordinary life, remember the church bells and the noon guns and the falling balls.

Before wireless, towns needed some way to catch the correct time as it passed. So observatories dropped it to them, almost literally. On rooftops by the harbors, big painted balls climbed their masts each day and fell at exactly one o'clock, so every captain in the port could set his chronometer by eye. One still falls above Greenwich, every afternoon, as it has for two hundred years. In other towns a cannon coughed the hour, once, politely, and every clock in earshot was corrected.

Time used to arrive like weather. It rolled down from the hill, rang from the tower, dropped from the mast, and everyone beneath simply caught it and carried on. Nobody chased it. It came to them. It still does. Morning will roll down over this roof by itself, with no help from you at all. All you have to do tonight is what harbors do. Lie still, and let the hours arrive.

§

The astronomer who works this hill has a bedtime habit, and it is worth borrowing.

At the end of a clear night, the last entry in the logbook is always the same. Conditions, position, time. And then one word. Closed. The dome rolls shut. The instruments are covered. The pencil is set down. Whatever the sky did not reveal tonight, it will offer again tomorrow. The stars are not going anywhere, and they have never once been early or late.

You can close your own logbook the same way. The day's entry is finished. Conditions, fair. Position, home. And then the one word, the good word. Closed.

The dome eases shut overhead, and the round room goes dark and kind. Down the hill, the town has put out nearly every lamp. The pendulum swings in the dark, unhurried, keeping the night for you.

Somewhere above the closed dome, the old light keeps arriving, star after star, moment after patient moment, whether anyone is watching or not. It will go on arriving all night, gentle as snow, on the roof, on the hill, on the town, on this house. Time is falling softly on everything, and everything has agreed to let it.

Let it fall on you too. Let the words go now. Keep nothing.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
