Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, you can let the day come to an end now. Whatever kind of day it has been. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders drop, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing slow, all on its own, the way it does when a long day is finally done. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to walk, very slowly, through a small town at the edge of the desert. A town of bakers and boatmen and stone workers, at the foot of a hill, beside a wide river. It stood there four and a half thousand years ago. The people in it built something you have seen in pictures all your life. They did it a little at a time, and then they went home to supper. Nobody hurried. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin on the river, late in the afternoon, around the year 2550 BC.

This is the Nile, and it is in flood. Every summer, rain falls on far mountains to the south, and the river rises. It spreads out over the fields on either side, brown and slow and shining. For a few weeks the valley becomes a long shallow lake. The villages sit up on their mounds like islands. The palms stand with their feet in the water.

The air is warm, and it smells of wet earth and river weed. The light is going gold. Boats move on the water. Low wooden boats, with a single square sail or a row of oars, carrying stone and grain and jars. Their wakes spread out behind them and fade. Somewhere a heron stands, very still, watching nothing in particular.

To the west, the land rises out of the flood in a low pale shelf of rock. That is the plateau. And on top of it, catching the last of the sun, stand two great shapes of pale stone. One is finished, and one is climbing. The finished one belongs to a king called Khufu. It is the tallest thing that people have ever built. It will stay that way for nearly four thousand years.

But we are not going up there tonight. We are going to the town below it, where the people who built it are coming home.

§

Come down the slope toward the town, with the plateau behind you and the river to your left.

There is a long stone wall here, running east to west, taller than a house. The people who dig here now call it the Wall of the Crow. It has a great gateway in the middle, roofed with enormous slabs, and the road passes through it. Past the gate, the town begins.

It is a town of mud brick, low and warm-colored. The walls are the color of the ground. The lanes between them are narrow, and the light in them is soft, because the sun is already low. There are courtyards, and doorways, and small windows set high. There are storerooms with rounded roofs. There is a long building with rows of pillars. And a bigger one, with thick walls, that looks like it belongs to somebody important.

And the whole place smells of bread. Warm, faintly sour, a little smoky. It is the smell of ovens that have been burning all day and are only now beginning to cool. It hangs in the lanes. It follows you round every corner. You could find the bakeries with your eyes closed.

The archaeologists who dug this town, in our own time, called it the Lost City of the Pyramid Builders. They have been working here since 1988, slowly, brushing the sand away a little at a time. What they found was not a camp. It was a town. It had streets, and workshops, and kitchens, and places to sleep. And a great many ovens.

§

Let us go and stand in one of the bakeries, now that the fires are low.

The room is long and narrow, with a low mud roof. Along one wall there is a trough for mixing dough. Along the other, a bed of ash, still warm, where the fire was. The floor is packed earth. It is dark in here, and close, and the warmth comes up around you from the ground.

The bread was made in pots. Thick, heavy pots of red clay, shaped like bells, each one about the size of a big bucket. The archaeologists found thousands of them, some whole, most in pieces. The Egyptian word for them was bedja. A baker set the pots in the embers until they were hot right through. Then she poured in the dough, thick and pale, made of barley and a kind of wheat called emmer. Then she set a second hot pot upside down on top, like a lid. And she left it there in the ash to bake.

The grain came in whole, and it had to be ground. That was done on a saddle quern. A flat stone laid on the floor, and a smaller stone held in both hands. The grinder kneels, and pushes the upper stone forward, and draws it back. Forward, and back. The grain goes to meal, and the meal goes to flour. A good miller could feel the texture change under her hands, and stop exactly where she wanted it. Coarse for one kind of loaf, fine for another. It is a slow sound, that grinding. Stone on stone, over and over, all morning, in every courtyard in the town.

The loaves that came out were tall and heavy. Dense bread, the kind that fills you. Beside the pots there were flat trays too, for a thinner bread that was baked quickly on the surface. Both kinds were counted and stacked and carried out.

Think about how much bread this town made. Dozens of bakeries. Thousands of loaves every day. All of it from grain that came in by boat. It was stored in great round silos, in a walled compound near the center of town. Flour, water, salt, fire, and a clay pot. That was the whole recipe. And it fed everyone.

Somewhere in the room a pot ticks softly as it cools. That is the only sound.

§

Come out into the lane again, and walk toward the long buildings with the pillars.

These are the galleries. There are dozens of them, set side by side in blocks, each one long and narrow like a hall. The roofs are vaulted, curved like the top of a loaf. Inside, a row of low columns runs down the middle. Along the walls there are raised platforms of mud brick. Sleeping platforms. A man rolls out his mat on one, lies down, and is asleep in a moment.

The crews who slept here did not stay forever. That is the important thing to know about this town. A man came in from his village for a stretch of time, a few weeks or a few months. He worked, he ate, he slept in a gallery with his own people. Then he went home, and someone else came in and took his place on the platform. The galleries were like a great warm inn that never closed.

At the end of each gallery there is a small house of its own. A hearth, and a couple of rooms. That was for the man who was in charge of the crew. He lived beside them, and ate what they ate.

Picture the gallery now, in the evening. Men coming in from the plateau, dusty, tired in the good way. Sandals left by the door. The low murmur of talk. Somebody laughing at something small. Somebody already asleep, an arm over his face. The light from a single oil lamp, going soft along the columns.

§

Now let us talk about supper, because it was better than you might think.

For a long time, people imagined the men who built the pyramids lived on hard bread. Hard bread, and little else. The bones in the rubbish heaps of this town say otherwise. The archaeologists have sorted through tens of thousands of animal bones here. Thousands of them are cattle. Tens of thousands are sheep and goat. There was fish, too, from the river, cleaned and salted in a yard beside the bakeries. There were lentils and beans and onions. There were dates.

Somebody worked out how much meat this meant. On an ordinary day, the town was eating close to two tons of beef and mutton and goat. Meat on the table, every day, for ordinary working people. That was a remarkable thing, in a world where most families ate meat only at festivals.

There was a yard for the fish, over near the ovens. The archaeologists found the floor of it thick with small bones and scales, trodden into the earth. Most of it was catfish, from the shallow flood water, and some was Nile perch. The fish were brought in by the basket, cleaned, split open, and laid out with salt. Then dried in the sun on racks, or packed into jars. Salted fish keeps for a long time, and it travels well. It smells sharp and briny, and the smell sits in that corner of the town, quite separate from the bread.

The cattle did not come from Giza. They came from estates up and down the valley, driven in on the hoof. A few at a time, week after week. Herds coming down the desert edge in the afternoon, raising a low pale dust. Somebody counted them at the gate. Somebody wrote the number down. The whole country was quietly feeding this one town, and the records of it were kept as carefully as the stone.

And there was beer. Not strong, not clear. A thick, cloudy, nourishing drink, brewed from bread, a little sweet, a little sour, drunk from wide clay cups. It was food as much as drink. The breweries were next to the bakeries, because they shared the same grain and the same fires.

And there was cloth. Linen, woven from flax, which is the other great crop of the valley. Flax is pulled up by the roots rather than cut, so the fibers stay long. The stems are soaked in water for days until the soft parts rot away. Then they are beaten, and combed, and what is left is a pale shining thread. Spinners twist it. Weavers work it on low looms laid flat on the ground. Egyptian linen could be woven so fine that light went through it. A worker took home lengths of it, folded, as part of what he was owed.

This is how the workers were paid. Not in coins, because there were no coins yet. They were paid in bread, and beer, and grain to take home, and lengths of woven linen. It was a wage, and it was a good one, and it was written down. The people who built the pyramid were not slaves. They were farmers and craftsmen, fed and housed and paid, doing a job for their king. It is worth saying plainly, and warmly, because it is true.

§

Let us stay at supper a little longer, and listen to the crews talking about themselves.

The men who moved the stone were organized in gangs. A gang was large, a couple of thousand men. Each gang was split into smaller groups. Each of those into smaller ones still. Down to a team of a dozen or so, who worked together every day. The names of the smaller groups were old boat words. Words like starboard, and prow. As if the whole workforce were the crew of one enormous ship.

And the gangs gave themselves names. We know this because they painted them on the stones. In red ochre, on the rough sides of blocks that were never meant to be seen. High inside the pyramid, in spaces no one would enter for thousands of years. One gang called itself the Friends of Khufu. Later, on the third pyramid on this plateau, another gang called itself the Drunkards of Menkaure.

Think about that for a moment. The Drunkards of Menkaure. That is a joke. It is a work gang teasing itself, and the king, and the next gang who would see it. It is the kind of name a crew paints on the side of its truck. People have not changed very much.

The names are still there, in the dark, in red paint. Nobody has touched them up. They did not need to.

§

There is a rhythm to the year in this town, and it is the rhythm of the river.

The Egyptians divided their year into three seasons, and all three belonged to the Nile. First came the flood, when the water rose and covered the fields. Then came the going down. The water drew back and left the mud behind, and the farmers went out to plant. Then came the harvest, in the dry warmth before the river rose again.

During the flood there was nothing to do on the farms. The fields were under water. And so the farmers came here. Thousands of them, from villages up and down the valley. They came for the flood season, and they hauled stone, and ate well, and slept in the galleries. And when the water went down, most of them went home to plant.

So the town breathed with the river. It filled in the summer and emptied in the autumn. The skilled people stayed all year, the stone cutters and the copper smiths and the bakers. The farmers came and went like a tide.

It is a lovely arrangement, when you sit with it. The same water that fed the fields also lifted the boats, and freed the hands that did the building. Nothing was wasted. Everything took its turn.

§

Now let us go down to the water, because a boat is coming in.

We know about this boat because of a man named Merer. He was an inspector, in charge of a team of about forty boatmen. And he kept a logbook. Day by day, in black ink and red, on a roll of papyrus. He wrote down where his boat went, and what it carried. His logbook was found in 2013, in a cave by the Red Sea, at a place called Wadi al-Jarf. A French team found it, led by a man named Pierre Tallet. It is the oldest written papyrus anyone has ever found.

And what it describes is ordinary. That is the beauty of it. Merer's team loaded fine white limestone at the quarries of Tura, on the east bank, across the river. They sailed it down the flooded channels to Giza. They unloaded it. They went back for more. A round trip took a few days. They made two or three trips in every ten. Perhaps thirty blocks a time, each one about the weight of a couple of cars.

He wrote the name of the place he was delivering to. Akhet Khufu, he called it. The Horizon of Khufu. That was the pyramid's own name, the one its builders used.

Picture Merer's boat now, coming in on the last of the light. Long and low, riding deep with the stone. The men at the oars, pulling slow and even. The water dark and gold. And Merer at the stern, with his roll of papyrus, writing down one more ordinary day.

§

The harbor is here, at the foot of the plateau, where the flood comes closest to the stone.

The builders did something clever with the river. They dug. They cut basins into the low ground at the edge of the desert. They joined them to the river by channels. When the flood came, the water poured in and filled them. For six or eight weeks each summer, the river rose by something like seven meters. That is the height of two floors of a house. And the harbor rose with it. Heavy boats that could never have reached the plateau in the dry season. Now they floated right up to its foot.

So the flood was a kind of lift. It carried the stone up, for free, once a year. The builders only had to be ready for it.

Stand at the edge of the basin now. The water is still, and full, and dark. It smells of mud and reeds. Boats lie tied up along the quay, riding gently, their ropes creaking. Blocks of white limestone sit on the bank where they were unloaded, pale in the dusk. Tomorrow they will start their journey up the slope. Tonight they simply rest here, and so do the boats, and so do the men.

Far out on the water, a lamp is lit on one of the boats. A small warm point, doubled in the river.

§

Let us think about how a block moved, once it left the water.

There were no wheels here for this kind of work. There were sledges. Long wooden runners, joined by crossbars, with the block roped on top. Ropes ran from the front of the sledge to a line of men. And they pulled.

But here is the trick, and it is a lovely one. Somebody walked in front of the sledge, pouring water on the sand.

We know this because it was painted on a wall. In the tomb of a man named Djehutihotep, painted some centuries after our evening, there is a picture. It shows a huge statue being hauled on a sledge. And on the front of the sledge stands a figure, tipping water out of a jar onto the ground ahead. For a long time people thought it was a ceremony. Then, in 2014, a group of physicists in Amsterdam built a small sledge and tried it. On dry sand, the runners plow in, and a heap builds up ahead of them. On sand that is just damp, the grains hold together, the surface stays firm, and the sledge slides. The pull needed drops by about half.

Too much water, and it gets worse again. The sand turns to mud. So it had to be just right. Damp, not wet. The person with the jar knew how much. That knowledge lived in a wrist and an eye, not in a book.

Picture it, then. A line of men leaning into the ropes, all together. The sledge creaking. The soft hiss of runners on damp sand. And one person out in front, walking backward, pouring.

§

Most of the stone did not come by boat at all. It came from right here, from the plateau itself.

Just south of the pyramid, the builders opened a quarry in the rock. The great bulk of the monument, the millions of blocks in its heart, came out of that hollow. The fine white stone from Tura was only for the outside, the part you would see. The inside was the hill itself, taken apart and stacked up again.

The tools were simple. Chisels of copper, hardened with a little arsenic in the metal. Wooden mallets. Stone pounders, held in both hands. Wooden levers. Rope. That was all. Copper is soft, and the limestone is soft too, and the chisel bit into it a little at a time. A quarryman cut a channel around a block, and then down behind it. Then he worked levers under it until it came free.

The chisel edges wore down quickly. So they went back down to the town. The coppersmiths worked in a yard near the galleries. The worn chisels were melted, and cast again, and sharpened, and sent back up. The archaeologists found the traces of that yard, too.

Listen to the quarry now, in the evening. Nothing. The tapping has stopped. The men have gone down. The channels they cut today stand open in the rock, clean and square, with pale dust settling into them. And the plateau is quiet, the way a workshop is quiet after everyone has gone home.

§

And now the one question nobody has settled, which is how they got the blocks up.

The pyramid rose in layers, in horizontal courses, one on top of another. The bottom ones are enormous. They get thinner toward the top. Each block had to be dragged up to the level where it was needed, and set in place. And the only way anyone knows to do that, with rope and sledges and people, is a ramp.

But what shape was it. Some people think a single straight ramp, running out from one face, raised and lengthened as the pyramid grew. Some think a ramp that zigzagged back and forth up one side. Some think it wound around the outside like a spiral stair. And one architect suggested a ramp built inside the pyramid, turning up through the masonry, hidden in the walls.

Every idea has something for it and something against it. A straight ramp would have been very long by the end. A spiral would have hidden the corners the builders needed to sight along. Traces of small ramps have been found beside other pyramids, but not the whole answer. So the honest position is that we do not know. Perhaps they used more than one method, at different heights. Perhaps the answer is sitting under the sand nearby, waiting.

It does not need settling tonight. What we know for certain is that they did it. Two million blocks and more, each one moved by hand, each one set down in its place. Twenty years or so of patient work. And then they went home to supper.

§

Let us go back to the very beginning of it, before a single block was laid, and think about the base.

The pyramid stands on a square of rock about two hundred and thirty meters on each side. That is a good few minutes to walk around. And the whole square is level. Not roughly level. Level to within about two centimeters, corner to corner, the width of a finger. Surveyors with modern instruments have measured it and shaken their heads.

How they did it is not written down. But there is a good guess, and it is a quiet one. Water. You cut a grid of shallow channels into the rock. You fill them with water. Water finds its own level, always, without being told. So the surface of the water in every channel is at exactly the same height. You mark that height on the walls of the channels. Then you cut the rock down to the marks.

Simple, and slow, and exact. Some people think it was done this way. Others think they used sighting rods and a long straight edge. It may have been both. Either way, the builders left a low knob of the natural hill standing inside the base. They built around it. Why level what nobody will ever see.

Think of that first evening, whenever it was. A wide flat square of pale rock, swept clean. A few pegs. A line of cord. And the whole enormous thing still to come, all of it resting on the patience of that one flat floor.

§

And there is one more thing they did before they began, which happened at night.

They squared the pyramid to the world. Its four sides face north, south, east and west. Not approximately. Each side is off true north by only a few minutes of arc. That is a small fraction of one degree. Hold your arm out and look at your thumbnail against the sky. The error is far smaller than that.

They had no compass. Nobody would have one for another three thousand years. And a compass would not have helped much anyway, because magnetic north is not true north. So they found it another way, and we are not certain which.

One idea is that they used the stars. Two stars, on opposite sides of the pole, turning slowly around it all night. When one sits directly above the other, the line between them points down to true north on the horizon. So you set up a plumb line, and you wait, and you watch. When the two lights line up with the cord, you mark the ground. An astronomer named Kate Spence worked this out, and noticed something else. Because the sky slowly shifts over the centuries, that pair of stars was only exactly right for a few generations. Near enough to the years the pyramid was built.

The other idea uses the sun instead. You set a rod upright, and you follow the tip of its shadow through a whole day. The shadow tip traces a shallow curve. A line drawn through the right two points on that curve runs true east and west.

Both work. Both leave the same kind of tiny error. We may never know which they used, or whether they used both and compared. It is a quiet argument, carried on by careful people, and nobody is in a hurry to win it.

Picture the surveyors, then, on a warm night before anything was built. Standing on bare rock in the dark, with a cord and a plumb bob. Looking up, and waiting, and saying nothing. The stars turning over them, very slowly.

§

Deep inside the pyramid there is a room they call the king's chamber. The stone in it came a very long way.

The chamber is lined and roofed with granite. Not limestone, granite. Dark, hard, speckled red and gray. It came from Aswan, far to the south, about nine hundred kilometers up the river. That is the distance from one end of a country to the other.

Granite cannot be chiseled with copper. It is far too hard. So the men at Aswan pounded it. They used balls of a harder stone, called dolerite, held in both hands. They struck the granite over and over. Bruising it away a little at a time, until a channel formed. Then a deeper one. Then the block came free. It was patient work of the most patient kind.

The beams that roof the chamber are the largest pieces in the whole building. Each one weighs as much as a few loaded trucks. They were floated down the river on the flood, on great barges. They were dragged up the slope. And lifted, somehow, to a height of forty meters or more. And laid side by side over the room where they still lie.

You cannot see them tonight. Nobody in the town can. They are inside, in the dark, doing their work. Holding up the weight of a hill.

§

Now come back outside, because the light is doing something to the pyramid, and it is worth watching.

The outside of the pyramid was not stepped, the way it looks in pictures now. It was smooth. The builders covered the whole thing in blocks of fine white limestone from Tura. The stone Merer brought across the river. Each block was cut on a slant, so the faces ran together in one flat plane. And each was polished. Rubbed smooth with sand and stone, until the surface was as even as a plastered wall. The joints between the blocks were so tight you could barely slip a blade into them.

There was mortar in those joints, and it did a job you might not guess. It was gypsum. A soft white plaster, mixed with sand and water, spread thin. It was not there to glue the blocks together. Gypsum is far too weak for that. It was there to let them slide. A block set down on wet gypsum will shift under a lever, gently, while a man judges the fit. Then the plaster sets, and the block stays where he left it. So the mortar was a kind of grease that turned into a bed. Millions of tons of stone, floated the last few inches into place on something like butter.

So on this evening, the pyramid is a smooth white shape, four flat triangles meeting at a point. It stands nearly a hundred and forty-seven meters high. That is about four hundred and eighty feet. Taller than anything else people will build for a very long time. And as the sun goes down behind it, the white turns to gold. Then to a warm rose. Then to a soft violet.

For a moment, the very top holds the last of the light after the town has gone into shadow. A small bright point, high above everything. Then that goes too.

The people in the lanes below have seen it every evening of their lives here. Some of them glance up. Most do not. It is simply where they work, and it is beautiful, and it is time for supper.

§

Down in the town, the day is closing in the small ways that days close.

By one of the bakeries, on a warm patch of ground beside the ash pit, a cat has settled. A lean, sandy cat, the color of the walls. She has tucked her paws under her chest and half closed her eyes. The warmth is coming up through the ground from the oven that burned all day. She has found the best place in the town, and she knows it.

A brewer is sealing the last of the jars. He presses a cap of wet clay over each mouth, and smooths it with his thumb. A woman is stacking the empty bread pots in the corner, one inside the next, with a soft hollow clink. Somebody is sweeping a doorway. Somebody is calling a child in.

Up on the plateau, the pale rock has gone blue. The quarry is a darker blue hollow in it. The unfinished pyramid beside the finished one is a shape without edges now. A line of men, small at this distance, is still coming down the slope. Unhurried, toward the gate in the wall.

The first stars are out over the desert. The river is a wide dark band, with a few lamps floating on it. The air has cooled, but the walls are still warm to the touch, giving back the day.

§

And the town sleeps.

The galleries are dark, and full of even breathing. The ovens tick as they cool. The silos stand round and full in their walled yard. Along the quay the boats lie still on the flood water, tied up, their sails furled, waiting for morning. Merer's papyrus is rolled and put away.

Up on the plateau the white pyramid stands in the starlight, faintly luminous, saying nothing. Inside it, in the dark, the granite beams hold. The painted names of the gangs sit where they were painted. Tomorrow there will be bread, and the ropes will creak. One more course of stone will be set in place. Tonight there is nothing at all to do.

Nothing in this town is in a hurry. It never was. It was built by people who understood that big things are made of small days. They were content to cut one block, and move it, and set it down. And then to eat, and to sleep.

§

So let it be quiet now.

Let the last lamp go out along the lanes. Let the cat by the oven go on sleeping. Let the river go on moving slowly past, dark and wide, carrying nothing tonight. Let the pyramid stand in the starlight, whether anyone is looking at it or not.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the bread pots, not the boats, not the names, not a single number from the whole quiet evening. It will all keep itself, as it has for four and a half thousand years. Without any help from you.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, like flour dust on a warm floor. Let the warmth of the walls fade. Let the cool of the desert night come down around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
