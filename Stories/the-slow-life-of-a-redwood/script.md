Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is over now. It is done with. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing find its own slower pace. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are going to spend the night beside a tree.

Not an ordinary tree. The tallest kind of living thing there has ever been. It stands in the fog, near the edge of an ocean. It has been standing there, growing very slowly, for something like two thousand years.

Nothing happens quickly in this story. That is rather the point of it.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

It is late afternoon, and you are walking into a forest on the coast of northern California.

The light changes first. It goes green and dim, the way light changes when you step into deep water. The sky is still up there somewhere, but it arrives secondhand, filtered through a ceiling you cannot quite see.

The sound changes next. The road, the wind, the gulls. All of it fades within a few steps, as though a door has closed softly behind you.

The ground is deep with fallen needles. They are soft and reddish-brown, and they take your footsteps and give almost nothing back. Walking here is nearly silent.

The air is cool, and damp, and completely still. It smells of wet earth, and cedar-ish sweetness, and something faintly like rain arriving.

And on every side, going up and up, are the trunks.

Choose one, and walk to it, and stand at its base.

It is wider than a room. If you and a dozen friends joined hands, you might just reach around it.

The bark is a soft grey-red, and it is deeply furrowed. Great vertical folds and channels, like a heavy curtain gathered at the hem. Moss sits in some of the folds. In others there are small ferns, growing out of the bark itself, ten feet off the ground.

Put your hand on it. It is not hard like oak. It gives very slightly, like cork, and it is cool, and faintly damp.

Now lean back and look up.

The trunk does not taper the way you expect. It goes up, and up, barely narrowing, past where the other trees stop, past where you can comfortably tilt your head. The first branches are higher than most church roofs. The top is simply not visible from here.

You are standing at the foot of something over three hundred feet tall. Some of these trees pass three hundred and seventy. The tallest known living thing on Earth is one of them.

Here is the first quiet fact of the night. A tree like this is mostly made of water and air.

The carbon in all that wood came out of the sky, one molecule at a time, through the needles. The water came up from the ground, and — strangely, wonderfully — down out of the fog.

We will get to the fog. The fog is the heart of this story.

But first, the height. Because the height is not an accident, and it is not simple. The way a tree this tall stays alive at the top is one of the nicest pieces of plumbing in the natural world.

Think about what the top of this tree needs.

Every green needle up there, three hundred and something feet in the air, has to be supplied with water. Continuously. On a warm afternoon, a large redwood can move hundreds of gallons of it in a day.

There is no pump. A tree has no heart. Nothing pushes the water up.

Instead, it is pulled.

At the very top, water is always evaporating out of the needles. It leaves a little at a time, through millions of microscopic pores. As each molecule leaves, it tugs gently on the one behind it.

And water molecules hold hands. They cling to each other. It is the same clinging that pulls water into a rounded drop on a leaf, or holds a film of it on glass.

So the tug at the top travels all the way down. Through the twigs. Down the branches. Down the whole great column of the trunk, through millions of fine tubes, each thinner than a hair. Down into the roots, and out into the damp ground.

The whole tree contains one continuous, unbroken thread of water, from the deepest root to the highest needle. And the sun, drawing vapour off the top, quietly lifts the entire chain.

That chain has a limit, and the limit is written in the shape of these trees.

The taller the column, the harder the pull has to be. Somewhere around four hundred feet, the arithmetic stops working. The tension gets so great that the thread of water is at the edge of what water can do.

The tallest redwoods stand just short of that line.

You can see the struggle at the very top, if you climb. The highest needles live in a kind of permanent gentle drought, even when the roots are standing in wet soil. They grow short, and tight, and stubby, like the leaves of a desert plant. The water arrives up there last, and barely.

The tree is not reaching for anything anymore. It has, more or less, arrived. What is left is the long, slow business of thickening. A little more wood is laid on, all over, every year. A coat of paint, about the width of a coin.

Now, the fog. It is evening now. The fog is beginning to think about coming in. So this is the right time to talk about it.

Offshore, out past the beaches, the ocean here is cold. A slow current runs down this coast. Deeper water wells up along it. It keeps the sea surprisingly cold, all summer long.

Warm air drifts in off the Pacific, touches that cold water, and cools. The moisture in it condenses into droplets too small to fall. That is all fog is — a cloud that has given up on altitude.

And in summer, on most evenings, that fog slides quietly inland. It comes in low, through the river valleys, the way water finds a drain. It arrives in the forest at night, moving at walking pace. By morning the whole grove is standing inside a cloud.

Here is the part that matters. Summer here is the dry season. It may not rain properly for months.

The fog is the rain.

Watch what happens when the fog meets the tree.

The droplets are too small to fall on their own. But a redwood crown is an enormous comb. Millions upon millions of fine needles, held up in exactly the layer of air the fog moves through.

The droplets touch the needles and cling. They merge with their neighbours. They grow. And when they are finally heavy enough, they fall.

Under a big redwood, on a foggy summer night, it rains. Genuinely. You can stand under the crown and hear it. A steady soft patter on the ground. And ten feet beyond the branches, everything stays dry.

The tree is harvesting the cloud, and watering its own roots with it.

Something like a third of all the water these forests get in a year arrives this way. In the driest months it is most of what there is. And the needles can drink some of it directly, up there in the crown. They take water in through their surfaces, at the exact end of the plumbing where the supply runs thinnest.

The tallest trees on Earth grow precisely where the summer fog runs thickest. Away from the fog belt, the species simply stops. That is not a coincidence. It is the whole arrangement.

Fog is the summer arrangement. Winter here works differently, and it is worth a moment.

In winter the storms come in off the Pacific, one after another, wide and grey and slow. It rains properly then. Not hard, mostly. Just long. A day of it, a pause, another day of it.

The crowns catch the rain first, far overhead. You hear it up there long before anything reaches you. Down on the floor it arrives secondhand, gathered into large drops, falling one at a time from three hundred feet.

The creeks fill and go quietly busy. The moss swells and turns a deeper green. The whole forest drinks for five months.

And the trees spend that season doing their year's growing, while the ground is generous. By the time the rains taper off in spring, the work is mostly done. Summer, with its fog, is not the growing season. It is the holding-on season, and the fog is what makes the holding easy.

Before the fog thickens tonight, pick up a cone from the ground, because the cone is a joke the forest likes to tell.

It is tiny. The cone of the tallest tree in the world is about the size of an olive.

Roll it in your palm. It weighs nothing. Inside it are the seeds, and each seed is a small flat flake, lighter than a grain of oatmeal. It would take well over a hundred thousand of them to weigh a pound.

Everything above you started as one of those. A flake of almost nothing, landed on damp ground, in some autumn nobody remembers.

The arithmetic of it is very gentle. A big redwood sheds millions of seeds in a good year. Almost none of them become anything. The moss is thick, the floor is dark, and a seedling needs a rare patch of bare, open ground.

The tree does not mind. It has centuries of autumns to try. One success in a lifetime is enough. And even the failures are not really failures. Most new redwoods do not come from seed at all. They rise from the base of their elders, already connected to a living root.

Let us talk about time, because this tree keeps a different calendar than we do.

A redwood can live past two thousand years. The elders in these groves were seedlings around the time of the Roman Empire. They were already old when the cathedrals were going up. Everything in recorded history since then has fit comfortably inside their lifetime, and they have spent it doing almost exactly this.

And the species itself is far older than any single tree. Redwoods, as a kind, go back well over a hundred million years. Their relatives once grew across much of the northern world.

The world moved on. It got drier, and cooler. The great redwood forests drew back, and back. In the end they held only this. A narrow ribbon of foggy coastline, a few hundred miles long, where the conditions they like still hold.

There is nothing sad in that. It is what it looks like when something finds the one place that suits it perfectly, and settles in, and stays.

The name people gave this tree is worth a moment.

The botanists called it sempervirens. It is Latin, and it means always green, or, read a little more warmly, ever-living.

It is a fair name. This species does not really have a natural end built into it. A redwood does not age the way an animal ages. There is no winding down written in it anywhere.

The living part of the tree is only a thin sleeve under the bark. Each year that sleeve makes a new layer of wood on its inside, and new bark on its outside. The sleeve itself stays young. A two-thousand-year-old redwood is, in the only tissue that matters, about as young as a sapling.

The great age is simply what happens when nothing manages to interrupt it. Given water, and fog, and its family around it, there is no particular reason for it to stop.

And the tree writes all of it down.

Every year of growth is one ring of wood. A wet, kind year makes a wide ring. A hard, dry year makes a narrow one. The record is continuous, and it cannot be revised, and in an elder it runs back well over a thousand years.

When an old redwood does come down, people with patience can read it. Ring by ring, counting inward. They match the pattern of wide and narrow against other trees. It is a walk backwards, through centuries of coastal weather.

Here is a wet decade in the twelfth century. Here is a long dry spell before that. Here, near the centre, is the seedling's first year. A summer eighteen or twenty centuries gone, recorded by the only witness still available.

A library where each book takes a thousand years to write, and the writing is simply the weather, remembered in wood.

The bark deserves a section of its own, because the bark is the reason there are elders at all.

It is astonishingly thick. On a mature tree it can be a foot deep. A full twelve inches of soft, fibrous, cork-like padding sits between the world and the living wood.

It is also full of tannin. That is what makes the wood so red, and it is bitter, and insects want nothing to do with it. Fungus struggles with it too. The usual agents of decay can take an oak or a pine apart in decades. Here, they mostly cannot get started.

So the tree is wearing a coat that does not burn easily, does not rot easily, and does not taste good. Underneath it, the living layer gets on with its work, year after year, largely undisturbed.

That is the whole secret of the age. Nothing about a redwood is in a hurry, and almost nothing can interrupt it.

Now the roots, because the roots are a genuine surprise.

A tree three hundred and fifty feet tall should, you would think, have a root like an anchor. A great taproot, driven down as deep as the tree is tall.

There is no taproot. The roots of a redwood go down barely ten or twelve feet. In a tree of this size, that is nothing. It is a dinner plate under a lamppost.

What the roots do instead is spread. They run outward, just under the surface, for fifty feet, a hundred feet, out beyond the reach of the crown above.

And there they meet the roots of the other redwoods. And they interlace. Root crosses root, and grips. Over the years, neighbouring roots press together and slowly graft. They genuinely grow into one another, until the plumbing of one tree runs into the plumbing of the next.

A grove of redwoods is not a collection of trees standing near each other. Below the ground, it is very close to being one connected thing.

When the winter storms come off the Pacific and lean on a single tree, that tree does not stand alone. It is held by every root system around it. They hold each other up. None of them could stand alone at this height, and none of them has to.

The connectedness goes further than holding on. This is my favourite part.

When a redwood falls — and after a thousand or two thousand years, eventually, one does — the story does not end. The roots are still alive. The stump is still alive.

Within a season or two, a ring of bright green shoots comes up around the base. They rise from the stump's edge, and from the roots. They come from dormant buds that have been waiting under the bark, in some cases for centuries.

The shoots become saplings. The saplings become trees. And because they all rose from the rim of the one original base, they stand in a circle.

Walk through these forests and you will find them everywhere. Perfect rings of great trees, ten or twelve of them, standing around an open centre. People call them fairy rings, or family circles.

The open centre is where the elder stood. The circle is the same tree, continuing. Same roots. Same living tissue. The ring may be far older than any trunk in it. The trunks are merely the current generation. Underneath them is a thing that has been quietly reissuing itself, on the same spot, for thousands of years.

And sometimes, in these groves, you come across something that looks like a small miracle.

A redwood with white needles.

Not pale green. White — soft, waxy, ghost-white, like a shrub cut from paper. They are rare. A few hundred are known, scattered through the whole range, and people who love these forests keep their locations fairly quiet.

The white tree is a sprout that came up without the ability to make chlorophyll. No chlorophyll means no green, and no green means it cannot make a single grain of its own sugar. By every rule of botany, it should live a few weeks and starve.

It lives for decades.

It lives because its roots are joined to its neighbours' roots — usually the very tree it sprouted from. Sugar made in the green crowns high above flows down, crosses through the grafted roots, and feeds the white one.

Nobody is entirely sure what, if anything, the white tree gives back. There are theories. But the plain fact on the ground is simple, and it is a gentle one. The grove carries it.

For most of history, everything above the first branches was a mystery. The crowns of these trees begin twenty storeys up. Nobody had been there.

Then, a few decades ago, a small number of researchers worked out how to climb.

It is done gently. A thin line is sent up over a high branch, and a rope follows it. The climber goes up the rope, touching the tree barely at all. They walk up alongside three hundred feet of bark.

What they found up there was not the top of a tree. It was a landscape.

The crown of an ancient redwood is not one trunk with branches. Centuries of weather break the top. The tree answers by sending up new trunks. They rise from branches, and from the old broken places. Those trunks grow branches of their own, and some of those turn upward and become trunks in turn.

An elder can carry dozens of them. Whole extra trees, growing out of itself, fused and bridged and crossing, hundreds of feet in the air.

And in the angles and hollows where they meet, debris has been collecting for centuries. Needles, bark, dust, rain. It has rotted down, slowly, into genuine soil. In places the soil in the crown lies a metre deep.

Soil is an invitation. Ferns arrived, and made dense green mats the size of garden beds. Huckleberry bushes grow up there, and produce berries, three hundred feet off the ground. Other trees — hemlocks, spruces — sprout in the canopy soil and live their whole lives as passengers.

The fern mats soak up fog and rain and hold thousands of gallons of water aloft, like cisterns. Birds nest up there. Small things live and die up there without ever touching the ground.

It is a garden, on a roof, that took a thousand years to assemble. And from below, you would never guess any of it.

There is one resident of that high garden worth meeting before we come down.

It is a salamander. Small, brown, unhurried — the kind of creature you would expect to find under a damp log on the forest floor.

Except this one lives in the crown. Whole populations of them spend their entire lives up there. Hatched in the wet moss of a fern mat. Feeding in it, sheltering in it. Generation after generation, hundreds of feet in the air.

They have no need to come down, so they never do. The fern mats hold all the moisture and food a salamander could want. Their forest floor simply happens to be on top of a tree.

It breathes through its skin, which is why it loves the fog as much as the tree does. On a grey wet night the whole crown suits it perfectly.

A creature that has never touched the ground, living in a garden that has never been planted, watered by a cloud. That is the top of a redwood.

Before we settle into the night, look down for a moment, because the floor has residents too.

The ground here is not bare. It is covered in redwood sorrel. Small clover-shaped leaves, in wide soft mats, a deep fresh green even in this dim light.

Sorrel is built for shade. It runs on a hundredth of the sunlight the treetops get. When a rare direct beam finds it through a gap, the leaves actually fold themselves down. It takes them minutes, as though the light were rain. Too much sun is the one thing here it has no use for.

And there, crossing a fallen branch at its own speed, is a banana slug.

It is bright yellow, the length of your finger, and it is in absolutely no hurry. It may be the least hurried animal in North America. It eats fallen leaves, and mushrooms, and it tidies the floor of the grove a few inches a minute.

It suits this place. Everything here moves at one of two speeds. Centuries, or slug.

Come back down now, gently, in your mind, all the long way, and stand on the needles again.

It is night now. The grove at night is one of the quietest places there is.

The needle floor absorbs sound. The trunks absorb sound. There is no wind down here. The crowns high above take all of it. What reaches the ground is a hush, like distant water.

Somewhere an owl calls, once, and does not insist.

And the fog has come in. You can feel it more than see it. The air has thickened, and gone soft. Your breath is visible when the moonlight finds it. The trunks fade upward into pale grey and disappear at about the height of a house. Everything above that is cloud.

Then it begins. First one drop, somewhere off to the left. Then another. Then a slow scattered patter all around you, on the needles, on the ferns, on the broad leaves of the sorrel.

The trees are combing the fog. All through the grove, all night, millions of needles are collecting the cloud drop by drop and letting it down.

It is not rain. The sky beyond the crowns is clear. This is the forest watering itself.

The sound is irregular and soft, like the very beginning of rain that never has to arrive. Each drop lands on deep needles and is absorbed at once. By morning the top foot of ground will be damp through. The fog will draw back to the sea. No one passing at noon would know anything had happened.

This happened last night. It will happen tomorrow night. It has happened, on some thousands of summer nights, for the whole life of every elder in this grove.

Stand with it a while. There is nothing here to follow anymore.

The tree beside you is doing what it was doing when you arrived, and what it was doing a thousand years ago. It is lifting water, a molecule at a time. It is adding wood, a coin's width a year. It is holding its neighbours, and being held.

Nothing about it will change tonight. Nothing about it will change this century.

Somewhere above, a salamander is sitting in a wet fern mat, hundreds of feet up. Somewhere below, sugar is moving through joined roots, to a white tree in the dark, from a family it has never seen.

The drops keep falling, here and there, without pattern and without hurry.

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the fog, or the chain of water, or the rings of trees standing where their elders stood. Not the garden on the roof of the forest.

It is all going on anyway, whether or not anyone is standing under it tonight. It was going on long before this evening. It will be going on afterwards, at exactly the same pace. Which is barely any pace at all.

Let the words go. Let the picture soften and drift apart, like fog does around the trunks.

There is nothing out there that needs you tonight. There is nothing here that does either.

Only tall old trees in a cloud, drinking it slowly, holding each other up in the dark.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
