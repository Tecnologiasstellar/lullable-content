Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been. You can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to sit, very quietly, at the edge of a table in an old palace in Milan. A candle is burning on it. A man is filling a page with small brown writing, and drawings of water, and birds, and the moon. He is not in a hurry. He never finished most of what he started. That was not the point. Neither will we hurry.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

We begin in Milan, in the early evening, a little more than five hundred years ago.

The year is somewhere in the fourteen-nineties. It is late in the season. The plain around the city is flat and wide, and the air off it is cool and a little damp. The light is going. The cathedral in the middle of the city is still being built. It has been building for more than a century. Nobody minds. Its pale stone goes grey, and then blue, as the daylight leaves.

Beside the cathedral, on the south side of the square, stands an old palace. It belonged to the dukes before this one. The court has moved away to a newer castle across the city. So the old palace has been given over to other things. Storage. Workshops. And to a painter from Florence, who has been lent some rooms in it.

The city is threaded with canals. They come in from the rivers and the lakes to the north. Barges bring the cathedral its marble on them, block by block. It comes from a quarry beside a lake, far up in the mountains. The stone rides down one river, and across the lake, and down another river, and along the canal. It comes ashore at a quay in the south of the city. It has done this for a hundred years. The stone is pale, and faintly pink. In the evening light it looks almost warm.

Walk in under the arch. The courtyard is quiet. There is a smell of horses from somewhere, and of damp plaster, and of wood shavings. A lamp is lit in a window on the upper floor. That is where we are going.

Climb the stairs slowly. They are stone, and worn, and cool under the hand.

The room at the top is not large. It has a table, and a chair, and a shelf. There are sheets of paper on the table, and a stack of small bound books, and a jar of pens. On the wall, a row of powders in jars. Red, and yellow, and brown. A cloak hangs on a peg. Under the window, a bowl of water, and beside it a folded cloth.

One candle burns in the middle of the table. It gives a soft amber light. It reaches the paper, and the man's hands, and not much else. The corners of the room stay in shadow. The ceiling is lost.

He is sitting with his back half to us. He is in his forties. His hair is long, and it is going grey. He is fond of rose-colored clothes. It is in his lists. He is holding a pen. He is looking at the page. He is not writing yet.

Everything about the room says that nothing is urgent. Find a place to settle. A stool by the wall will do. The stone behind you holds the cool of the evening. The candle holds the warmth.

Now watch his hand, when it moves.

It moves from right to left. That is the first strange thing. He is left-handed. He begins each line at the right edge of the page, and works toward the left. The letters are small, and slanted, and joined. And every one of them is backward. A word he writes tonight could be read easily in a mirror. Read straight, it looks like a private language.

Nobody knows quite why. The plainest guess is the best one. A left hand, moving left to right across wet ink, drags through what it has just written. Moving the other way, the hand stays ahead of the ink, and the page stays clean. He learned it young, and he kept it. When he wrote something meant for other people, he wrote the ordinary way. These pages are for himself.

Some people, later, thought it was a code. It was not much of one. Anyone with a mirror could read it, and many did. He did not seem to care. He simply wrote the way his hand went, and let the rest of the world turn the page around.

So the hand goes right to left, in the amber light. The pen makes a soft dry sound. Now and then it stops, and lifts, and he looks at the line. Then it goes on. Backward, as he sees it. Forward, as we would say.

The ink is brown. It was black when it went on. As it dries it warms, and settles. Over the years it will go the color of walnut shells. Most of what we have of him is in that color now. Warm, dark brown, on paper the color of cream.

Let us stay with the ink a moment, because it is a lovely thing to know.

It begins with a wasp. A small wasp lays its egg in a young oak twig. The tree grows a hard round knob around the egg, the size of a marble. That is a gall. Gather the galls, and dry them, and crush them, and soak them in water. The water fills with tannin, the same sharp stuff that is in strong tea.

Then add iron. A little green iron salt, dissolved in the same water. The two meet. The liquid darkens, almost at once. A pinch of gum from an acacia tree makes it flow from the pen instead of soaking away. And that is the ink.

It goes onto the paper thin and black. As it dries, it bites in a little. The iron and the tannin bind with the air, and with the fibers. The line settles down into the sheet. It cannot be wiped off. It cannot be washed out. Slowly, over many years, it goes from black to that deep brown.

It has a smell, if you lean in. Faintly sour. Faintly of iron, like a wet coin held in the hand. It is not a strong smell. It is the smell of every page he ever wrote.

That is what is on the table, in a small clay pot with a lid. Oak, and iron, and a little gum. He dips the pen. The nib comes up dark and full. He touches it once to the rim, to let the extra fall. And he goes back to the page.

The pen itself is a feather. A quill from the wing of a goose.

It was cut with a small knife, on the table, by hand. The soft end of the feather is trimmed away. The hard hollow shaft is cut on a slant, and shaped to a point. Then a fine slit is made down the middle. That slit is what makes it work. The ink climbs into the hollow and rests there. Every stroke draws a little more down through the slit and onto the page.

It needs sharpening every so often. He stops, and pares the tip, and turns it in the candlelight, and tries it on a scrap. Then he goes on. The little knife lives on the table for exactly that.

The paper is made of cloth. Old linen shirts and sheets, worn to rags. Gathered up, and torn, and beaten in water until the fibers come apart. Then a fine wire mesh is dipped into the vat, and lifted. The water runs through. A thin even mat of fibers is left behind. Pressed. Dried. Sized with a little gelatin, so the ink will sit on it and not spread.

Hold a sheet up to the candle, if you like. You can see the faint lines of the mesh in it. And a mark in the middle, like a watermark, which is what it is. Somebody's sign.

Not everything on the table is ink. There is a short stick of red chalk. Warm and soft, the color of dry earth. And there is a thin metal point, in a holder, for drawing on paper that has been coated first. It leaves a line as fine and grey as a hair. He uses all of them, depending on what the thing in front of him needs.

Now look at what he is drawing tonight. It is water.

Not the sea. A stream. He has been standing beside one this afternoon, on the edge of the city. There is a channel there, and it runs past a stone. And he has been watching what the water does.

It does not go straight. It meets the stone and divides, and folds back on itself behind it. Little wheels of water turn in the shelter of the stone. The wheels spin off smaller wheels. And those spin off smaller ones still. Where the stream falls into a pool, the surface rolls under. Then it comes up again in ropes and curls.

He draws them. Line after line, in ink. The curls, and the ropes, and the little wheels. On one sheet, beside the water, he writes himself a note. He tells himself to notice how the surface of moving water looks like hair. Like braided hair. The heavy strands turn one way, and the fine strands another. And both are moving all the time.

The canals have locks in them, too. Wooden gates, a pair at each end of a chamber. A boat goes in, and the water rises, and lifts it to the next level. He is interested in those. He draws a pair of gates that meet in the middle at a shallow angle, pointing upstream. The water pushes on them, and the push holds them shut. Nobody has to hold them. The harder the water leans, the tighter they close. It is a small idea. It is still in use, in the largest locks in the world. But to him it is only more water. The stream, and the lock, and the curl behind the stone.

He will come back to water for the rest of his life. He fills whole notebooks with it. How it wears a bank away. How it carries sand, and lets it go. How a bubble rises. How a river bends, and why the outside of the bend is deep, and the inside is shallow. He is trying to understand it by looking, since there is no other way. He knows he will not finish. He does not seem to mind.

The candle flickers. The water on the page holds still.

Turn the page with him, and there are birds.

He has been watching them too. From the roof of the old palace, in the afternoons, he can see the kites turning over the city. And he has noticed things.

He has noticed that a bird does not flap all the time. It rides. It leans on the air the way a swimmer leans on water. When it wants to rise without effort, it turns into the wind, and the wind lifts it. When it wants to turn, it drops one wing a little. When it wants to slow, it spreads its tail and tilts it. Like a hand held flat against a current.

The kite is a good bird to watch, because it hardly seems to work. It has long wings, and a forked tail. It hangs over the rooftops for an hour at a time. Turning. Adjusting. Dropping a shoulder. The tail is the part he watches most. It twists a little, this way and that. And the whole bird answers.

He draws the wing. Feather by feather, and then the whole shape of it. He notes how the bone runs along the front edge. How the feathers trail behind. How the tips can open like fingers. He watches how the air must press up under the wide part, and slip past the narrow part.

Years from now he will gather these thoughts into one small book. Eighteen leaves, not much bigger than your hand. It is in Turin today. It begins with birds. It ends with wondering whether a person could ever do the same. Tonight it is only a few sheets. A kite over the roofs. A man with a pen, watching how it leans.

Outside the window, the sky has gone dark. There is a thin moon over the cathedral.

He looks at it for a long moment. And here is a thing he sees, that most people see, and few think about.

The moon is a crescent. A thin bright curve of it is lit by the sun. But the rest of the moon, the dark part inside the curve, is not fully dark. You can just see it. A faint grey disc, held in the arms of the crescent. Ghostly, and plainly there.

He asks himself where that faint light comes from. And he works it out, sitting at a table, with no instrument but his eyes.

It comes from here. From the Earth. The sun lights up the Earth. The Earth throws some of that light back into space. Some of it reaches the moon, and lights the dark side softly. The way a lamp in one room lights the doorway of the next. He thinks it is the oceans that do the reflecting, because water shines. We would say now that it is mostly the clouds. But the idea is right. And he is the first person we know of to write it down.

He writes it in his book of water, which is also his book of the moon. To him they are the same subject. Light, and what it lands on. That book is in private hands today. It travels from city to city, and people stand in line to see the page.

There is another thing in that same book, and it belongs to the water too. Up in the hills, far from any sea, people find shells in the rock. Sea shells. Whole beds of them, in the stone, high above the valleys. Most people say a great flood carried them there. He does not think so. He has looked at them. They lie in layers, the way shells lie on a beach. A flood would have tumbled them.

So he says, quietly, that the sea was here once. That the hills were sea floor. That rivers laid mud down over the shells, and the mud went hard. And that the whole bed was lifted, slowly, over a time he cannot count. He is right about nearly all of it. It would be a long while before anyone else said so plainly.

Look at the crescent once more, if you like. The bright curve. The soft grey held inside it. That is Earth-light, falling on the moon, and coming back to you.

And while we are looking out of the window, let us look at the far hills.

In daylight, from the roof, he can see the mountains to the north. They are not brown, or green, though he knows they are made of rock and trees. They are blue. The farther away a thing is, the bluer it looks. And the softer. And the paler. The nearest ridge is nearly grey. The farthest is almost the color of the sky.

He wants to know why. So he thinks about the air. There is a great deal of air between his eye and a distant hill. And the air is not nothing. It is full of tiny drops of moisture, too small to see. When the sun strikes them they glow faintly blue, all together, against the dark of the space beyond. That is why the sky is blue, he says. Not because the sky is painted. Because you are looking through lit air into darkness.

A far hill is seen through a great deal of that lit air. So the air's own color lies over it, like a veil. And he tells himself, as a painter, how to paint distance. Make the far things bluer. Make them paler. Soften their edges, so that nothing far away has a hard line. Let them dissolve a little.

That last idea has a name in Italian. A word that means something like smoke. The way a shape can fade into the air around it, without an edge. He spends his life on it. You can see it in his faces, where the corner of a mouth simply goes soft, and is gone.

Tonight it is a note. A few lines about air, and blue, and distance. In mirror writing, beside a sketch of hills.

On the shelf there is a jar with a cut branch in it. Something he brought in from a garden.

He turns it in the candlelight. He is looking at where the leaves join the stem.

They do not come out in a straight line, one above the other. They spiral. Each leaf sits a little way round the stem from the one below it. Follow them up, and you go round, and round. By the sixth leaf you are back above the first. He writes that down. The sixth above the first. A rule of five, going round.

He guesses why. If the leaves came out one above the other, each would shade the one below. This way, each gets its own piece of the sky. The rain runs down from one to the next. And the dew at night, he says, falls on every upper face. Because every leaf turns its upper face to the sky.

He draws the branch. Then he draws the shape of a whole tree. He notes how the trunk splits. He notes the branches at any height. Add their thickness together, and it comes to about the thickness of the trunk. He is not a botanist. He is a painter who wants his trees to be right. But the looking is the same.

The branch stands in its jar. The leaves hold their spiral in the small warm light.

There is a larger sheet at the back of the table, weighted at the corner. Let us look at it.

It is a drawing of a man. He stands inside a circle, and inside a square, at the same time. His arms are out. His legs are together, and then apart, so that there are two figures in one. Around him, in mirror writing, run notes about proportion. The span of the arms equals the height. The face is a tenth of the height. The foot is a seventh. And so on.

The ideas come from an old Roman book on building, written fifteen centuries before. That author said a well-made body fits both shapes. He tested it. He measured people. He drew.

The drawing is in pen and ink, over a first faint sketch in metal point. The ink has gone brown. The paper has gone soft and cream. It is in Venice today, kept in the dark. It is brought out only now and then, because light is not kind to paper.

A few years on from this evening, a friar will come to the court. A mathematician, from a small town in the hills, who teaches arithmetic and geometry. The two of them become friends. The friar is writing a book about proportion, and about the solids. Cubes, and pyramids, and shapes with twenty faces, and shapes with many more. He asks the painter to draw them. So he does. Dozens of them, in ink and color. Each one is shown as if it were made of wood, and hollow, and hanging in the air on a ribbon. So that you can see through it to the far side.

It is the only book he ever illustrated. It was finished here in Milan, and printed years later. And when people want to show what those shapes look like, his are still the drawings they reach for.

But tonight it is just a sheet, on a table, in Milan. A circle, a square, a man, and the calm brown writing running around him.

Across the city, on the far side, there is a monastery. It has a long dining hall, and a plain wall at the end of it.

That wall is his other work these years. He is painting on it. A long table, and thirteen figures, and the light of a window behind them. It is very large. Almost thirty feet across. He has been at it for years.

He does not work on it the way most wall painters do. The usual way is quick. You paint on wet plaster, a patch a day. The color dries into the wall, and there is no changing it. He does not like that. He wants to go slowly. He wants to look, and change his mind, and come back. So he paints on dry plaster instead, with a kind of tempera, the way you would paint on a panel.

He walks there most days. It is a long walk, from one side of the city to the other. Through the market, where the stalls are being taken down. Past the walls of the new castle. Along a street of workshops, where the shutters are closing and the smell of sawdust is in the air. He walks slowly. He looks at everything. People are used to it.

The monks tell stories about him. That some days he comes at dawn, and paints until dark, and does not eat. That other days he comes, and climbs the scaffold, and looks at the wall for an hour. Then adds one stroke, and leaves. That some days he only looks.

It is that looking that we can leave with. A quiet hall, in the evening, with the tables cleared. The smell of bread and damp stone. A man on a scaffold, under a lamp, studying the tilt of a hand he painted last month. And deciding, after a long time, to leave it as it is.

Back at the table, turn a few pages, and the notebook stops being grand.

Here is a list. It is not a list of ideas. It is a list of things to do, and people to ask. Ask a man from Florence how they walk on the ice in Flanders. Find a master of arithmetic, and have him show how to square a triangle. Find someone who knows about water. Have him explain how they mend a lock, and a canal, and a mill. The way it is done here in Lombardy. Ask about the measurement of the sun, which a Frenchman promised. Measure the old courtyard. Draw Milan.

He knows what he does not know. So he goes and asks. That is the whole method, written out in a column. Half of these he will never get to. That is all right. The list is the pleasure.

Farther down, a shopping list. Paper. Chalk. Charcoal. On another sheet, a list of his clothes. On another, a joke about a friar. And a riddle in the shape of a solemn prophecy, made to be read aloud at the duke's table. And a fable about a stone that wished to be somewhere else, and rolled down the hill, and was sorry.

He puts them all in the same book. The moon, and the groceries, and the joke. Because it is one mind, and it is all one evening. And paper was expensive, and every corner of the page got used.

Let us think for a moment about the books themselves, in a stack at his elbow.

Some are small. Pocket size, bound in leather. He could carry one on his belt, and draw a face in the street. Some are single loose sheets, folded once. Some are large. He writes on whatever is near.

We have about seven thousand pages of them. That sounds like a great deal. It is probably less than half of what he made. The rest went where paper goes when nobody is watching. He knew it might. He tells himself, more than once, to put the pages in order. He never does.

After him, the pages traveled. Some stayed in Milan. More than a thousand leaves are kept together there, in a library beside a church. They are bound in a great set that took its name from the size of the sheets. Some went to England, into the king's collection at Windsor. Six hundred sheets of them, the finest drawings. Some went to a library in London. And the little book of water and the moon went from hand to hand for centuries. It is still in private hands tonight.

So he is scattered. But he is kept. In cool rooms with low light, where the humidity is watched, and the pages are lifted with both hands. Seven thousand pages, in that walnut ink, on that cream paper, in that backward hand.

He sets the pen down for a moment and leans back. And we can let him remember, since he is not writing.

He was born in a hill town, a long day's ride from Florence. It is called Vinci, and that is all the surname he ever had. Leonardo, from Vinci. Olive trees on the slopes. A small stone house in a hamlet above the town, with a wide view down the valley. He was a country child. He learned the shape of things from things, not from books, because he had few books.

He has one memory from that time that he writes down, years later, in the corner of a page about birds. That when he was very small, still in the cradle, a kite came down to him. It brushed his lips with its tail, and went. He says he took it as a sign that birds would be his subject. Whether it happened or not, he believed it. And it is a lovely thing to have kept from being one year old.

At fourteen he went down to Florence, to a workshop. The master was a goldsmith who had become a sculptor and a painter. It was one of the best shops in the city. And a shop, in those years, made everything. Altarpieces. Bronze bells. Painted banners. Armor for a festival. Tomb slabs. So a boy in it learned everything. How to grind color. How to cast metal. How to make plaster. How to draw a fold of cloth from a real fold, soaked in wet clay so it would hold still.

He stayed for years. By twenty he was a master in his own right. He stayed anyway, because he liked it there. Then, at thirty, he came north to Milan, with a letter offering his services to the duke. And here he has been since.

He sits with that a moment. The olive slopes, and the loud shop, and the long road north. Then he picks the pen up again.

And let us go forward now, a long way, to where the story rests.

More than twenty years from this evening, he will make one more journey. He will be sixty-four. He will cross the mountains on a mule, with his notebooks in his baggage. And three paintings he cannot bring himself to part with. He is going to France.

The young king there admires him. He gives him a manor house of soft red brick and pale stone. It is in a village called Amboise, on the river Loire. It stands a short walk from the royal castle, in a small green valley, with a stream running through the garden. The king gives him a title, and a pension, and asks nothing much in return. Only that he be there, and talk.

The king's own castle stands on the rise above the town. There is a story of a passage between the two houses, under the ground. So that the king could come and go as he liked. Whether or not there was one, he came often. He sat, and asked questions, and listened. He was young, and the old man was patient with him.

So picture that last table. A wide room. A fireplace. A window on the garden. The Loire below the town, broad and slow and pale, with sandbars in it, and willows on the bank. The light in the valley is softer than the light of Milan. Damper. More silver.

And on the table, the same notebooks. The same brown ink, the same backward hand, slower now. He is still drawing water. He is still writing lists. On one late sheet there are cats. A whole page of them. Sleeping, stretching, washing, curled up. He drew them the way he drew a river. To see how the spine bends.

The river goes by. The pen goes on. He never finished, and that was never the point.

Come back to Milan now, to the small room, and the candle, which has burned low.

Something moves on the windowsill. It is a cat. It has come in over the roofs. It sits on the sill with its tail around its feet, and looks at the flame. It has done this before. It is not a surprise to anyone.

He looks up, and looks at it a long moment, the way he looks at everything. Then he pulls a scrap toward him and draws it in four lines. The curve of the back. The ears. Then he lets it go, and returns to the page.

The candle gutters. The flame leans, and shrinks, and then stands up again. There is not much wax left. The amber light on the paper has gone smaller and warmer. The shadows in the room have come in closer, gently, like a blanket being drawn up.

He writes one more line, right to left. Then he lifts the pen and looks at the page. He decides it will do. He wipes the nib on the cloth, and lays the pen in the jar, and puts the lid on the ink.

And the room grows still.

The pages lie on the table, drying. Water, and birds, and the crescent moon. The blue hills, and a branch in a jar, and a list of things to ask. The ink is going from black toward brown, very slowly. It will go on doing that for five hundred years.

The cat has closed its eyes. The candle is a small blue bead on a wick. Outside, the thin moon hangs over the cathedral, with the soft grey Earth-light held inside its curve. Down in the courtyard, a horse shifts, and is quiet again.

He does not know that any of this will be kept. He does not know the pages will be lifted with both hands in cool rooms, in cities he has never seen. He only knows that the water curled around the stone today. And that he saw it. And that he wrote it down.

The flame goes out. There is a thread of smoke, and the smell of it, and then the dark.

So let it be quiet now.

Let the ink dry on the page in the dark. Let the moon go on lighting the roofs of the city. Let the stream on the edge of town go on curling around its stone, whether anyone is watching or not. Let the birds sleep in the towers.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the ink, not the names, not a single fact from the whole quiet evening. It will all keep itself, as it has for five hundred years. In cool rooms, with the light kept low. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, like a sheet of paper laid down on a table. Let the candle warmth fade. Let the cool of the old stone come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
