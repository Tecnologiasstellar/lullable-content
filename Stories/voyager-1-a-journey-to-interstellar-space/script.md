Good evening.

Whatever kind of day it has been, you can set it down now. It is finished. It has no more claim on you. Let your shoulders come away from your ears. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw soften. Let your breathing find a slower rhythm on its own. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting for your answer.

Tonight we are going to follow something small and very far away. It is a machine. It is about the size of a small car, and it is made of aluminium and gold and old wire. It left here in nineteen seventy-seven, and it has been travelling ever since.

It is called Voyager one. It is not going anywhere in particular. It is simply going, steadily, in a straight line, through the dark between the stars. We are going to travel out along its path and sit beside it for a while.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin to travel.

We begin somewhere warm and quiet, on the ground.

Picture a wide dish of white metal, tilted up at the night sky. It stands in a valley of dry grass outside Canberra, in Australia. It is seventy metres across. From below it looks less like a machine and more like a shallow bowl, big enough to hold a house.

It is very still. Dishes this size move slowly, in small careful increments, tracking one point in the sky as the Earth turns underneath them.

There is a hum from the cooling gear. There is wind in the grass. There is a smell of eucalyptus and warm dust.

The dish is listening.

It is not listening for anything loud. What it is waiting for is a whisper of radio. The machine that sent it is so far away that the signal has been in flight for almost a full day.

The dish gathers that whisper into a point, and the point becomes numbers, and the numbers say: still here.

Let us follow that signal back the way it came.

Go up. Softly, and without effort.

The valley falls away. The coastline appears, and then the curve of the whole world, with its thin blue rim where the air gives out.

You pass the Moon. You pass Mars, small and rust-coloured. You cross the wide belt of drifting rock, spread so thin you could pass through it a thousand times and touch nothing at all.

You pass Jupiter, banded in cream and amber. You pass Saturn, tilted, with its rings casting a thin shadow across its own clouds.

Now keep going. Uranus is off somewhere to one side. Neptune, further still.

And still keep going.

The Sun behind you is shrinking. It is still the brightest thing in the sky, but it has stopped being a disc. It is only a very bright star now, and it gives no warmth at all.

You are going a long way past all of it. This part of the journey takes a while. Let it.

It is worth sitting with the distance for a moment, because the distance is the whole story.

Voyager one is about fifteen and a half billion miles from here. That number does not mean anything to a human being. Nothing does, at that size.

So use light instead.

Light is the fastest thing there is. It crosses from the Moon to your window in about one and a quarter seconds. It takes about eight minutes to come from the Sun.

To reach Voyager one, light needs almost a whole day.

Send a message from that dish in Australia, and it arrives roughly twenty-three hours later. If the spacecraft answers, the answer takes another twenty-three hours to come back.

So every question anyone asks it is followed by a wait of nearly two days. You send. You go home. You sleep. You come back. The next day, the answer arrives.

There is no way to hurry it. Everyone who works on this has simply learned to be patient.

The listening is not done from one place. It is done from three.

There is a valley in the Mojave desert in California. There is a site in the hills outside Madrid. And there is the one in the dry grass near Canberra.

They are spaced roughly a third of the way around the world from each other. As the Earth turns, one of them is always facing any given patch of sky. Nothing is ever out of earshot for long.

Hearing is the easy half. A very faint signal can be gathered by several dishes at once, and the results added together, which makes a small sound larger.

Speaking is harder. To send a command that far you need a transmitter with real power behind it, and a dish wide enough to aim it precisely.

For Voyager one, that is the seventy-metre dish in Australia. It is the only one on Earth with enough push in its transmitter to be heard at that distance.

So when that dish went down for a long refit, the team planned around it for months in advance, the way you would plan around a bridge closing.

It was launched on a Tuesday, in the late summer of nineteen seventy-seven.

There is a small pleasing oddity in that. Voyager two went first, on the twentieth of August. Voyager one went sixteen days later, on the fifth of September.

The one called number one left second. It took a faster path, and it overtook its twin, and it has been ahead ever since.

They were sent because of an accident of arithmetic. Every hundred and seventy-six years or so, the four outer planets happen to line up along one long curve. A single spacecraft can visit several of them, one after another, using each one to reach the next.

Whoever was alive in the nineteen seventies got the alignment. That was the only reason for the timing. The planets were standing in the right places.

The trick that made it possible is worth understanding, because it is quietly elegant.

You cannot carry enough fuel to cross the solar system. Nobody can. So instead you borrow.

A spacecraft falls towards a planet and swings around behind it. On the way in, the planet's gravity pulls it faster. On the way out, that same gravity pulls it slower. Straight in, straight out, no gain.

But the planet is moving. And if you arrange the approach correctly, you leave with a little of the planet's own motion added to yours.

The spacecraft speeds up. The planet slows down by an unmeasurably small amount, and continues around the Sun as though nothing happened.

It is a very gentle theft. Nothing is spent. Nothing is burned. You simply arrive at the right angle and let the geometry do the work.

Voyager one did this twice. At Jupiter, in March of nineteen seventy-nine. At Saturn, in November of nineteen eighty.

At Saturn there was a decision to make, and it is one of my favourite things about this mission.

Saturn has a moon called Titan. It is the only moon in the solar system with a real atmosphere — thick, orange, and hiding whatever is underneath it. In nineteen eighty nobody knew what was under that haze.

The planners could send Voyager one onward to Uranus and Neptune. Or they could bend its path in close past Titan, and look.

Choosing Titan meant giving up the rest of the tour. The angle needed to pass the moon would throw the spacecraft up and out of the flat plane where the planets live. After that it could never visit another one.

They chose Titan.

So the spacecraft came in close over that orange haze, took its measurements, and then curved upward and away — northward, out of the plane, out of the neighbourhood.

Voyager two, following behind, took the long road instead, and went on to Uranus and Neptune. Between them they covered everything. That was the arrangement.

The haze did not give much away, but it gave enough.

The cameras could not see through it. They never did. What came back was a smooth orange ball with no features on it at all, which must have been a strange thing to receive after such a long journey.

But the other instruments could work. They found that the air there is mostly nitrogen, like ours. They found that it is thicker at the surface than the air in this room. And they found that it is extremely cold — something like a hundred and eighty degrees below zero.

Nitrogen, thick air, deep cold. That combination meant something interesting could be happening down there, in slow motion, in the dark.

It took another twenty-five years to find out what. A small probe was carried out by a later mission and let down through the haze on a parachute, in January of two thousand and five.

It landed on damp sand, among rounded pebbles, on what looked very much like an old streambed. The streams there are not water. They are methane, and it rains in slow fat drops.

Voyager could not see any of that. It only established that the question was worth asking. Sometimes that is the whole job.

Now let us go alongside it and look at the thing itself.

It is smaller than you expect. The main body is a ten-sided drum, about the width of a dining table.

On top of that sits the dish — a white antenna, three and a half metres across, aimed permanently back at Earth. That aim matters more than anything else on board. Everything else is arranged around keeping it pointed correctly.

Two long booms stick out at angles. One carries the instruments. One carries the power.

There is no engine running. There has not been for decades. It coasts, at about seventeen kilometres per second, and it will keep coasting because there is nothing out here to slow it down.

Small thrusters fire in tiny puffs to hold its angle steady. Each puff lasts a few thousandths of a second. That is all the moving it does.

There is no light on it except starlight. Its surfaces are wrapped in gold-coloured insulating blanket, and out here that gold is very dark.

Its power comes from warmth, which is a lovely way to run a machine.

On that second boom there are three generators. Inside each one is a quantity of plutonium, sealed in ceramic. It is not a reactor. Nothing is happening in there except decay, which happens whether anyone is watching or not.

Decay makes heat. The heat is turned directly into a trickle of electricity, with no moving parts at all. Nothing spins. Nothing wears out.

The trade is that the trickle gets slightly smaller every year, as the plutonium quietly gets on with it. About four watts a year, from the whole spacecraft.

The radio transmitter that talks to us runs on roughly twenty-two watts. That is about a refrigerator bulb.

Then that whisper crosses fifteen billion miles. By the time it reaches the dish in the dry grass, it arrives at less than a billionth of a billionth of a watt.

That it arrives at all is the part I find restful. It is faint beyond any sensible definition of faint, and it comes in every day.

The computers on board are worth a moment, because they are so very small.

There are six of them, in three matched pairs. Between them they hold about sixty-nine kilobytes of memory. That is less than a single photograph on the phone beside your bed.

They handle roughly eight thousand instructions a second. A modern phone does billions.

There is also a tape recorder. An actual reel of magnetic tape, which was used to store readings until a dish on Earth was in position to receive them.

And the data comes home at a hundred and sixty bits a second. That is slower than the modems people used to connect to the internet in the nineteen nineties.

None of this is a problem. There is nothing out there that needs deciding quickly. The whole machine was built by people who assumed there would be plenty of time, and they were right.

Bolted to the side of the drum, under a cover, there is a record.

An actual record. Twelve inches across, copper, plated in gold. There is a stylus in a bracket beside it, and instructions etched into the aluminium cover explaining which speed to play it at.

On it there are greetings in fifty-five languages. There is music from many countries. There are the sounds of surf, and wind, and rain, and a train, and a kiss, and a mother speaking to her child.

There is the song of a humpback whale.

And there is an hour of one woman's brainwaves and heartbeat. They were recorded while she sat in a laboratory, thinking about what it feels like to be in love.

None of it was ever expected to be found. It was made anyway, carefully, by people who knew that.

The cover of the record is the part I keep coming back to.

In one corner there is a small diagram of the record and its stylus. Beside it is the speed to turn it at. That speed is written in the language of the hydrogen atom — because hydrogen is everywhere, and it behaves the same everywhere.

There is a map. Fourteen lines radiate from a single point, and each line names a pulsar — one of those small dense stars that pulse at an extremely regular rate. The length of each line gives the distance. The markings give the rhythm.

Anyone who could read it could work out where the Sun is, from the pattern of those fourteen clocks.

And in a patch about two centimetres across, electroplated onto the aluminium, there is a little ultra-pure uranium.

That is a clock as well. Uranium decays at a known and unhurried rate. Half of it goes in four and a half billion years.

So whoever finds it can measure what is left, compare it to what has become of the rest, and work out how long the record has been drifting.

It answers a question nobody has yet asked. It will keep answering it for a very long time.

There is one more thing it did before it left the neighbourhood, and then it stopped looking.

On the fourteenth of February, nineteen ninety, it was already some three and a half billion miles out. On that day the spacecraft was turned around, to face the way it had come.

It took a set of photographs of the planets it had left behind.

In one of them, in a band of scattered sunlight, there is a small pale speck. It is less than a single pixel across. It is slightly blue.

That is here. That is the whole thing — every ocean, every city, everyone who has ever lived, all of it, in a dot you could cover with a fingertip.

The cameras were switched off shortly afterwards, to save the warmth for other work. That photograph was the last thing they were asked to do.

Then came a very long quiet stretch. Decades of it.

You have to understand what the space between the planets is actually like. It is not empty, exactly. The Sun is constantly breathing outward — a thin wind of charged particles, streaming away in every direction, at hundreds of kilometres a second.

That wind blows a bubble. The bubble is enormous, and everything you have ever heard of lives inside it. The planets, the comets, all of it.

Voyager one spent thirty-five years crossing that bubble.

As it went, the wind grew thinner and slower and cooler. There was nothing to see. No landmarks. No arrivals. Only the instruments, taking their patient readings, and the numbers coming home at the speed of light and no faster.

That is most of the mission. Not the flybys. The waiting.

And then, in the late summer of two thousand and twelve, it crossed out of the bubble.

There is no wall there. Nothing to feel. What happens is that the character of the surroundings changes, and it changes in a way you can measure.

Inside the bubble, the gas is thin — around two particles in every thousand cubic centimetres. Outside, in the space between the stars, it is roughly forty times denser. Still emptier than any vacuum ever made on Earth. But denser.

The way they proved it is beautiful and slightly indirect.

There is an instrument on board that listens to plasma the way you might listen to a wine glass. Give the gas a knock, and it rings. And the pitch it rings at depends only on how many electrons are in it.

In April of two thousand and thirteen, a wave from the Sun rolled outward and knocked. The gas around the spacecraft rang at about two point six kilohertz.

They read the pitch and converted it to a density. It matched the space between the stars.

Then they worked backwards through the older data. The crossing had happened the previous August. It happened at about a hundred and twenty-two times the Earth's distance from the Sun.

Nobody was there to see it. They found it afterwards, by listening to what the dark was ringing at.

Even so, be careful with the word "left".

The Sun's gravity still holds it. It will hold it for tens of thousands of years. Far out beyond where Voyager is now, there is thought to be an enormous loose cloud of icy bodies. They are still faintly bound to the Sun. And the spacecraft is still deep inside that cloud.

So it has not gone. It has crossed a boundary of wind and pressure, not a boundary of belonging.

It is a bit like leaving the harbour. The land is out of sight, the water is different underneath you, and you are still very much on the same ocean you started on.

The people are the part I would like to spend a moment on, because they are still there, and they are quietly remarkable.

In the autumn of two thousand and twenty-three, the spacecraft kept sending its carrier tone but its data turned to nonsense — the same meaningless pattern, over and over.

It took months to find. Eventually they traced it to a single failed memory chip in one of the on-board computers, which had taken a small piece of the software down with it.

You cannot replace a chip fifteen billion miles away. So they did something else.

They took the affected code, cut it into pieces small enough to fit into the working parts of the memory, and tucked each piece into a different spare corner. Then they rewrote all the internal references so the computer could still find everything.

To make room, they deleted routines the spacecraft no longer needed — including some of the software written for the Jupiter flyby in nineteen seventy-nine.

The first piece was sent on the eighteenth of April, two thousand and twenty-four. The confirmation came back on the twentieth. Two days, for a single yes.

There was a similar piece of quiet work the following year, on the thrusters.

Voyager has small thrusters that roll it slightly, to keep the dish aimed at Earth. The original set had been considered out of service since two thousand and four. A backup set took over and did the job for twenty-one years.

Over that time, a fine residue had been slowly narrowing the backup lines. Very slowly. But narrowing.

So the team went back to the set that had been written off two decades earlier. They worked out what had actually failed. Their conclusion was that it might be a switch, rather than the thruster itself.

They warmed the hardware, sent the commands, and waited out the two-day round trip.

In March of twenty twenty-five the old thrusters fired. They had been cold and unused for twenty-one years, and they worked.

Most of the engineers who built them have long since retired. Someone opened the manuals, read what people had written down in the nineteen seventies, and made it run again.

There is also, now, a sort of household thrift on board, and it is done with great care.

The spacecraft launched with ten instruments. Most were for looking at planets, and their work finished long ago.

As the plutonium's trickle narrows, the team goes through the remaining ones. They decide, slowly and with a great deal of argument, which to switch off.

It is the way you might turn off the lamps in rooms nobody is sitting in. You do it so that the lamp in the room you are using stays lit.

Two are still on. One measures magnetic fields. One is that same instrument that listens to the gas ringing.

Between them they are still reporting, every day, on the character of the space between the stars — a place no other object built by human hands has ever been.

Managed carefully, that could continue well into the next decade.

And that listening instrument found something rather lovely, a few years ago, that nobody had been looking for.

Until then, the readings came in bursts. A wave would roll out from the Sun, arrive months or years later, knock the gas, and the gas would ring. Then quiet. Then eventually another one.

A doctoral student in New York went back through the raw data, patiently, looking at the gaps between the bursts.

And in the gaps there was something. A very faint, very narrow band of emission, running steadily on and on from two thousand and seventeen onward.

Not a knock. Not an event. Just the gas itself, sounding quietly at its own pitch, all the time, with nothing setting it off.

It is faint, and it is monotone, because it sits in such a narrow band of frequency. A hum, essentially. The space between the stars turns out to have a hum in it.

And it means the density can be measured continuously now, rather than only when something loud comes past. A background nobody noticed, doing more work than the events did.

And there is something coming, quite soon, which is simply an arrival.

On the eighteenth of November this year, at sixteen minutes past two in the morning, California time. At that moment Voyager one will be sixteen billion and ninety-four million miles from the Earth.

That is exactly the distance light travels in twenty-four hours. One light-day.

Nothing will happen. There is no marker out there. The spacecraft will not notice, and neither will the dark around it.

But for the first time, something made by people will be a full day of light away from the place it was made.

It has taken forty-nine years of steady, uninterrupted coasting to get there. And it will simply keep going.

So let us leave it there, drifting, and just look for a moment.

There is no sound. There is no wind, no weather, nothing to erode it. The gold blanket is not tarnishing. The record under its cover is not decaying. Out here almost nothing happens to anything.

Ahead, the stars do not move. They will not appear to move for a very long time.

In about forty thousand years the spacecraft will pass reasonably close to a small dim star in the constellation of the Giraffe. Reasonably close, out here, means about a light and a half years away.

It is not aiming for it. It is not aiming for anything. It is simply going in the direction it was pointed in nineteen eighty, at seventeen kilometres a second. It has a gold record bolted to its side. And its dish is still turned patiently back towards us.

And every day, without fuss, the whisper arrives.

It crosses fifteen billion miles of nothing at all. It falls into a white bowl in a valley of dry grass. Somebody reads it, and writes down that all is well, and goes to get a cup of coffee.

That is happening tonight, while you lie here. Somewhere it is the middle of the afternoon. A dish is tilted at an empty patch of sky. It is listening to something the size of a small car, twenty-three hours away.

Nothing about it is urgent. Nothing about it ever was.

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the distance. Not the numbers. Not the record, or the dish, or the small steady puffs that keep it aimed at home.

It is all still going on, whether or not anyone is following it tonight. It has been going on since long before this evening, and it will carry on afterwards, at exactly the same unhurried pace.

Let the words go. Let the picture go soft and come apart.

There is nothing out there tonight that needs you. There is nothing here that does either.

Just the dark, and something small and gold moving through it, very slowly, in a straight line.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
