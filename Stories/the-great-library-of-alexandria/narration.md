Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to walk, very slowly, through a building that no longer stands. A library, in an old city, beside a warm and quiet sea. The work they did there was patient work. Done by lamplight, a little at a time, for hundreds of years. Nobody hurried. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin at the harbour, late in the afternoon, more than two thousand years ago.

The city is Alexandria, on the northern edge of Egypt, where the land meets the Mediterranean. The air is warm and salted. The light is going gold. Out in the water stands a great pale lighthouse, and its fire has not been lit yet, because the sun is still up.

The harbour is calm. Wooden ships lie at anchor with their sails furled, rocking a little, their ropes creaking softly. Somewhere a gull calls, once, and then lets it go. The water slaps gently against stone. And the whole city smells of salt, and warm dust, and the cedar of the ships.

Grain comes through here, in enormous quantities, in sacks stacked under awnings. Linen comes through. Glass, and spices, and rope. And so do books. Every ship that ties up at these quays is carrying something written, somewhere in its hold.

Behind the harbour, the streets run straight and wide. They were laid out that way on purpose, in a neat grid. The sea wind could move through the city, and cool it. You can feel that wind now, moving up from the water. Soft against your face. It carries the last of the day's heat away with it.

§

Walk inland a little, away from the water, into the quiet quarter of the city.

Here the buildings are pale stone, and the light comes off them softly. There are gardens. There are covered walkways with rows of columns, and long shaded colonnades where the air stays cool all day. There are fig trees. There is the sound of water somewhere, moving slowly in a channel.

This is the Museum. Not a museum as we mean it now, with things in cases. It was a house for the Muses. Which is only an old way of saying a quiet place set aside for thinking. Scholars lived here. They were given rooms, and meals. And, best of all, they were given time. They had nothing to do but read, and talk, and slowly work things out.

And attached to it, filling hall after hall, was the library.

§

Let us stay outside a moment longer, under the colonnade, and think about that life.

A scholar here was chosen by the king, and appointed for life. He was given a salary. He was given lodging inside the walls. He ate at a common table with the others, at the end of the day. He paid no tax. Nothing was asked of him except that he think, and teach, and write things down.

It is a strange and lovely arrangement, when you sit with it. Somebody decided that understanding was worth paying for. Not a particular result. Not a discovery by a certain date. Just the slow work itself, funded, and left alone.

One of the early ones was a man named Euclid. He gathered up what was known about lines and circles and numbers, and he put it in order. Thirteen books of it. He invented very little of it himself. What he did was arrange it, so that each proof rested only on what came before.

That is why it lasted. It was still being taught, almost unchanged, two thousand years later. There is a story that a king once asked him for a shorter way through it, and was told, kindly, that there was not one. Whether or not he said it, the building agreed with him. Nothing here had a shorter way.

§

Step inside now, out of the last of the daylight.

The air changes at once. It is cooler in here, and still, and it smells of dry papyrus and old cedar oil and a faint trace of lamp smoke. The floor is stone. It is worn smooth in a soft path down the middle, where feet have passed for generations.

Along both walls, from the floor to well above your head, run rows of wooden shelves. And in the shelves are not books as you know them, but scrolls. Thousands upon thousands of them, lying on their sides in niches. Each one rolled around a wooden rod. Each one with a small tag hanging from its end, so a reader can tell what it is without unrolling it.

The cedar oil is not there for the smell. It was rubbed on to keep the small insects away, the ones that eat paper and glue. A resin from cedar and juniper, brushed on, year after year. The whole building is quietly defending itself, all the time, with something that smells like a forest.

Think about how a reader works in here. He does not browse a spine at eye level. He walks along the niches, and reads the little tags, and tilts his head. When he finds the one he wants, he draws it out by the rod, slowly, so the edges do not catch.

Then he takes it to a table near the light. He unrolls a hand's width at a time. The papyrus is stiff at first, and it wants to curl back, so he weights the corner with something small and smooth. And then he begins.

The tags stir a little when the door opens. That is the only movement in the room.

§

Let us stay here a while, and think about what a scroll actually was.

They were made from papyrus, which grew in the marshes of the Nile in tall green stands. Picture that place for a moment. Shallow warm water. Reeds well above the height of a person. A soft green roof of them, moving together when the wind comes across.

The cutters went out early, while it was cool. They worked in the shallows, taking the thickest stems near the base. The stems are three-sided, and they come away with a clean wet sound. The bundles were carried up onto dry ground and laid out in rows. And the marsh closed over the gap, and went on growing.

The stems were cut, and the pith inside was sliced into long thin strips. The strips were laid down side by side, in one direction. Then a second layer was laid across them, at right angles.

Then it was pressed. Simply pressed, under weight, for days. The plant's own sap held the layers together. As it dried it became a single smooth sheet, pale as cream. No glue. No stitching. Just the plant, and pressure, and time.

The sheets were then joined, edge to edge, into a long roll, sometimes many metres long. The joins were made with a little overlap, and smoothed down, so a pen would not catch as it crossed one. A good roll had joins you could barely feel with a fingertip.

There were grades of it, too, sold at different prices. The finest sheets came from the innermost pith, the palest and the smoothest. The coarser ones went to lists, and receipts, and the wrapping of parcels. A scribe would know the difference by touch, in the dark.

And on the good rolls a scribe would write in narrow columns. So a reader could unroll a little with one hand, and roll up a little with the other. Moving through the text like a slow window.

§

The ink was simple too, and it is a lovely thing to know.

It was made of soot. Fine black carbon, gathered from lamps and cooking fires, ground very smooth. That was mixed with a little gum from an acacia tree, and a little water. Then dried into a hard cake. When a scribe wanted to write, he wet the tip of his pen and touched it to the cake. The ink came up again, black and ready.

Carbon does not fade. It does not react with much of anything. It simply sits on the surface of the fibre and stays. That is why words written this way have lasted for thousands of years in the dry air of Egypt. Still perfectly readable. Long after the people who wrote them were forgotten.

Soot, and tree gum, and water. That was all it took to speak to us across two thousand years.

§

And the pen itself deserves a moment, because it changed here.

The Egyptian scribes had written for a very long time with a rush. A thin stalk, chewed or bruised at one end until the fibres separated. It behaved rather like a soft brush. It laid the ink down gently, and it suited the shapes of Egyptian writing.

The Greeks who came to Alexandria brought a different tool. A reed, cut on a slant, with a small split in the tip. Harder. Sharper. It held a fine line, and it suited Greek letters, which want an even stroke and a clear edge. Over the Ptolemaic years the reed slowly spread through the workshops. In the end, even the Egyptian scribes were using it for their own script.

It is a small thing to sit with tonight. A cut piece of river reed. It needed trimming every so often, with a little knife kept on the table for exactly that. The scribe would stop, and pare the tip back, and turn it in the light. Then wet it, and go on.

Almost everything we have from the ancient world came to us through that tip. A hollow stem, from the edge of the water, sharpened by hand.

§

Now let us watch the scribes at work, at the long tables near the lamps.

There is no other way to make a book here. If the library wants a second copy of something, a person must sit down. And write out the whole thing by hand. So they do. Quietly, hour after hour.

A scribe sits cross-legged, or at a low table. The scroll he is copying is propped where he can see it. He reads a line. He writes a line. He reads the next. His pen makes a soft dry sound on the papyrus. Every so often he pauses to re-wet it. The pause is part of the rhythm.

Watch his hands, if you like. The left one rests flat on the roll, holding the place. The right one moves a short distance, and stops, and moves again. His shoulders are down. His breathing has gone slow, the way yours does when a task is long and there is no end of it in sight tonight.

Nobody is rushing him. A long work might take him weeks. Somewhere in the middle of it his handwriting will loosen slightly, and then tighten again, and a reader two thousand years later will notice, and smile.

And when he is finished, another scribe will read his copy against the original. Looking for slips. Marking them gently in the margin. Two people, and a great deal of patience. And at the end of it there is one more copy in the world than there was before.

§

The library gathered its scrolls with a kind of gentle greed.

There is an old story, and it may be true. Any ship arriving in the harbour had to hand over the books it carried. The library would copy them. It kept the original, and gave the copy back. Whether or not that is exactly how it happened, the spirit of it is right. They wanted everything.

They sent people out to buy scrolls in other cities. They collected works in Greek, and had works translated into Greek from other languages. They wanted medicine, and geometry, and astronomy. Poetry, and law, and farming, and cookery. Not the best of it. All of it.

And slowly, over generations, the shelves filled. Then they overfilled. So a second library was opened, in a temple precinct on higher ground across the city. A daughter library, people called it. Later writers say it held some forty thousand scrolls of its own, though the numbers we have are late, and probably soft.

Hall after hall, in the cool dark, in two buildings now, all of it written by hand, one line at a time.

§

With so many scrolls, they ran into a problem that will sound very familiar.

They could not find anything.

So a poet named Callimachus sat down and did something quietly extraordinary. He went through the collection and made a list of it. Author by author. He noted who had written what. He grouped the works into classes, and then into narrower subjects. Poetry here. Law there. Medicine, and rhetoric, and history.

Within each class he put the authors in alphabetical order. He wrote a line or two about each one, so a later reader would know who the person had been. He recorded how many lines long each work was. And he gave the opening words of each one, so it could be told apart from another with a similar name.

It ran to a hundred and twenty scrolls, just to describe the other scrolls. It was, as far as we know, the first real library catalogue anyone had ever made. Somebody had to invent the idea that a collection could be described at all. He did it by hand, alone, at a table, by lamplight, like everything else here.

§

And then there is the smallest invention in the building, which you still use.

The librarians here spent lifetimes on old poems. They had many copies of Homer, from many places, and the copies did not agree. A line would be in one and missing from another. A passage would appear twice. A word would have quietly changed on its way through three hundred years of copying.

So they made marks. Little symbols, set in the margin beside a line, in place of an argument. A short horizontal stroke meant a scholar doubted the line belonged. An arrowhead meant there was something here worth noticing. A dotted arrowhead meant, politely, that another scholar had read it differently.

And there was a small star, for a line that had been repeated, or that seemed to belong somewhere else. The Greek word for it means, more or less, little star. It is where our asterisk comes from. It has been sitting in the margins of human writing ever since.

Think of the temper of that. They disagreed about a poem for generations, and what they built out of the disagreement was a set of quiet symbols. No shouting. Just a mark, and a note, and the line left standing for the next reader to judge.

§

And in the rooms next door, with all this within reach, people were working things out.

A man named Eratosthenes worked here, and ran the place for a while. He had heard about a town far to the south. There, at noon on midsummer's day, the sun stood directly overhead, and a stick cast no shadow at all. In Alexandria, on that same day, at that same hour, a stick did cast a shadow. A short one.

He measured the angle of it. It came to about a fiftieth of a full circle. He knew roughly how far away that southern town was. And from those two small facts, and a little geometry, he calculated the distance around the earth.

He gave the answer in stadia, an old unit of length. And here is the gentle open question. We are no longer sure how long his stadion was. The estimates run from about a hundred and sixty metres to a little over two hundred.

So the honest position is this. If his stadion was the short one, he was within a couple of percent of the true figure, which is astonishing. If it was the long one, he was out by a few percent more, which is still astonishing. We cannot settle it, and it does not need settling tonight. He worked out the size of the planet from a shadow, and a walk. And the arithmetic he could do on a sheet of papyrus. He never had to leave the building.

§

Others sat in these same rooms and did the same patient thing.

Someone here measured the year. They found it a little longer than three hundred and sixty-five days. So it was proposed, in a royal decree, that a day be added every fourth year, to catch it up. That was more than two centuries before Rome did the same thing. The idea was written down, and then quietly set aside, and then taken up again much later. It is in your calendar tonight, either way.

Someone here made maps with lines of latitude on them, so that a place could be described by number and not only by name. Someone here worked on the sizes and distances of the sun and moon, and got some of it wrong, and wrote down the method anyway, which is the useful part.

None of it was dramatic. It was all slow, and careful, and done sitting down.

§

There is one idea from these years that I would like to leave with you, because it is so quiet, and it was so far ahead.

A man named Aristarchus, from the island of Samos, proposed that the earth is not the centre of things. He suggested that the earth turns on its own axis each day. And that it travels around the sun, along with the other wandering lights.

Almost nobody accepted it. And the objection was a good one, which is what makes this worth telling. If the earth really swings from one side of a great circle to the other, then the stars ought to shift a little as we go, the way a near tree shifts against a far hill when you lean. Nobody could see them shift.

Aristarchus had an answer. The stars must be enormously far away. So far that the whole width of our path around the sun is nothing beside the distance. That is a very large claim to make in the third century before Christ. And it is exactly, precisely right.

His own book on it is lost. We know the idea mainly because Archimedes described it in passing, while working on something else entirely. One geometer mentioning another's odd notion, and that mention is the reason we still have it at all.

§

Meanwhile, far to the north, a second library was doing the same work with a different material. It is worth a moment, because it is beautiful craft.

The city was Pergamon, in what is now Turkey. The story that comes down to us is that Alexandria stopped sending papyrus, and Pergamon had to find another surface to write on. So they perfected one, out of animal skin. The story is probably tidier than the truth. Skins had been written on long before. But the name stuck, and our word parchment carries that city inside it still.

The making of it is all patience. The skin is soaked for days in a bath of lime, until the hair loosens and lifts away. Then it is washed, and stretched on a wooden frame, and tightened evenly all round.

Then, while it dries, it is scraped. With a crescent-shaped blade, held in both hands, in long slow arcs. Wetted, dried, scraped again. Thinner, and thinner, and more even. At the end it is rubbed with pumice until it is smooth, and dusted with chalk so the ink will sit without spreading.

Two cities. Two materials. Reeds pressed flat by the Nile, and skins stretched on frames in the hills. Both of them made by hand, slowly, by people who would never read most of what was written on them.

§

Back in Alexandria, listen. There is water moving somewhere in the building.

An engineer named Ctesibius lived in this city, and he was interested in air, and water, and what they do under pressure. Among other things, he built clocks.

A water clock is a simple idea. Water drips out of a vessel at a steady rate, and you read the time from how much has gone. The trouble is that a full vessel pushes harder than an empty one, so the drip slows as the day goes on, and the hours come out uneven.

His answer was to add a third vessel. It kept the middle one always brimming, always at the same pressure, so the drip never changed. The water ran down into a bowl below, and a float rose on it, and the float carried a small figure with a pointer. Through the day, without anyone touching it, the little figure rose slowly past the hours.

It corrected itself. Nothing in it needed a person. That is a very old and very deep idea, and this is one of the first places it appears in the world. And for something like sixteen hundred years, no one built a clock that kept better time.

You can hear it now if you like. A slow, even, unhurried drip, somewhere down the corridor, marking the evening for nobody in particular.

§

The light is going now. Let us watch the lamps.

As the evening comes on, the attendants move through the halls. They light the oil lamps, one after another. Small clay lamps. Each has a little wick floating in olive oil. Each gives a soft round light, warm and yellow and steady.

The scrolls turn amber where the light reaches them. The shelves throw long soft shadows up the walls. And the smell of the place deepens, papyrus and cedar and now the faint sweet smoke of burning oil.

The scribes work a little longer. Then, one by one, they set down their pens, and rinse them. They roll up what they were copying, and tie it, and slide it back into its niche. The reading rooms grow quiet.

Out in the harbour, the great lighthouse fire is lit. It stands on an island at the mouth of the port, and it is very tall, taller than anything else these people have built. A square base, an eight-sided middle, a round tower at the top. Pale limestone, block on block, the joints run with lead against the sea.

The fire burns in the upper chamber. Behind it, they say, was a polished sheet of metal, to gather the light and throw it out across the water. It lays a long trembling line of gold across the waves. Ships far out will see it and know where the land is, hours before they arrive.

§

And the library sleeps.

It is fully dark now inside. The shelves are only shapes. Somewhere a shutter moves in the sea wind. The stone floors hold the coolness of the evening. Thousands of scrolls lie in their niches in the dark, saying nothing. Waiting patiently for morning, and for hands that know where to find them.

Down the corridor the water clock goes on dripping, and the little figure goes on rising, very slowly, through hours that no one is reading.

Nothing in this building is in a hurry. It never was. It was built by people who understood that understanding takes a long time. They were content to add one careful line to it, and then another. And then to put down the pen, and go to sleep.

§

So let it be quiet now.

Let the lamps go out, one by one, along the halls. Let the sea wind move slowly through the colonnades. Let the harbour water go on lapping at the stone. Let the lighthouse go on turning its soft gold over the waves, whether anyone is watching or not.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the scrolls, not the names, not a single fact from the whole quiet evening. It will all keep itself, as it has for two thousand years. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, like dust on a shelf in a dark room. Let the warmth of the lamplight fade. Let the cool of the stone come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
