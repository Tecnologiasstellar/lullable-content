Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this. Whatever kind of day it has been. You can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to stand in a quiet gallery, at closing time, in front of a dark stone. And then we will go back, gently, to a lamplit study in Paris, two hundred years ago. A patient man is working out how old signs sound. Nobody hurried him. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

We begin in London, in a long stone gallery, near the end of the day.

The museum is closing. You can hear it happening. Voices thin out toward the doors. Somewhere a rope is clipped across an entrance. A trolley rolls away down a corridor and is gone. The air is cool, and still, and smells faintly of floor polish and old stone.

The gallery is wide and high. Its floor is pale and worn smooth. Along both sides stand carved figures from Egypt. Seated, standing, hands on knees. They have been waiting here a long time. They are good at it.

And near the middle of the room, in a glass case of its own, there is a slab of dark stone. It is not large. It stands about as high as your waist. Its face is flat, and dark grey, and covered from edge to edge with small carved writing. Three bands of it. One above the other.

The last visitors drift past and out. Their footsteps fade. And then it is only you, and the stone, and the soft evening light coming down from the high windows.

Come closer, and let your eyes rest on it.

The stone is granodiorite. A hard, dark, speckled rock, cooled slowly deep in the earth a very long time ago. It is about a hundred and twelve centimeters tall. That is a little under four feet. It is about seventy-six centimeters wide, and twenty-eight thick. It weighs roughly seven hundred and sixty kilograms. Three quarters of a ton of quiet.

Its face is dark grey. Look closely, and there is a faint vein of pink running across the top left corner. A different mineral, finding its way in, long before anyone carved anything.

The rock itself came from far up the river. Geologists have matched it to a small quarry at Gebel Tingar. That is on the west bank of the Nile, near Aswan, hundreds of miles to the south. The pink vein is part of how they knew. Granodiorite from that stretch of river often carries one.

Think of how such a rock is made. Molten stone, deep underground, cooling very slowly. Slow cooling grows large crystals. That is why the face has a faint sparkle to it, at the right angle. Pale feldspar. Glassy quartz. Small dark grains of mica.

So it was cut in the south, and floated north on a barge. Dressed, and ruled, and carved. Then stood up in a temple, in the sun.

The top is gone. It was broken away long ago, before anyone now alive had seen it. The break runs at a slant across the upper edge. Scholars think the stone once rose higher, with a rounded top. Up there, most likely, was a carved picture. A king standing before the gods, with a winged sun above him. That part is lost, and the stone does not seem to mind.

What is left is the writing. Small, careful, cut into the hard face with patient tools. The lines are perfectly straight. Somebody ruled them first, and then carved for weeks.

For most of its time in London, the stone did not look the way it does tonight.

Soon after it arrived, someone filled the carved letters with white chalk. It let a visitor read them from a step or two back. And the rest of the face was rubbed with wax. Carnauba wax, from the leaves of a palm tree. It was meant to protect the surface from a hundred years of curious fingers.

The wax darkened with time. The stone went nearly black. For a long while, books called it black basalt, and it was not. It was only wearing a coat.

In 1999 the conservators took the coat off. Slowly, with cotton swabs and soap and water, a small patch at a time. Under it the true surface came back. Dark grey, not black. With a soft sparkle of crystals in the light. And that pink vein, across the top left corner, which nobody had seen for two hundred years.

At the bottom left, they left a small square untouched. A window into how it used to look. Dark wax, white letters. You can find it, if you like, and then look back at the rest.

They found ink too, deep in the letters. Printer's ink. That is a story for a moment from now.

Now look at the three bands, one at a time, from the top down.

The top band is hieroglyphs. Little birds. Reeds. A seated figure. A hand, a mouth, a loop of rope. It is the oldest script of Egypt. Priests used it, on temple walls and in the tombs, for well over three thousand years. Fourteen lines of it survive here. The first lines are lost where the top broke away.

The middle band is Demotic. That is a Greek word meaning, roughly, of the people. It is the everyday handwriting of Egypt at the time. Quick, flowing, joined up. Used for letters and leases and receipts. Thirty-two lines of it.

Demotic grew out of hieroglyphs the way handwriting grows out of print. Over centuries the pictures were drawn faster, and simpler, and joined together. In the end a scribe with a reed pen could write a whole line without lifting it. By the time of the stone, a Demotic sign looked nothing like the bird it came from. But it was the same bird, underneath. Gone quick.

The bottom band is Greek. Plain, square, familiar letters. The language of the court and the government, because the kings of Egypt in these years were a Greek family. Fifty-four lines of it, a little worn at one corner.

Three scripts. One text. The same words, said three times, so that everyone who mattered could read them. That is the whole secret of the stone. Somebody, long ago, wrote a thing out in a language we still know. And beside it, the same thing, in one we had forgotten.

Let us think about what the words actually say. It is gentler than you might expect.

The year is 196 before the common era. The king is Ptolemy the Fifth. He is young. Still in his early teens. He has lately been crowned in the old city of Memphis, and the priests of Egypt have gathered there for the anniversary.

Picture Memphis for a moment. An old white city on the west bank, near where the delta begins. The temple of Ptah at its heart, with high walls and shaded courts. Priests arriving from up and down the river, in clean linen, in the spring. Meeting in a cool hall to agree on the wording, line by line. Nothing about it was quick.

They issue a decree. They list the good things the king has done for the temples. He has given grain, and money, and relief from taxes. He has repaired shrines. He has been generous to the sacred animals. So, they say, the priests will honor him in return. His statue will stand in every temple. His name will be spoken on feast days. His birthday will be kept.

And at the end comes the instruction that made everything else possible. This decree, they say, shall be carved on a slab of hard stone. In the sacred writing, and the writing of documents, and in Greek. And set up in every temple of the first, second and third rank, beside the statue of the king.

So there were many such stones, once. This is the one that came back to us. And the Greek band is the one that told us what all three said. Because Greek had never been lost. People had gone on reading it, all that time.

And the lines that broke off the top came back, slowly, from elsewhere.

In 1848 a scholar noticed something at the temple of Philae, far up the river. A version of the same decree was carved on a wall there. Later builders had cut across parts of it. Much of it was still there. Then, in the early 1880s, another copy turned up in the delta. A limestone slab, at a place called Nubayrah. Worn, and chipped, and readable.

Between them they carried the sentences our stone had lost. So the missing lines are not missing. They are only somewhere else, on another wall, in another stone, waiting to be laid alongside.

Now let us go, softly, to where it was found.

The Nile flows north, and near the sea it opens like a hand. The fingers are branches of the river, and between them is flat green land. Reeds, and fields, and slow water. At the mouth of the western branch stands the town of Rashid. Europeans called it Rosetta.

The delta is a wet, flat, green country. Rice fields. Date palms. The river here is wide and brown and slow, and the sea wind comes up it in the evening. Boats with tall triangular sails move on the water without hurry.

Near the town stood an old fort. In the summer of 1799, in July, soldiers were strengthening its walls. It was hot work. They took down an old section, to rebuild it stronger, and there in the rubble was a dark slab, covered in writing.

Somebody looked at it properly. A young officer, an engineer, whose name was Bouchard. He saw the three bands. He saw the Greek at the bottom, and knew it for Greek. He understood, at once, that this might be something. The stone was lifted out, carefully, and carried away from the wall.

It had been lying there, mortared into a fortress, for a very long time. Nobody knows quite how it came to be there. Very likely it was carried down the river as building stone, centuries earlier, from some temple that no longer stood. A useful flat block. A good face for a wall.

Even before it left Egypt, the stone had been printed. French scholars in Cairo inked its face, the way you would ink a printing plate. They laid damp paper over it, and pressed, and peeled it away. The letters came off pale on black, backwards, and clear. Those sheets went to Paris ahead of the stone itself. And a little of that ink is still down in the letters tonight.

The stone came to London in 1802.

It landed at Portsmouth in February of that year, and was taken up to the Society of Antiquaries in the city. Their rooms were at Somerset House then, beside the river. Scholars gathered round it. And then something patient and generous happened, which is worth sitting with tonight.

They made copies.

Four plaster casts were taken from its face. One went to Oxford. One to Cambridge. One to Edinburgh. One to Trinity College in Dublin. Then the Greek text was engraved, and good prints of it were sent to universities and libraries and academies right across Europe. The two Egyptian bands were printed too, and traveled the same roads.

Before the year was out, the stone went into the British Museum. It has stood there ever since. Almost every day since then, someone has stopped in front of it. It is the most visited thing in the building.

Think about what those copies did. The stone could only be in one place. But its writing could be in a hundred. On desks across Europe, and in a seaside town in Sussex. Under lamps, at night, in front of people who had never seen Egypt. The stone stayed still. The words went traveling.

The first thing the scholars noticed was the ovals.

Here and there in the hieroglyphs, a little group of signs is enclosed in a ring. A long loop with a flat bar at one end. The French soldiers had a word for it. They called it a cartouche, because it looked to them like a paper cartridge. The name stuck.

Look at the Greek band. The king's name, Ptolemy, appears in it several times. Now look up at the hieroglyphs. The little ring appears about as often. So the first careful guess was this. The ring holds the name of the king. And if it holds his name, then the signs inside it might be spelling out how his name sounds.

That was a big, quiet idea. Because most people, for a very long time, had believed that hieroglyphs were pictures of ideas. A sun for the sun. A bird for a bird. Mysteries, not letters. If the signs inside the ring spelled a sound, then the whole thing might be, at least partly, an alphabet.

The loop, by the way, is a rope. A length of rope tied in a circle, with the knot at the bottom. It means, more or less, all that the sun goes around. The king's name, inside the world.

An English doctor took that idea and worked it.

His name was Thomas Young. He was a physician in London, and a physicist, and he had a mind that wandered into everything. In the summer of 1814 he took a print of the stone with him to the seaside town of Worthing. He called it the amusement of a few leisure hours. The leisure hours became four years.

He worked from copies. He never needed the stone itself. He laid the Greek beside the Demotic, and the Demotic beside the hieroglyphs. He looked for shapes that came round again in the same places. Slowly he matched signs in the everyday script to signs in the sacred one. About eighty of them.

And he took the cartouche of Ptolemy apart, sign by sign. A square. A half circle. A lion. Two reeds. He gave each one a sound from the Greek name. Not all of his sounds were right. Enough of them were.

He noticed something else, and it was a good thing to notice. The everyday script was not simply an alphabet, either. Some of its marks were worn-down pictures, and some were sounds, mixed together. He wrote that down too. He did not quite follow where it led. That would take a different kind of reader.

In 1819 he set it all down in a long article for an encyclopedia, under the plain heading, Egypt. He gave sound values for thirteen signs. It was the first careful step, and he took it in a quiet room, by the sea.

Across the water, in France, another mind was moving toward the same place from a different direction.

Two patient men, working in two cities, each with a piece. That is the fair way to tell it. One of them started from the copies. The other started from the language itself.

Jean-François Champollion was born in 1790 in Figeac, a small stone town in the south of France. Narrow streets. Old houses with steep roofs. A river running through. His father sold books, and the house was full of them, in stacks, on stairs. So the boy grew up among paper. He was the youngest of the family. His older brother Jacques-Joseph, twelve years ahead of him, became the steady hand in his life.

In 1801, when he was ten, he went to live with that brother in Grenoble, in the mountains. Grenoble sits in a bowl of high country. In winter the snow comes down close to the roofs. The brothers shared a house, and a table, and a lamp.

And there the languages began. Latin and Greek first. Then Hebrew, and Arabic, and Syriac, and Persian, one after another, the way another child collects stones. At sixteen he stood up before the local academy in Grenoble and read a paper. Its argument was simple. The language of ancient Egypt was not lost. It had a living descendant.

That descendant was Coptic.

Coptic is the last form of the Egyptian language. It is Egyptian written in Greek letters, with a few extra signs for sounds that Greek did not have. After the old scripts were forgotten, the language itself went on. People spoke it in the villages for centuries. And when everyday speech moved on, the church kept it. In the Coptic churches of Egypt, the old words were still being sung.

Greek had no letter for the sound sh, or for a hard breath in the throat, or for a few others. So the Coptic scribes borrowed shapes from the old Demotic. Six or seven of them. They set them at the end of their alphabet. A small bridge, left standing, between the new writing and the old.

So the sounds of Egypt were not gone. They were only in a different dress. The sounds of a language are the last thing to go.

Champollion went to Paris as a young man and gave himself to it. He found a Coptic priest, and studied with him. He wrote to his brother that he was devoting himself entirely to Coptic. He wanted, he said, to know Egyptian as well as he knew French. He read Coptic in the mornings. He thought in it on walks. He made lists of its words, and its sounds, and the shapes of its grammar.

This was the key that the others did not have. When he finally looked at a sign and asked what sound it made, he had somewhere to check. A living language. The stone gave him the shapes. Coptic gave him the voice.

The next piece came from an obelisk, and a country house in Dorset.

An English traveler named William Bankes had been up the Nile. On the island of Philae, far to the south, he had found a small obelisk lying near the temple of Isis. It was pink granite, and it was covered in hieroglyphs. On its base was an inscription in Greek. He arranged to have it brought home. In time it stood on his lawn at Kingston Lacy, where it stands tonight, among the trees.

The Greek on the base named a king, Ptolemy, and a queen, Cleopatra. And in the hieroglyphs above there were two cartouches. Bankes made a small note in the margin of his copy, beside the second one. He guessed it was Cleopatra. In 1821 he had the whole thing drawn and printed, and sent the prints around.

A copy reached Champollion in January of 1822. Now he had two royal names in the old script, side by side. He laid them out on his desk.

Ptolemy. Cleopatra. Say them slowly, and listen for the sounds they share. There is a p in both. An l. An o. The signs for those three sat in the right places in both rings. A square for p. A lion for l. A little rope loop for o. They matched.

And the t was written with a different sign in each. A half circle in one. A hand in the other. He decided, calmly, that two signs could carry the same sound. As they can in any alphabet. He was right.

There was one more thing in Cleopatra's ring, at the end. An egg, and a half circle, after the last sound. They spelled nothing. They were only there to say, this is the name of a woman. He noted it, and set it aside for later. It would matter.

From two names, he now had about a dozen sound-signs he could trust.

And then, one September morning in 1822, the sun came in.

He had, until now, believed that the sound-signs were used only for foreign names. Greek kings. Roman emperors. Names that had to be spelled out, because they did not belong to the old language. For real Egyptian words, he still thought, the signs were pictures of ideas.

That morning, drawings arrived. An architect named Huyot had been far up the Nile, to the great rock temple at Abu Simbel. He had copied the cartouches there. These were old names. Egyptian names. Older than any Greek.

Champollion looked at one of them. Four signs. First, a sun disc. A plain round circle. In Coptic, the sun was re. Then a sign he did not yet know. Then two signs at the end, the same shape twice, and he knew those. They both made the sound s.

Re, something, s, s. He sat with it. In Coptic, the word for to give birth was mise. Ra, mes, es. Ramesses. The name of the great king, whose statues sit at the door of that very temple. And the sun sign was not spelling a sound at all. It was the sun, standing for its own name, with sound-signs around it.

So the script mixed both. Pictures that meant a thing, and signs that only made a noise, in the same word, side by side. That was the whole system, in four small marks.

He looked at another ring. An ibis, then the same middle sign, then an s. The ibis was the bird of the god Thoth. Thoth, mes. Thutmose. Another king. It held.

What he did next was very human, and very quiet.

He gathered up the papers and walked across Paris to the Institute, where his brother worked. He came into the office, and set the papers down on the desk. He said, in so many words, that he had it. And then the long weeks caught up with him all at once. He went home, and lay down, and rested for several days.

When he got up, he wrote it all out. He addressed it as a letter to Monsieur Dacier, the secretary of the Academy of Inscriptions, who had been kind to him. It ran to eight pages. On the twenty-seventh of September, 1822, he read it aloud to the Academy, in a crowded room. Thomas Young, who happened to be in Paris, was in that room. He sat and listened.

The letter did a modest thing. It set out an alphabet of the sound-signs, and showed how they spelled the names of Greek and Roman rulers. In it was a table, in columns. A Greek letter. The Demotic sign that matched it. The hieroglyph that matched them both. It hinted at the rest.

By late October it was printed as a small booklet. Forty-four pages, with four plates of drawings. Two years later he published the fuller account, and said plainly what he had found. Hieroglyphic writing, he wrote, is a complex system. Figurative, and symbolic, and phonetic, all at once.

Fourteen centuries of silence, and then an eight-page letter, read out on a September afternoon.

Let us slow down now, and look at how a sentence in hieroglyphs actually works. It is calmer than it looks.

There are, roughly, three kinds of sign. The first kind make sounds. Some make a single sound, like a letter. An owl is m. A quail chick is w. A mouth is r. A ripple of water is n. There are about two dozen of those. Others make two sounds at once, or three. The sign that Champollion found in Ramesses makes two, m and s, together.

The vowels are mostly not written. Egyptian, like Hebrew and Arabic, wrote the bones of a word and let the reader supply the breath. So when we say Ramesses, we are guessing at the vowels. The consonants are what the stone gives us.

The second kind of sign is a word in itself. A picture of the sun, meaning sun. A picture of a house, meaning house. Often it has a small upright stroke beside it, to say, read me as the thing I am.

And the third kind is the gentlest. It is called a determinative. It is a sign at the end of a word that is not spoken at all. It is only there to tell you what kind of word you have just read. A pair of walking legs means the word was about movement. A seated man means it was a person. A rolled scroll of papyrus means the word was an idea. Something you could write down, but not hold.

Take one small word. The Egyptian word for house was pr. Two sounds. You can write it with a picture of a house. A plain rectangle, with a gap for the door, and a small stroke beside it. House. Or you can spell it out. A square for p, and a mouth for r. Then the little house at the end, silent, to be sure. Both are right. A scribe chose whichever fitted the space.

So a word might be spelled out in sounds, and then finished with a small quiet picture, like a nod. Nothing in it is random. Every mark is doing a job. That is what it was. Now you know.

And there is the matter of which way to read.

Hieroglyphs can run from left to right, or from right to left. They can also be stacked in columns and read downward. The carver chose whatever suited the wall. Around a doorway, the two sides often face each other, so that the signs seem to turn toward the person coming in.

The rule for finding your way is simple. Look for a sign with a face. A bird, a person, an animal. Whichever way it is looking, that is where the line begins. The birds face the start. You read into their eyes. If the owl looks left, you begin at the left. If it looks right, you begin at the right.

There are about seven hundred signs in the classical script. Later there were more. But a scribe used the same few hundred over and over, the way a hand knows a keyboard. The birds face the beginning. The legs walk toward the end. The rope ring holds the king.

Once you know that, the wall stops being a mystery, and becomes a page.

Six years after the letter, Champollion finally went to Egypt.

He had spent his whole life reading it from copies. Now, in the summer of 1828, he sailed from Toulon with a small party of French and Italian scholars and artists. His friend Rosellini came with him. They landed on the Egyptian coast in August, and began, slowly, to go up the river.

For sixteen months they went from temple to temple. The boat moved by sail, and by the current. The banks slid past, green and then gold and then green. Palm groves. Mud villages. White birds standing in the shallows. The heat of the day, and the cool that came down the river at dusk.

And at each place they tied up, and walked to the walls, and he read them. That was the wonder of it. No one had read those walls for well over a thousand years. He stood in front of them in the low morning sun. He read the names of the kings, and what they had built, and which gods they thanked. Where earlier travelers had copied a sign wrongly, he noticed, and corrected it.

They went as far as the second cataract, where the river runs white over rocks, and then turned for home. On the way they stopped at Abu Simbel. Sand had drifted high against the great doorway. They dug a narrow way in and slid down it into the dark. Inside, the rock held the heat of the day. They worked by candlelight, a little at a time, and came out to breathe. The carved kings along the walls had waited three thousand years for someone to read their names. Now someone did.

He wrote long letters home to his brother, describing all of it. Every evening, by candlelight, on the boat. The river went on below him. The stars came out over the desert. He read, and wrote, and slept.

There was one more piece of patient work, and his brother finished it.

When Champollion came home, he began to set everything down. Not just the alphabet now, but the whole grammar. How a verb was built. How a sentence held together. How the sound-signs and the word-signs and the quiet determinatives fitted into a line. And beside the grammar, a dictionary. Sign by sign, word by word.

He worked at it for the rest of his days. And when his own part was done, his brother took it up. Jacques-Joseph gathered the pages, and saw them through the press. The Grammar came out in 1836. The Dictionary followed in 1841. Thick volumes, full of small drawn signs, each one with its sound beside it.

So the work began with a boy in Grenoble, saying that the language was not lost. And it ended with his brother's steady hand, turning the last page over. And every person since who has stood at a temple wall, and read it, has been standing on those two books.

Come back now to the gallery in London. It is later than it was.

The light from the high windows has gone from gold to grey. The seated figures along the walls have lost their edges. The glass case in front of you holds the stone. And a faint reflection of the room, and a faint reflection of you.

The case has a faint hum to it, if you listen. Air, moving very slowly, keeping the stone dry. It is the only sound in the room. Then, not the only one.

Somewhere at the far end, footsteps. Slow, even, unhurried. A guard, walking the last round of the evening. The sound comes closer, and passes behind you, and goes on. A door, somewhere, closes softly. Then the lamps along the walls begin to dim, one bank at a time, until only the case is lit.

The stone sits in its small pool of light. Three bands of writing. Fourteen lines. Thirty-two. Fifty-four. The little birds in the top band all look the same way, and that is where each line begins. You know that now. You did not, an hour ago.

Then the last lamp goes down too, and the stone is only a darker shape in the dark.

And the gallery sleeps.

The stone is cool, though no one is touching it. It has been cool for a very long time. Deep in the earth it was cooler still. It was warm once, in a temple court, under the Egyptian sun. It was warm again in a fortress wall, near the sea. Now it rests in a quiet room, in the north, and holds the temperature of the night.

Outside, in the courtyard, the tall columns of the front stand pale in the streetlight. A bus goes past on the road beyond the railings. Its sound comes through the walls as a hush, and is gone. London goes on, softly, around the stone.

The words on its face are not going anywhere. They were carved to last, and they have. A young king, and a room of priests, and a promise about statues and feast days. Said three times, so that no one would be left out. And a doctor by the sea, and a boy from Figeac. An obelisk on a lawn, and a sun sign in a ring. All of it holding still, in the dark, until morning.

Nothing here is in a hurry. It never was. The stone waited two thousand years to be read. And it was read by people who sat down, and looked, and looked again.

So let it be quiet now.

Let the museum settle around its stone. Let the guard finish his round and sit down. Let the Nile go on moving north in the dark, past the reeds, past the old fort, out to the sea. Let the obelisk stand on its lawn under the trees. Let a lamp in a Paris study burn low, and go out.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the signs, not the names, not the sounds of the birds and the reeds. It will all keep itself, as it has for two thousand years. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let your thoughts settle, like dust on a glass case in a dark room. Let the warmth of the lamplight fade. Let the cool of the stone come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
