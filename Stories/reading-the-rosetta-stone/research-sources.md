# Research sources — Reading the Rosetta Stone

Each line: URL — the fact(s) in the narration it supports. All checked by web search/fetch on 2026-09-14.

- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone — granodiorite; 112.3 cm high, 75.7 cm wide, 28.4 cm thick, about 760 kg; found mid-July 1799 at a fort near Rashid (Rosetta) while soldiers worked on its walls, noticed by Lieutenant Bouchard; the rounded top is lost and probably showed the king before the gods under a winged sun disc; 14 hieroglyphic, 32 Demotic and 54 Greek lines survive; landed at Portsmouth in February 1802; Society of Antiquaries made four plaster casts for Oxford, Cambridge, Edinburgh and Trinity College Dublin; in the British Museum by the end of 1802 and on display almost continuously since; the most visited object there; 1999 cleaning removed carnauba wax and white infill, revealing dark grey stone, crystalline sparkle and a pink vein across the top left corner.
- https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1179/sic.2000.45.Supplement-1.128 — the 1999 examination and conservation at the British Museum: wax coating from the early 1800s, white infill in the text, printer's ink from 1799 cleaned away with cotton swabs, soap and water; a small square at the bottom left left untouched to show the old surface.
- https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/everything-you-ever-wanted-know-about-rosetta-stone — the three scripts and their audiences (priests, everyday administration, Greek-speaking government); the missing top; on display since 1802.
- https://egypt-museum.com/the-rosetta-stone/ — found 15 July 1799 by soldiers working on a fort near Rashid in the Nile delta.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone_decree — decree issued by a council of priests at Memphis in 196 BC for Ptolemy V, marking the first anniversary of his coronation; written in hieroglyphs, Demotic and Greek; copies to be set up in temples of the first, second and third rank.
- https://www.attalus.org/egypt/rosettastone.html — the Greek text of the decree: the king's gifts of grain and money and tax relief to the temples, repair of shrines, care for sacred animals; statue in every temple; his birthday and feast days kept; the decree to be carved in sacred, native and Greek writing on hard stone.
- https://history.rcp.ac.uk/blog/thomas-young-and-decipherment-hieroglyphics — Young a physician and physicist; began in 1814 at Worthing working from a copy, "the amusement of a few leisure hours", then four years; identified cartouches as royal names and took the Ptolemy cartouche apart sign by sign; 1819 Britannica supplement "Egypt" with sound values for thirteen signs; sat beside Champollion at the 1822 reading.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decipherment_of_ancient_Egyptian_scripts — Society of Antiquaries engravings sent to institutions across Europe; Young correlated about 80 hieroglyphic signs with Demotic equivalents and noted Demotic mixed pictures and sounds; Bankes's obelisk copy reached Champollion in January 1822; Huyot's Abu Simbel copies on 14 September 1822: sun disc (Coptic re) + ms + ss = Ramesses; Lettre finished 22 September and read 27 September 1822 with Young present; Précis of 1824: "figurative, symbolic and phonetic all at once".
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Fran%C3%A7ois_Champollion — born 23 December 1790 in Figeac; moved to Grenoble on 27 March 1801 to live with his brother Jacques-Joseph; father a bookseller; studied Coptic with a Coptic priest in Paris and wrote to his brother that he was devoting himself entirely to Coptic, wanting to know Egyptian as well as French; on 14 September 1822 went to his brother's office, said he had it, then rested for days; the Ramesses and Thutmose cartouches; the 1828-29 expedition up the Nile; Grammaire egyptienne (1836) and Dictionnaire (1841) seen through the press by his brother.
- https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/social-sciences-and-law/archaeology-biographies/jean-francois-champollion — at sixteen he read a paper before the Grenoble Academy arguing that Coptic was the ancient language of Egypt.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lettre_%C3%A0_M._Dacier — read to the Academie des Inscriptions on 27 September 1822; Dacier was its secretary; eight-page draft; printed by Firmin-Didot in late October 1822, 44 pages with four plates; contained a comparative table of Greek, Demotic and hieroglyphic characters.
- https://www.nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/1257614 — the Philae obelisk: from the Temple of Isis on Philae, pink granite, about 9 m; Greek base inscription naming Ptolemy and Cleopatra; William John Bankes brought it to Kingston Lacy, Dorset, foundation stone laid 17 August 1827; Bankes's identification of Cleopatra; his 1821 drawings.
- https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/william-john-bankes/ — Bankes had lithographs of all four sides made and sent prints to Young and to France, from where copies reached Champollion; Champollion matched the Ptolemy and Cleopatra cartouches against the Greek names.
- https://sacred-texts.com/egy/trs/trs05.htm — Ptolemy and Cleopatra cartouches share the signs for p, l, o (and the reed for e/i); the t is written with a different sign in each (a hand in Cleopatra); Champollion concluded the two were homophones.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_hieroglyphs — reading direction: signs with faces look toward the beginning of the line, text runs left to right, right to left, or in columns; three sign types: phonograms (uniliteral, biliteral, triliteral), logograms usually marked with a vertical stroke, and unspoken determinatives at the end of a word (walking legs for motion, seated figures for persons, papyrus roll for abstract ideas); about 700-800 signs in the classical period, more later.
- https://www.principiaparva.co.uk/post/ten-minute-hieroglyphs-lesson-4-direction-of-writing-and-reading-words — read into the faces of birds and people; columns; the owl (m), quail chick (w), mouth (r), water ripple (n) as single-sound signs.
- https://www.historyskills.com/classroom/year-7/how-hieroglyphs-work/ — logograms, phonograms and determinatives explained; determinatives are placed at the end of words and carry no sound.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartouche — the word given by French soldiers of the 1798-1801 expedition because the oval looked like a gun cartridge; the shape is a rope loop (shen ring) with a bar at one end, enclosing royal names.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_alphabet — Coptic is Egyptian written in Greek-derived letters with six or seven extra letters borrowed from Demotic for sounds Greek lacked (sh, f, kh, h, j, g, ti); it survives as the liturgical language of the Coptic Church.
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franco-Tuscan_Expedition — the joint French and Tuscan expedition of 1828-29 led by Champollion and Rosellini; sailed from Toulon in July 1828, landed in August; sixteen months on the Nile as far as the second cataract; Abu Simbel entered through drifted sand and worked by candlelight; Champollion read and corrected inscriptions on the temple walls and wrote long letters to his brother.
- https://www.thecollector.com/jean-francois-champollion/ — Franco-Tuscan expedition dates (left Toulon 31 July 1828, landed 18 August 1828) and its sixteen months of travel from the delta to the border of Nubia; Champollion reading inscriptions no modern scholar had read and correcting earlier travelers' drawings.
