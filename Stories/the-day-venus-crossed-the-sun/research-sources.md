# Research sources — The Day Venus Crossed the Sun

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Venus — Venus's orbit tilted about 3.4 degrees to ours; nodes crossed in early June and early December; the 243-year cycle of 8, 105.5, 8, 121.5 years; transit dates 1631, 1639, 1761, 1769, 1874, 1882, 2004, 2012, 2117, 2125; a transit lasts about six hours; Halley's 1716 proposal; the black drop effect.

https://www.astronomy.ohio-state.edu/pogge.1/Ast161/Unit4/venussun.html — Halley's method and his call for timings good to two seconds; why 1761 disappointed (war, cloud, scattered stations); the black drop limiting accuracy to roughly ten seconds; Lalande's 1771 figure of 153 million km; modern AU of 149,597,870.691 km known to within 30 metres; the spread of 1769 stations (Siberia, South Africa, Newfoundland, Tahiti, Philippines, Norway, Hudson Bay).

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Halley_1716_proposal_of_determining_the_parallax_of_the_sun.jpg — Halley's 1716 paper "A new Method of determining the Parallax of the Sun, or his Distance from the Earth", Philosophical Transactions XXIX, 454–464.

https://www.royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1189 — Halley timed a transit of Mercury from St Helena in 1677 and argued from 1691 onward that Venus transits would serve better; in 1716 he named where the 1761 and 1769 transits should be observed, knowing he would be dead by then.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1769_transit_of_Venus_observed_from_Tahiti — Fort Venus on the sandy spit at Point Venus, begun two days after arrival; earthworks, palisade, casks of wet sand, guns, fifty-four tents, portable observatory with astronomical clock; Cook, Green, Solander and Banks observing; secondary parties on Eimeo (Moorea) under Gore and on the east coast under Hicks; "not a Cloud was to be seen the whole day and the Air was perfectly clear"; Cook's note of "an Atmosphere or dusky shade round the body of the Planet"; the observers differing more than could be expected; Hornsby's 93,726,900 miles against the modern 92,955,000 miles.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_voyage_of_James_Cook — Endeavour a converted collier about 106 feet long with roughly 94 aboard; departed Plymouth August 1768; across the Atlantic, round Cape Horn, Tahiti April 1769; transit observed 3 June 1769 at Point Venus; Charles Green astronomer, Joseph Banks botanist; Tupaia joined July 1769; the second, secret objective of a southern continent; New Zealand October 1769, Australia April 1770.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_Venus — Point Venus is a peninsula on the north coast of Tahiti at the north-east end of Matavai Bay, about 8 km east of Pape'ete; Cook anchored April 1769 and built Fort Venus at Te Auroa.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matavai_Bay — Wallis landed there 17 June 1767; Cook arrived 13 April 1769 and departed 13 July 1769; the Dolphin Bank coral shoal; a half-mile curve of black sand from Point Venus to Tahara'a (One Tree Hill); the Vaipopo'o river emptying behind the beach.

https://www.moanavoyages.com/en/blog/the-point-venus-and-its-lighthouse/ — Point Venus surrounded by a beach of black sand; Cook arrived aboard Endeavour to observe the transit planned for 3 June 1769.

https://www.whipplemuseum.cam.ac.uk/explore-whipple-collections/astronomy-and-empire/stolen-quadrant — the Bird astronomical quadrant went missing from the guarded tent on 2 May 1769 and was brought back to the fort the same evening with local help. (Used in the narration only as "it was borrowed, and by evening it was quietly back".)

http://www.ianridpath.com/stamps/cook.html — the transit telescopes were two-foot Gregorian reflectors by James Short magnifying about 140 times; Cook and Green used identical instruments and Solander a third of higher power; a Bird quadrant served for latitude and clock rate.

https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co1220/clock-used-for-transit-of-venus-observations-1769 — John Shelton of London made five astronomical regulator clocks for the 1769 transit; one went to Tahiti with Cook.

https://www.royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1184 — Shelton regulators beat seconds on a long pendulum and were the standard timekeeper for transit work; Howse and Murray place the Fort Venus clock in the National Museum of Scotland, Edinburgh.

https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03036758.2010.482971 — Green fixed the longitude of Point Venus by lunar distances on sixteen evenings between 30 April and 30 June, getting 149°36'38" W, and by eclipses of Jupiter's satellites on seven nights between 4 June and 6 July, getting 149°32'30" W.

https://www.rasc.ca/1769-transit — William Wales and Joseph Dymond sent by the Royal Society to Fort Prince of Wales, Churchill on Hudson Bay; they arrived in the summer of 1768 because of the ice and wintered over; a prefabricated wooden observatory designed by John Smeaton; a Bird quadrant and two Short Gregorian reflectors, one with a micrometer; good weather on the day; about fifty micrometer measures of Venus against the sun.

https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/education/maximilian-hell-northernmost-transit-venus-expedition-1769/ — Maximilian Hell went to Vardø in northern Norway at the invitation of Christian VII of Denmark-Norway, with János Sajnovics; he built a small wooden observatory and also a magnetic one, and successfully observed the transit.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilian_Hell — Hell and Sajnovics at Vardø, then part of Denmark-Norway; his results later vindicated by Simon Newcomb's study of his notebooks.

https://thonyc.wordpress.com/2012/04/08/and-then-he-missed-it-david-rittenhouse-and-the-transit-of-venus-1769/ — David Rittenhouse, clockmaker, observed the 1769 transit from his farm at Norriton outside Philadelphia, from a temporary observatory with a clock and instruments he built himself.

https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1769.0041/120164/ — the Norriton observation of 3 June 1769 reported by William Smith, John Lukens, David Rittenhouse and John Sellers for the American Philosophical Society.

https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2214132423000018 — Christian Mayer of Heidelberg observed the 1769 transit from the Imperial Academy observatory at St Petersburg; the wider Jesuit and academy networks of 1769 observers.

https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1765.0035/119927/ — Thomas Hornsby's pre-transit discourse on how the 1769 transit should be reduced; his later analysis gave a solar parallax of 8.78 arcseconds and a mean distance of 93,726,900 miles.

https://astro.ukho.gov.uk/nao/transit/V_1769/index.html — the 1769 results gave solar parallax values from about 8.43 to 8.80 arcseconds against the accepted 8.79; the global list of 1769 stations.

https://link.springer.com/article/10.1134/S0038094613060038 — Mikhail Lomonosov, observing the 1761 transit at St Petersburg, saw a hair-thin arc of light around the part of Venus still off the sun's disc and correctly inferred refraction in an atmosphere.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tupaia_(navigator) — Tupaia of Raiatea, arioi priest and navigator; met Banks during the 1769 transit visit; joined the Endeavour in July 1769 at Banks's request; drew a chart naming 74 of about 130 islands within a 2,000-mile radius; served as interpreter.

https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Endeavour_Journal_of_Sir_Joseph_Banks/July_1769 — Banks's journal on breadfruit brought down in loads, breadfruit planted on hillsides to keep the succession going when the flats failed, canoes in the river, and neighbours crowding to visit with gifts.

## Not verified / handled

- The exact clock times of first and last contact at Fort Venus (about 9:21 a.m. and about 3:32 p.m. local apparent time) could not be confirmed from a source I could reach. The narration says only "a little after nine in the morning" and "a little after half past three", which the six-hour transit duration and the clear-sky day support.
- The "canvas shade" over the observers is a reasonable inference from the fifty-four tents and the portable observatory rather than a sourced detail; it is described plainly and carries no factual claim.
