Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders drop, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to visit an English village, on a spring morning, more than nine hundred years ago. Some riders are coming to ask a few quiet questions. And then we will follow their answers to a warm room in winter, where one man writes them all down. Nobody hurried. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin on a green, early on an April morning, in the year 1086.

The village is small. A few dozen houses of timber and clay, with thatched roofs that come down low. Smoke rises from each one, straight up, because there is no wind yet. The air is cool and damp. It smells of woodsmoke, and wet earth, and the first cut grass of the year.

At one end of the green stands a church. Stone, and simple, with a square tower no taller than the elms. At the other end the land slopes down to a stream. You can hear it from here. A steady low rush, and under it a slower sound, a wooden creak that repeats. That is the mill wheel, turning.

Between the church and the stream lie the fields. They are long and open, with no hedges, striped by the plow into gentle ridges. The ridges catch the low light, and the furrows hold the shadow. It is the softest kind of countryside. Nothing in it is steep.

Twenty years ago a new king came from across the sea. That is long enough for the village to have grown used to it. This morning the cows are milked as they always were. The bread is baked as it always was.

§

Let us walk down toward the stream, slowly, past the houses.

Someone is leading a team of oxen out to the fields. Eight of them, in pairs, big and patient and slow. Their breath makes small clouds. Their hooves press soft prints into the mud of the lane. A plow follows behind them on a sledge, its iron share dull with last week's soil. This is the most important machine in the village. It moves at a walking pace.

The plow itself is worth a look, while it passes. At the front is an iron knife set upright, called the coulter. It cuts a clean vertical line down through the turf. Behind it, a flat iron share slices the same slice off horizontally. And behind that, a curved wooden board turns the slice over and lays it down.

That is why the fields are striped. The board turns the soil the same way every year, toward the middle of each strip. So the middle rises, slowly, over generations. And the edges sink. Ridge, and furrow, and ridge again. The shape of the ground here was made by patience, an inch at a time.

Nearer the water the air grows cooler. There is a smell of wet stone and weed. The mill stands where the stream has been guided into a narrow channel, so that it runs faster. The water strikes the paddles of the wheel and turns it. The wheel turns a shaft. The shaft turns a stone inside, against another stone. Grain goes in at the top. Flour comes out, warm, at the side.

Beside the mill, the stream widens into a still pool. That is the fishpond. Eels live in it, and the miller keeps traps of woven willow at the outlet. In the meadow beyond, the grass is already ankle high. It will be cut for hay in June and left to dry in the sun.

This is what the riders are coming to see. A mill, a pond, a meadow, some fields, and the people who work them. Nothing more than that.

§

They come at mid-morning, along the road from the market town.

There are perhaps a dozen of them, on horseback, riding at a walk. A few are men of rank, in good wool cloaks. The others are clerks, with satchels across their chests, and rolls of parchment tucked inside against the damp. Nobody is dressed for anything but travel. The horses are tired, and glad to stop.

These are the king's commissioners. At Christmas, in the city of Gloucester, the king held his court. He sat with his great men for several days and talked. The old record says they talked about the land. How it was settled, and by what sort of people. And out of that talk came the survey.

The kingdom was divided into circuits. About seven of them, each a group of neighboring counties. And for each circuit a small party of commissioners was chosen. Men who held little or no land in the counties they were sent to. That way they would have no reason to favor anyone. They have been riding since the turn of the year, county by county, hundred by hundred.

They dismount on the green. A boy takes the horses to drink. The commissioners stretch, and look around at the fields, and someone brings them ale. There is no ceremony. They have done this in a great many villages by now, and they will do it in a great many more.

§

The people they need are already waiting, near the church.

A county was divided into hundreds. A hundred was a group of villages that met together, in the open air. There they settled small matters, and swore to the truth of things. It is an old English way of doing business, older than the king, older than his father. The commissioners have simply borrowed it.

From each hundred, twelve men have been sworn. Half of them are English, and half are French. That was the rule, and it was kept. The idea is simple and rather lovely. If the two halves agree, the answer is probably right. They stand together in the churchyard, in the thin sun, not quite mixing yet.

And from each village, a few more. The priest, who can read a little and knows everyone. The reeve, who manages the fields for the lord and knows every strip of them. And six of the villagers themselves, chosen for their memories. Older men, mostly. Men who can say what was here before the new king came, because they were here too.

The commissioners also sit, later, in the county court. That is the larger meeting, for the whole shire, and the sheriff presides over it. There the hundreds bring their answers together. Anything doubtful can be raised there, and settled, and written down agreed. It is slow, and it is orderly, and it is mostly people waiting their turn on benches.

A clerk sets a small table on the grass and weights a sheet with a stone. The wind has come up a little. The sheet stirs at the corner. He unstoppers a horn of ink. And the commissioners begin to ask.

§

The questions are always the same. That is the whole secret of the thing.

The commissioners carry a list, and they ask it in the same order in every village in England. First, the name of the place. The reeve says it, and the clerk writes it down as best he can. Names were spelled by ear in those days. Sometimes the spelling on the sheet is the oldest record of that name there is.

Then, who held it in the time of King Edward. That is the question about the past. The old king, the one before the new king came. The jurors think back twenty years and more. A name is offered. Another juror nods. The clerk writes it.

Then, who holds it now. That is the question about the present. Often it is a different name, and often it is a French one. The jurors give it without comment. The clerk writes it down beside the other, and the two names sit together on the sheet. Then, and now.

Nobody argues. It is not that kind of morning. The commissioners are courteous, and the jurors are patient. The questions go on, one by one, like beads on a string.

§

The next question is about hides, and it needs a moment of gentle explanation.

A hide is not a field you can walk around. It is a way of saying how large a place is for the king's purposes. In the oldest sense, a hide was the land that would support one household. Over the centuries it had softened into a unit of account. A village might answer for five hides, or ten, or two and a half. That number was the one that mattered when the king asked for anything.

So the commissioners ask, how many hides. And the jury tells them, and the clerk writes it in. Five hides. It is the first number on the sheet.

The next number is plows. Not the wooden tools themselves but the teams. A full plow team was eight oxen. A village was measured by how many such teams it could keep busy. Some belonged to the lord, and worked his own land. Some belonged to the villagers, and worked theirs. The commissioners ask for both. Land for six plows, the jury says. Two teams on the lord's land. Four among the men.

Think about what those numbers really are. They are the sound of the oxen you heard in the lane, counted. They are the whole working strength of the place, set down in a line. And they were set down for every village in the kingdom. In the same words. In the same order. In a single year.

§

After the plows come the people. And here the language of the survey is worth a moment.

The commissioners do not ask for names. They ask for kinds, and for numbers. How many villagers, who held a good share of the fields and owed work to the lord in return. How many smallholders, with a cottage and a garden and a few strips. How many cottagers, with less than that. How many free men, who owed little to anyone. Everyone who worked the land had a place in the list somewhere.

The reeve counts them off on his fingers. Twelve villagers. Six smallholders. Three cottagers. One priest. He knows them all by name, of course. He has known most of them all their lives. But the survey does not need the names. It needs the count.

Then the land that is not plowed. How much meadow, which is measured in acres, because hay is precious and everyone knows where the meadow ends. How much woodland, which is often measured by pigs. Woodland for forty pigs, the jury says. That means a wood big enough to feed forty pigs on its acorns through the autumn. How much pasture, for the sheep and the cows.

The clerk's pen scratches steadily. Meadow, twenty acres. Woodland for forty pigs. Pasture for the village cattle. The sheet is filling from the top down.

§

Now the commissioners ask about the mill. And everyone on the green turns a little toward the sound of the water.

There are more than six thousand mills in the survey when it is finished. Nearly every village that has a stream has one. It grinds the grain for everyone, and the miller takes a share, and the lord takes a share of that. So a mill has a value, and the commissioners ask for it. One mill, the jury says, worth ten shillings a year.

Sometimes the value is not given in money at all. A mill on a good stream might render eels instead. A hundred eels a year, or two hundred, or a thousand, from the traps at the mill race. The clerk writes it down just as it comes. One mill, rendering a hundred and twenty eels. Nobody finds that strange. It is simply what the mill produces, and what the pond produces, and the two go together.

Fishponds are asked about too. So are churches, sometimes, though the list does not require them. If the village has a salt pan by the sea, or a vineyard, or a quarry, the jury says so. The clerk adds it. The list is fixed, but it has room at the bottom.

Across the kingdom, those extra lines add up to a portrait of how people lived. On the coasts there are salt houses, where seawater is led into shallow pans and boiled down over fires until only salt is left. The survey counts them carefully, county by county. It is the best record of that trade anywhere in Europe for its century.

There are fisheries on the big rivers, with weirs of stake and wattle. There are vineyards, more of them than you might expect in England, mostly on southern slopes and around abbeys. Someone, somewhere, said the words, and a clerk wrote them on a sheet, and they are still there.

The miller himself stands a little apart, with flour in the creases of his hands. He has heard his own mill valued now, out loud, in front of everyone. He seems neither pleased nor troubled by the figure. It is simply what the mill is worth.

The mill goes on turning while they talk about it. Creak, and rush. Creak, and rush. It does not know it is being counted.

§

The last questions are about worth, and they are asked three times over.

How much was the whole place worth in the time of King Edward. How much was it worth when the present holder received it. How much is it worth now. Three sums, in pounds and shillings. The jurors confer, quietly. The older ones remember. Six pounds then, they say. Four pounds when the new lord came. Six pounds now.

And is more to be had, the commissioners ask. Meaning, could this place yield more than it does. The jury considers the meadow, and the mill, and the plows. No, they say. It is about right. The clerk writes that down as well.

That is the end of the list. The whole village is now on a single sheet. Its name. Its two holders. Five hides. Land for six plows. Twelve villagers, six smallholders, three cottagers. A mill, a pond, a meadow, a wood. Three sums of money. It took perhaps an hour.

The clerk sands the ink and rolls the sheet with the others. The commissioners thank the jury, and the jury thanks the commissioners. The horses are brought back from the stream. By early afternoon the riders are gone, up the road toward the next hundred, and the green is quiet again.

They will keep this up all spring and into summer. By the first of August, the king is holding a court at Salisbury, and the survey is essentially done. Every county. Every hundred. In well under a year.

§

Now let the seasons turn, and come with me to Winchester.

It is winter, and it is evening. Winchester is the old capital, and the royal treasury is here, in a strong stone building. The rolled sheets from all the circuits have come in over the summer and autumn. County by county, in bundles tied with cord. They fill a room. They are far too much for anyone to use.

So someone has been given the task of making them into one book.

Climb a stair, and open a heavy door. The room beyond is small, and it is warm. An iron brazier stands in the middle of the floor, full of charcoal, glowing dull red. The heat comes off it in slow waves. The walls are dark oak paneling, gone almost black with age and smoke. There is one window, shuttered against the cold, with a little gray light at the edges.

At a sloping desk by the brazier sits a man in a plain wool gown. He has a candle on either side of him. He has a stack of the rolled sheets on his left and a stack of fresh parchment on his right. And he is writing.

We do not know his name. We know his handwriting better than we know almost anyone's from that century. Because, as far as anyone can tell, nearly the whole of the great book is in his hand alone.

§

Let us look at what he is writing on, because it was once walking in a field.

The pages are parchment, and parchment is sheepskin. The skin is soaked for days in a bath of lime, until the wool loosens and comes away. It is rinsed, and stretched tight on a wooden frame, and pegged all round. Then, while it dries, it is scraped with a curved blade. Again and again. Thinner and thinner, until it is smooth and pale and even. Then it is rubbed with pumice, and cut to size.

A book this large needs the skins of many hundreds of sheep. Somewhere between five hundred and a thousand of them, laid page by page. When it is finished it will run to eight hundred and eighty-eight leaves, close-written on both sides.

Before he writes, each page is ruled. A row of tiny holes is pricked down each margin with a point. Then a dry stylus is drawn across from hole to hole, so that faint lines are scored into the skin. Two columns to a page. The pen will follow those lines, and the lines will keep it straight.

Pick up a sheet, in your mind. It is slightly stiff. It is cool at first, then it takes the warmth of your hand. One side is a little smoother than the other. It smells faintly of lime, and of the animal it was, and of nothing else.

§

The pen is a goose feather. The ink is made from a tree.

The quill is a big flight feather from a goose's wing, cured in hot sand until it is hard. The end is cut on a slant with a small knife, and a slit is made in the tip. That slit holds a little ink by itself, and lets it down onto the page in a steady line. The slanted cut gives the letters their shape, thick on the down stroke and thin on the up. Every so often the tip wears soft, and the scribe pares it back, and goes on.

The black ink is called iron gall. It begins with oak galls, the round hard growths that a wasp makes on an oak twig. They are crushed and soaked, and they give a brownish liquid full of tannin. To that is added iron, in the form of a salt, and a little gum to bind it. It goes on the page pale, and darkens as it dries, and bites a little way into the skin. That is why it lasts. The words are not sitting on the surface. They are in it.

And then there is the red. For every heading, every county name, every landholder's title, the scribe changes pens and dips in a second pot. In the great book the red is made from vermilion, a bright mineral pigment, ground fine. He uses it to write the names of counties at the head of each page. He uses it to underline the name of every manor, so a reader's eye can find it in the column.

Black for the facts, red for the way in. Under the candles the red glows a little, like the brazier. Under daylight it is the color of rose hips.

§

Now watch him write, because he has a way of doing it that is all his own.

The sheets from the circuits are long and full. The book must be shorter. So he condenses as he goes. He writes in Latin, and he abbreviates nearly everything. A stroke above a word means letters have been left out. A little hook means a whole ending. Villagers becomes a few letters and a mark. Plows becomes a few letters and a mark. What took the clerk on the green a full line takes him half of one.

He is fast, and steady, and he does not decorate. There are no pictures in this book. No gold, no tangled initials, no beasts in the margins. Just column after column of small brown letters, marching down the page, with the red names standing out like signposts.

He is arranging, too, not just copying. The circuit sheets came in by hundred and village. He sets them out instead by county. Within each county, by landholder. The king first, then the bishops, then the great lords. Each one has all their manors gathered under their name in red. So anyone who wants to know what a certain lord holds in a certain county can open the book. And look. And find it.

Now and then he leaves a gap. A line is short, or a corner of a column is blank, because an answer had not come in yet. Later he goes back and fills some of them, in a slightly smaller hand, a little squeezed. You can still see those places. They are the only sign in the whole book that anyone was ever waiting for anything.

There are about two million words in the finished work. Almost all of them passed through this one hand. When he was done, a second scribe went through and checked it, and made small corrections in the margins. Two people. One winter. The whole of England.

§

Let us read one entry over his shoulder, as it goes down.

It is the village we visited. The name comes first, underlined in red. Then, in the compressed brown Latin, something like this. The lord holds this manor. So-and-so held it in the time of King Edward. Then, it answered for five hides. There is land for six plows. In lordship, two plows. Twelve villagers and six smallholders have four plows. There is a mill, worth ten shillings. Meadow, twenty acres. Woodland for forty pigs. It was worth six pounds. Now, six pounds.

That is all. Eight or nine lines, in a column, among thousands of others like it. But look at what it holds. The stream and the wheel are in it. The oxen you heard in the lane are in it, twice over. The meadow with its ankle-high grass is in it. The reeve counting on his fingers is in it. And the twelve men in the churchyard. And the clerk's stone on the corner of the sheet.

And it is in the book still. If you know the village, you can find its page, and read those same lines, in the same ink, tonight.

§

There is a second book, and it is worth a moment, because it is fuller and stranger.

Three counties in the east of England, Essex and Norfolk and Suffolk, were never condensed. Their returns were copied out much as they came, by at least six different hands, and bound together on their own. This is Little Domesday. Little in its page size, not in its length. It is in fact the thicker of the two.

Where the great book gives the plows and the people and stops, the little book keeps going. It counts the livestock on the lord's land. So many sheep. So many pigs. So many goats and cows and horses and beehives. It records the value at three dates, as the questions asked. It names free men in their dozens. It was probably written first, before the great scribe began. It shows what the raw survey looked like before he smoothed it.

And one of the working drafts survives as well, which is a small miracle. It covers the five counties of the south west. Cornwall, Devon, Dorset, Somerset and Wiltshire. It was written by many hands, and it is untidy in places, and it has lists and sums and notes in it that never reached the great book. It counts the livestock too. It has been at Exeter Cathedral for at least six hundred years, and it is there now.

Read it beside the great book and you can watch the work happening. Here is the raw material, in a dozen hands. And here is what one scribe made of it, in a single winter, in a single style.

So there are two textures in the record. One is the great scribe's, tight and even, every village cut to the same shape. The other is a chorus of voices, less tidy, with the sheep and the beehives still in it. The great book is the answer. The little book is a glimpse of the counting itself.

§

Sit with the size of it, gently, for a moment.

There are a little over thirteen thousand four hundred places named in the two books. Villages, hamlets, towns, single farms. Nearly every settlement in the kingdom, from the south coast to the river Tees. More than six thousand mills, each one turning on its own stream. Many thousands of plow teams. Meadows by the acre and woods by the pig.

Where the animals were counted, the sheep outnumber everything else by a long way. Tens upon tens of thousands of them, on the marshes and the chalk downs. Then pigs, in the woods. Then cattle, and goats, and the small working horses called cobs. It is a very woolly kingdom, on the evidence.

A monk, writing the yearly chronicle in English at the time, marveled at how closely it had been done. He wrote that the land was searched so thoroughly that not a single hide was left out. Not a yard of land. Not an ox, nor a cow, nor a pig, that was not set down. He may have found it a little much. But he was not wrong about the thoroughness.

And think of the arithmetic of the year. From a Christmas court to a finished survey by the following August. Riders on every road in England. Juries in every hundred. Sheets by the thousand, carried in satchels to Winchester. And one man by a brazier, all winter, writing it down.

Nothing quite like it had been made before. Nothing like it would be made again for centuries. It was done with oxen, and horses, and goose feathers, and the memories of old men standing in churchyards.

§

The book did not have its famous name at first.

In Winchester they called it, simply, the Book of Winchester. Or the King's Book. Or the Book of the Treasury, because that is where it was kept. With the coin and the tallies, behind a strong door.

The other name came a century later. Around the year 1179, a treasurer of the king wrote a small handbook on how the treasury worked. He wrote it as a conversation between a master and a pupil. And in it he explained what the English called this book, and why. They called it Domesday. The day of judgment. Not because it was fearsome, but because its word was final. When the book said who held a place, and what it was worth, there was no appeal against it. Like the last judgment, it could not be set aside.

The name stuck, as good names do. And so a book of oxen and mills and meadows came to carry the grandest word in the language.

§

Let us follow the book to where it rests now, because it has traveled slowly, and it is safe.

For a long time it stayed at Winchester, in the treasury. Then the treasury moved to Westminster, beside the river Thames, and the book went with it. There it lived for centuries, in one chamber of the exchequer or another. Kept in a chest. Taken out when someone needed to settle what was held where. It was consulted, reign after reign. Its word was still good.

In the middle of the nineteenth century it went to a new home. The Public Record Office, built to keep the nation's papers safe. And today it is at the National Archives at Kew, on the western edge of London. It sits in a room where the temperature does not change and the air is kept dry. In the dark. Brought out only rarely.

It has been rebound at least six times in its life. The most recent time was for its nine hundredth year. Conservators found that an earlier binding had been too tight, and had creased the parchment. So they took the two books apart, gently, and rebound them as five. The great book in two volumes. The little book in three. Tawed pigskin over oak boards, so the pages could open flat and rest.

And it is still read. Not only by historians. A handful of times in living memory, a question about a mill or a piece of common land has gone to court. Someone has opened the survey of 1086, and read what the jury said. And the court has listened. Since 2006 the whole of it has been online, page by page. You could find your own village tonight, if it is old enough. But there is no need.

§

Come back to the warm room now, in Winchester, in the winter of the writing.

The charcoal in the brazier has settled. It gives a small dry tick as it shifts, and a few sparks lift, and go out. The candles have burned down to stubs, and their light has gone soft and low. The shutters hold the cold outside. The room smells of charcoal, and parchment, and the faint sharp smell of the ink.

The scribe finishes a line. He looks at it. He wipes the quill on a scrap of cloth and lays it in the groove of the desk. He puts the stopper in the ink. He rubs his eyes with the heels of his hands, the way people always have.

On the floor, in the straw laid down against the cold, something moves. A mouse. It comes out from under the desk, into the ring of candlelight, and sits up, and cleans its whiskers. It is not afraid. There has been no noise in this room all evening but a pen. Then it turns and goes back into the straw, and the straw rustles once, and is still.

The scribe watches it go. He pinches out one candle, and then the other. The brazier is the only light left, a low red glow on the dark oak. He goes down the stair to his supper, and the door closes softly behind him.

§

So let it be quiet now.

Let the room hold its warmth, with nobody in it. Let the charcoal settle, and settle, and go gray. Let the stacked sheets from all the circuits wait in the dark for morning. Let the half-finished page lie on the desk. The red names glowing faintly. The ink of the last line drying into the skin.

Far away, in the village, let the mill wheel turn in the dark. Let the eels move slowly in the cold pond. Let the oxen stand in their byre, breathing. Let the twelve men and the priest and the reeve be asleep in their beds. The questions all answered. Nothing more to be asked of anyone.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the hides, not the plows, not the names, not a single number from the whole quiet evening. It has all been written down. It has kept itself for nine hundred years, without any help from you. It will keep tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, like ash in a brazier at the end of the day. Let the warmth of the candle fade. Let the dark of the oak come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
