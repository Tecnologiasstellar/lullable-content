Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever the day has held, you can set it down now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink, away from your ears. Let the bed take your whole weight. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing slow, all on its own, the way it does when nothing is expected of you. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to stand inside a building made almost entirely of glass. It stood in a park in London for one summer, more than a hundred and seventy years ago. We will be there early, before anyone arrives. The light will be coming in, and the sparrows waking in the roof.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin in Hyde Park, in the spring of 1851, a little before sunrise.

The park is wide and gray and quiet. There is mist lying low over the grass. It moves a little when the air moves, and then settles again. The trees stand in it up to their knees. To the north, out of sight, the long lake called the Serpentine lies flat and pale. Somewhere near it a duck says something, once, and is quiet.

The air is cool and damp. It smells of wet grass, and of earth, and faintly of coal smoke from the city beyond the trees. Your breath shows a little in front of you. Then it is gone.

And along the southern edge of the park, there is a shape that should not be there.

It is very long. Longer than anything else in sight. It lies along the ground like something that has settled there for the night. And it is made of glass. All of it. From this distance it looks like a low bank of mist. One that has decided to hold still, and take a shape, and stay.

Eighteen hundred and forty-eight feet from end to end. More than a third of a mile. Something over four hundred feet wide. In the middle, the long body is crossed by a shorter one. There the roof lifts up into a great round arch. Like a held breath.

As the sky pales, the whole length of it begins to catch the light. Faintly at first. Then more. It does not shine. It glows, softly, from the inside, the way frost does.

§

Let us walk toward it, over the wet grass.

Up close it is not one thing but thousands of small things. Slim iron columns, standing in rows. Between them, panes of glass, pane after pane after pane, set in thin white bars. Every pane the same size. Every column the same distance from the next. It is like a piece of music written on a very long page.

Look closely at one of the columns. It is not bare iron. It is painted, and the painting was argued over for months.

The man who chose the colors was named Owen Jones. He had spent years measuring old buildings in Spain, and Egypt, and Greece. Under the dust he had found something surprising. The ancient world painted its architecture, and painted it in strong plain colors.

So he did the same here, by a rule. Blue on the surfaces that fall away from you. Yellow on the edges that come toward you. Red overhead, on the flat undersides. And thin lines of white between them, to keep each color to itself.

People said it would be dreadful. The newspapers said so at length, for weeks. Then the building opened, and it was not dreadful at all. Close up, you see the colors plainly. From a hundred feet away they mix. The whole great web of iron softens to a warm gray, with daylight coming through it.

Run your hand down a column now. The paint is cool, and smooth, and faintly gritty. The iron under it is colder still.

There is a door in the side, small against all that glass. It is not locked yet, or it has been opened for us. Step through.

And the air changes. It is warmer in here, by a few degrees. It smells of new-sawn boards, and damp earth, and something green. The floor is wood, and it creaks a little, and gives a little. Between the boards there are narrow gaps, on purpose. Dust can be swept straight through them, and fall to the ground below.

Look up. The roof is glass, and beyond the glass is the whole gray-and-gold sky. The light comes down through it evenly, from everywhere at once, so that nothing casts a hard shadow. It is the light of a bright morning under a thin cloud. It is the kindest light there is.

And ahead of you, in the middle of the building, stand full-grown elm trees.

§

They are not in pots. They are not brought in. They were here before the building was, growing in the park, and the building was raised up and over them.

When the plans were first drawn, the trees were going to be cut down. People in London did not like that idea, and said so. So the design was changed. The middle part of the building, the transept, was given a roof that curves up in a great barrel arch. High enough to hold an elm with room to spare. The trees were simply enclosed, and the glass closed over them, and they went on being trees.

They stand there now in the morning light with their leaves just out, small and new and bright. Their bark is dark and rough and cool to the touch. There is grass around their roots, and a bed of ferns. A little way off, under the same arch, a row of palms in tubs stretch their fronds toward the roof.

There were ten of them, or near enough, in the way of the plans. English elms, the tall kind that used to stand along every hedgerow in the country. An elm grows straight and goes up a long way before it spreads. That is exactly the wrong shape for a low building. It is why the roof had to rise.

The arch above them is seventy-two feet across, and a hundred and sixty-eight feet to the top of the curve. There is room in there for the highest branch and a good deal of sky besides.

You can hear the trees, if you listen. Not much. A dry stirring, high up, where the air moves between the glass and the leaves. That is all.

And it is an odd and lovely thing, to stand under a tree and be indoors. To see the sky through leaves, and then through glass. To feel the warmth of a room, and smell a wood.

§

The man who thought of this building was a gardener.

His name was Joseph Paxton. He was head gardener at Chatsworth, a great house in Derbyshire, in the north of England. He had been there since he was in his early twenties. He knew soil, and glass, and how to keep a plant alive in a cold country.

A few years before, in 1849, he had been given a seed of a water lily from South America. An enormous one. It was called Victoria regia then, and Victoria amazonica now. In the wild, its leaves lie flat on the river like round green trays, six feet across, and more. Nobody in England had managed to make it flower.

Paxton built it a house of its own. Glass, and a tank of warm water. Boilers kept the water at the heat of an Amazon backwater. A little wheel turned in the tank, all day and all night, so the water moved and did not go stale. The way a slow river moves.

He was copying a place he had never been. A brown warm river on the other side of the world, made again in Derbyshire, out of pipes and coal.

And the lily grew. Leaf after leaf, each one wider than the last. The leaves came up rolled, like a fist, and opened on the surface. In November of that year, it flowered. The first in the country. The flower opens white in the evening, and by the second night it has turned pink.

To show how strong the leaves were, he stood his small daughter on one. Annie, nine years old, in a fairy costume, on a floating leaf. The leaf held. The newspapers drew a picture of it, and everyone in England smiled.

§

Now turn one of those leaves over, in your mind, and look at the underside.

It is not flat and soft. It is ribbed. Thick ribs run out from the center like the spokes of a wheel. Thinner ribs cross between them. It is a web, a framework, with the thin green skin stretched over it. That is why a leaf so large and so thin can hold up a child.

Paxton looked at that underside for a long time. And he thought about roofs.

A year later, in the summer of 1850, London was looking for a building. There was to be a great exhibition of things made all over the world. Hundreds of designs had been sent in. None of them was right. They were all brick, and stone, and slow, and dear. And the opening was less than a year away.

Paxton was at a meeting of a railway company in Derby, on a June day. His mind was not entirely on the railway. He took a sheet of pink blotting paper and drew on it. A long low shape. A flat roof made of many small peaks, like the ribs of the leaf. Columns in rows. That was all. It took him a few minutes.

Nine days later he had a full set of drawings. The pink blotting paper still exists. It is in a museum, and it looks like what it is. A gardener's doodle, at a dull meeting, that turned into the largest glass building the world had seen.

§

Here is how it was made, because the making is the quiet heart of it.

Almost nothing was made in Hyde Park. The building was made in pieces, in foundries and workshops far away, and brought to the park and put together. Like a kit. The same few shapes, over and over.

The columns are hollow iron. The beams between them are iron too, cast in a lattice, light and stiff. Each one is the same length. Twenty-four feet. So every part fits every other part. The whole building is really the same small room, repeated a few thousand times.

The firm that built it was called Fox and Henderson. They did the sums, and cast the iron, and put it up. Some four thousand tons of iron in all. Timber for the floor and the glazing bars, a great deal of it. And the glass.

The first column went into the ground in September of 1850. The building opened on the first day of May, 1851. Nine months, from a hole in the park to a finished palace of glass. Nobody had built at that speed before. There were horses, and ropes, and pulleys, and tall timber shear legs, and a great many men in the cold.

§

The glass came from Birmingham.

It was made by a firm called Chance Brothers, at their works in Smethwick, on the edge of the city. They made it by blowing. A worker took a gather of molten glass on the end of a long iron pipe. He blew it into a bubble, and swung it, and blew again, until it became a long cylinder. Then the ends were cut off. The cylinder was slit along its length. Then it was laid in a warm oven, where it slowly opened out and lay flat.

Every pane was the same size. Forty-nine inches long, ten inches wide. Long and narrow, like a plank of light. About as thick as a coin. And they made close to three hundred thousand of them. Two hundred and ninety-three thousand panes, packed in straw, sent down to London on the railway.

Because every pane was the same, the building could be scaled around the pane. The columns were spaced so that the glass fitted between them without cutting. Think of that for a moment. The building was not designed and then glazed. It was designed to fit its glass.

And they went in fast. A team of eighty men could set eighteen thousand panes in a week. They worked in the rain and the winter cold, up on the roof. And the way they did it is worth knowing.

§

Look up again, at the roof, and follow the shape of it.

It is not flat. It runs in long low ridges, like a field that has been plowed. Ridge, and furrow, and ridge, and furrow, from one end of the building to the other. Paxton had used this shape on his glasshouses for years. It lets the low morning sun and the low evening sun both come in at an angle. And it sheds rain.

At the bottom of every furrow there is a gutter. A long channel of wood, with a groove cut in it. Rain runs down the glass into the groove, and along the groove to the nearest column. And the columns are hollow. So the water goes down inside the column, into a drain under the floor, and away. The building drinks the rain and passes it on, quietly, through its own bones.

Now here is the clever part. While the roof was being built, those same gutters were used as rails. The glaziers sat in little wheeled trolleys. Each trolley ran along a pair of gutters. The man sat in it, with his panes beside him, and his putty. He fitted the glass in front of him. Then he pushed the trolley along, and did the next. He worked under a canvas hood, so the rain did not wet the putty.

The putty he works with is linseed oil and powdered chalk, kneaded soft. In cold weather a glazier keeps his lump of it warm inside his coat. It smells of oil and dust. He lays a bed of it along the bar, sets the pane down into it, and presses with the flat of his thumb. Then he trims the edge away with a knife, in one long curl, and drops the curl in a tin.

One man, in a small cart, on a roof of glass. Setting a pane, and rolling a little further, and setting another. Miles of it. The gutters that would carry the rain for years carried him first.

§

Walk now toward the middle of the building, where the long nave and the transept cross.

There is a sound ahead of you, growing as you go. Water. Not a rush. A soft steady fall, like rain on a still pond.

In the very center, under the arch, in front of the elms, stands a fountain made of glass. Cut glass, clear as ice. It rises in tiers. A broad basin, and a narrower one above it, and a slender stem, and a crown. Twenty-seven feet high. Four tons of crystal. It was made by a firm called Osler, also from Birmingham, who made chandeliers.

In the early light it is barely there. You see the water more than the glass. The water goes up from the top and opens out and falls. Sheet by sheet, from tier to tier, into the basin. Each drop takes a little of the light with it. And the sound fills the crossing and goes no further.

It was made by casting the glass in thick blocks, and then cutting the faces by hand on a wheel. Every cut face throws the light a different way. That is the whole trick of cut glass. It is not that it shines. It is that it has been given hundreds of small flat surfaces, and no two of them agree.

The stem in the middle is solid glass, and it carries the weight of everything above it. Nobody was quite sure it would stand up until it did.

It stands there because people needed a place to meet. In a building a third of a mile long, you say, I will see you at the fountain. And everybody knows where that is.

You can stand beside it as long as you like. The mist from it is cool on your face.

§

Now let us walk down the long nave, and look at what has been brought here.

It is more than a hundred thousand things. From about fourteen thousand people and firms, half of them from Britain and its territories, half from other countries. The nave runs the whole length. On either side are courts, open rooms without ceilings, each one belonging to a country or a kind of thing. Above, on both sides, there are galleries, where you can walk and look down.

Nothing is switched on. That is what the morning gives you.

In the machinery court the steam engines stand still, dark and oiled and enormous. Their wheels are at rest. Their brass is dull in the low light. There is a smell of oil, and cold iron, and the coal that has not been lit. Later they will turn. For now they are only shapes.

There are looms with cloth half woven on them. There is a printing press, with a page still in it. During the summer it will print a newspaper here, in front of whoever cares to watch, and the sheets will come off damp and be hung up to dry. There are locomotives, standing on short lengths of rail that go nowhere. A machine that folds envelopes. Pumps. Lathes. A great many clocks, all ticking, none of them quite agreeing.

Walk on, and the iron gives way to softer things. Bolts of cloth. Silk from Lyon, in colors that seem to hold light inside them. Lace. Shawls. Carpets rolled and unrolled. Porcelain from the works at Sèvres, painted with birds. Cut glass. Carved wood. A pair of tall doors faced in green malachite, from Russia, veined like a leaf.

And a court made to look like a room from the Middle Ages. A carved stone chimneypiece. Stained glass. Candlesticks, and wallpaper of gold and red.

§

Let us slow down in one of the quiet corners.

There are telescopes here, on their stands, their brass tubes pointed at the roof. There are microscopes. There are barometers, and globes, and instruments for measuring things whose names you do not need tonight. They are laid out on green cloth. Each one has a small card beside it.

Somebody made every one of these. Somebody sat at a bench for months, filing a brass thread until it turned smoothly. Somebody ground a lens by hand and held it to the window and ground it a little more. All of that care is here, lying still, on green cloth, in a glass house in a park.

From the far countries come the things that are hardest to imagine. Fine wool from Australia. Furs. Grains in glass jars. Ivory carvings, and lacquer boxes, and paper. A reaping machine from America, waiting for a field. Mineral samples, each in its little labeled tray. A stuffed elephant, wearing a carved howdah, borrowed for the summer from a small country museum.

Each court was given its space by agreement, months ago, on paper. Britain and its territories took the western half. The eastern half was shared among some thirty-odd other countries. Every crate that arrived had a label, and a place waiting for it, and a person whose whole job that summer was to know where things went.

None of it is asking to be looked at. It is only here, and the light comes down on it, and that is enough.

§

Listen. There is another sound, above the fountain, above the ticking of the clocks.

Sparrows.

They live in the roof. They moved in while it was being built, when the roof was open to the sky. When the glass closed over them, they simply stayed. They nest in the angles of the iron. They come and go through the open slats in the walls. Nobody invited them. Nobody could think how to ask them to leave.

There is a story about that, and it is a gentle one. The Queen was worried about the birds, and what they might do to the exhibits. She mentioned it to the Duke of Wellington. He was very old by then, and a great deal wiser than he let on. He thought about it and said, sparrowhawks, ma'am. That was his whole advice. Whether any sparrowhawks were ever brought, nobody seems to know. The sparrows, at any rate, stayed.

And in the early morning they are all awake. Their small quick voices come down from the roof, from every direction. It is the sound of a hedge, in a building. The chirping does not echo, because the glass is too thin, and the building is too long. It just goes on, softly, everywhere.

They have it better than any sparrows in London. There is no wind in here, and no frost, and no cat that can climb glass. There are crumbs under ten thousand feet every afternoon. There is water falling in the middle of the building, all day, for nothing.

One of them comes down to the fountain to drink. It stands on the rim of the glass basin, and dips, and lifts its head, and dips again. Then it is gone, up into the light.

§

The building is not only glass and iron. It also knows how to breathe.

Along the walls, at the bottom and at the top, there are long slats. Louvers. They can be opened by turning a wheel, which pulls on a cord, which tilts them all at once. When the sun is high, the warm air rises and goes out through the top ones. Cooler air comes in through the bottom ones, and up through the gaps in the floor. The whole building becomes a slow chimney.

And over the roof, on the outside, there is canvas. Long strips of unbleached cotton, stretched across the glass. They take the hardest part of the sun and soften it. That is why the light in here is so kind. On the hottest days, men will go up and wet the canvas with water. The wind will dry it. And the drying pulls heat out of the building.

Paxton learned all this from keeping plants alive. A glasshouse that cooks its plants is no use to anybody. So this building is really a glasshouse for people, with the same care taken. The louvers, the canvas, the gaps in the floor. Nothing here is grand for its own sake. Everything is doing a job.

§

In a few hours, the doors will open, and people will come.

Six million visits, by the time the summer is over. In a country of about twenty million people. They will come in every kind of coat and every kind of shoe. Some for the day, and some who have saved for a year.

At the start, the tickets were dear. But after a few weeks, on four days out of the six, the price came down to one shilling. The shilling days. That was what most working families could manage, once. And they came. Mill hands, and farm workers, and shop girls, and whole villages together in their Sunday clothes.

They came by train. A man named Thomas Cook had run outings for a temperance society. Now he put together excursion trains from the north and the Midlands. Cheap tickets, up and back in a day, or two days with a bed. He brought something like a hundred and fifty thousand people to London that summer. Many of them had never seen the sea, or a city, or a train.

The trains themselves were a new kind of thing. Bare carriages, packed benches, a fare a working man could find. Cook printed his own little paper to advertise them. He hardly slept at home that whole summer, moving parties up and down the country.

Picture one of those families now. A father, a mother, three children. They have been up since four. They have eaten bread and cheese on a train. They are walking across the park in the morning, toward the long glass thing they have read about. Not quite believing it. In a few hours they will be standing under the elms. That is all. That is the whole story of the day.

§

And when they are tired of walking, there is tea.

At the far ends of the building, and in the middle, there are refreshment courts. They are run by a firm called Schweppes, who make soda water. There is no beer here, and no wine. That was decided early. There is tea, and coffee, and cocoa, and lemonade, and ginger beer, and Schweppes' own bubbling water, cold. There are ices. There are sandwiches, and Bath buns, sweet and heavy, sprinkled with sugar. Over the summer they will sell more than a million bottles of soft drinks, and nearly as many buns.

The buns come out of baskets, still a little warm from somewhere. The tea is poured from big urns, into thick white cups. People carry them to long tables, and sit, and take off their hats, and let their feet rest. The sound in the refreshment court is a soft roar of talk, and spoons, and cups set down.

And there is one more thing here, which people will still be talking about long after. Toilets. Proper ones, with water, for men and for women, the first that most people in the country had ever seen. They were built by a plumber named George Jennings. To use one cost a penny. For that you got a clean seat, and a towel, and a comb. Eight hundred and twenty-seven thousand people paid their penny that summer. And that is why, in England, going to the lavatory is still called spending a penny.

§

The name came before the building did.

Before a single column had gone up, a magazine called Punch printed a piece about the plans. Punch was fond of a joke. The writer was a man called Douglas Jerrold. He had been at Chatsworth, they say, and seen the little girl on the lily leaf. In his piece he called the thing that was coming a palace of very crystal.

Crystal Palace. The words were meant lightly. They stuck at once. By the time the first column went up, everybody in London was calling it that. Nobody has called it anything else since. Even the men who built it used the name. It is a good name. It says what the thing is, and it says how it feels.

The exhibition ran from the first of May until the middle of October. Then it closed, and the things were packed, and sent home.

The money left over was a great deal. One hundred and eighty-six thousand pounds. The people in charge of the exhibition used it to buy land. Just to the south of Hyde Park, in a place called South Kensington. They meant it for learning. And over the years, on that land, museums grew. A museum of art and design. A museum of natural history, with dinosaurs in it. A museum of science, with the steam engines. A round concert hall with a domed roof. Colleges. All of it grown from one summer under glass.

And the building itself was not thrown away. It was unbolted, pane by pane, and column by column, the way it had been put up. It was carried across the river to a hill in south London, at a place called Sydenham. There it was built again, larger, with gardens, and lakes, and fountains taller than the trees. It opened in June of 1854. People came to it for generations, for concerts and flower shows, and to walk in the park around it. The gardens there had fountains fed from two great water towers, and stone models of extinct animals standing among the trees. Some of those animals are still there, on their islands, looking out over the water.

The elms in Hyde Park stayed where they were. The building went up around them, and then went away again, and they went on being trees.

§

But we are not there yet. We are still in the early morning of one day, in the summer of 1851, before anyone has arrived.

Somebody has come in through a side door. A gardener, in a long apron, with a watering can in each hand. He walks up the nave without hurrying. His boots are soft on the boards. He goes to the ferns under the elms, and tips the cans. The water goes into the soil with a low dark sound. Then he goes back for more.

He has a rag over his shoulder. Now and then he stops and rubs a mark off a pane at head height, without much thinking about it.

He does this every morning. Nobody sees him do it. The trees do not need him, really. Their roots go down under the floor into the park, where the rain still reaches. But the ferns do, and the palms in their tubs do. So he waters them, and looks up into the elm leaves for a while, and goes away.

And as he goes, the light changes. A cloud has come over. The gold goes out of the roof, and it turns to pearl.

Then the first drops.

§

Rain, on a roof of glass.

It begins as single taps, far apart. Here. And then there. Then closer together. Then a soft steady hush, all along the length of the building. Over every one of those hundreds of thousands of panes. It is the softest sound a rain can make. The glass is so thin, and the roof is so wide. Not a drumming. A whisper.

Look up, and you can see it. Each pane goes dim and silver and streaked. Water gathers in the furrows, and runs, and finds the gutters, and runs along them toward the columns. And then, down. Inside the iron. Under the floor. Away. You can hear a faint gurgle, if you put your ear to a column. The building is doing what it was made to do, without anyone asking.

Thirty miles of gutter, all of it filling at once. Every drop that lands anywhere on this roof has somewhere to go, and knows the way, and is already going.

The sparrows go quiet. The fountain goes on falling, and its sound and the rain's sound become one sound. The elms stand in the gray light with their new leaves, dry, under the roof.

And you stand there with them. Warm. Dry. Under acres of glass and a whole sky of rain.

§

Let it be quiet now.

Let the rain go on, softly, over the roof. Let the gutters carry it. Let the fountain fall into its basin. Let the engines stand dark and still. Let the clocks tick to themselves, and the silk lie folded in the dim light. Let the sparrows sleep a little longer in the iron.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the numbers, not the names, not a single pane of glass. It will all keep itself. The trees will go on being trees. The rain will go on finding its way down. Nothing here needs you to watch it.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let your thoughts settle, like mist on the grass in a park before sunrise. Let the painted iron and the pale glass fade. Let the sound of the rain go on without you.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
