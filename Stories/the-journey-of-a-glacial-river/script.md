Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is over now. It is done with. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw loosen. Let your breathing find its own slower pace. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are going to follow water.

One long journey, in one direction only. It begins as a snowflake, high on a mountain, and it ends in the sea. In between it will be ice for a century, and then a river, and it will only ever go downhill.

There are no decisions on this journey. Water never has to choose. It simply goes the one way it can go, at whatever pace it is given.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

We begin very high up, on a wide field of snow, on a windless day.

It is utterly quiet here. Snow does that. It swallows sound. Even your own breathing seems wrapped in cloth.

The field tilts gently away in every direction, white on white, folding into the shoulders of the peaks. The sky is a deep, clean blue. It is the kind you only get at altitude, where the air is thin and has nothing in it.

It is snowing, very lightly. No wind. The flakes are coming down in that wandering, sauntering way they have when nothing is pushing them.

Watch one flake. It settles on the field, among trillions of others, and that is the whole event. A single soft arrival, adding the weight of almost nothing.

That flake is our traveller. Tonight we will follow it all the way down to the salt water.

It will take a while. A century or two, in fact. But we have all night, and the journey is not in a hurry, and neither are we.

The first stage of the journey is simply burial, and it is gentler than it sounds.

Snow keeps falling. This year's snow lands on last year's. Next year's will land on this. Our flake is covered over, season by season, a few feet at a time. It sinks. Not by moving, but by being built upon.

And as it is buried, it changes.

A fresh snowflake is mostly air. All those famous six-pointed arms hold each other apart, and the snow lies light as a duvet. But under the weight of the years above, the arms break down. The flakes shrink into rounded grains. The grains pack closer, like sugar settling in a jar.

Snow that has survived a full year of this has a name of its own. It is called firn. Old snow. Halfway to something else.

The firn keeps packing. The air between the grains is squeezed into smaller and smaller pockets. The grains press against each other and begin, very slowly, to fuse. It is the way ice cubes left together in a bowl will freeze into one lump.

And then, sixty metres down, decades after our flake landed, the last passages between the air pockets close.

It is ice now. Deep, dense, clear ice, made of pressed snow. Our flake has no shape anymore. It is one grain in a body of ice the size of a valley.

Two things about that ice are worth pausing over, because they are quietly wonderful.

The first is the colour.

Down here, the ice is blue. Not painted blue, not sky-reflected blue. Blue in itself — a deep, glowing, glassy blue, the colour of the inside of a wave.

The reason is what the ice does to light. Ice swallows the red end of light, just slightly, a little more with every metre the light travels through it. Fresh snow scatters light back out before any of it travels far, so snow stays white. But this ice is dense and clear, and light goes a long way into it.

And the deeper the light goes, the more red it loses. What comes glimmering back out is only the blue that survived.

The blue of a glacier is simply what light looks like after a long walk through ice.

The second wonderful thing is the air.

Those little pockets of air that were sealed off when the passages closed — they are still there. Millions upon millions of tiny bubbles, locked into the ice. Each one holds a breath of the sky, from the year the ice closed around it.

The air of a morning two hundred years ago. The air of a snowfall before your great-great-grandparents were born. Sealed, and held, and carried along.

Scientists drill into old ice precisely for these bubbles. Each one is a tiny sample of an old sky. A core of ice is a library of atmospheres, stacked in order, going down and back through the centuries.

Our flake carries its own small breath of its own old sky. It will hold it almost to the sea.

Now the whole body of ice begins to move, and this is the part that changes everything.

Ice this thick does not simply sit. Under its own tremendous weight, deep down, it softens. Not melting. Just yielding, the way old candle wax yields on a warm day.

And because the valley tilts, the whole mass leans downhill. And flows.

It is a river. A river of ice. It has a current, faster in the middle, slower at the edges where it drags against the rock. It bends around spurs of mountain the way water bends around boulders.

But its pace is its own. A few centimetres a day, perhaps. A brisk glacier might manage a metre. Our flake, locked deep in the body of it, rides downhill at the speed a garden grows.

If you stood beside it, nothing would ever seem to happen. Come back in twenty years, and the boulder that sat on the ice by your feet has moved. It has travelled down the valley, like a parcel on the slowest conveyor there is.

The glacier is a river that a lifetime is too short to watch.

People proved that flow with an experiment so simple it is almost a joke, and it is worth telling.

Two centuries ago, nobody was sure glaciers moved at all. Mountain farmers said they did. They had watched, across whole lifetimes, and they knew. The learned men wanted measurements.

So one summer, on a great glacier in the Alps, a scientist drove a line of stakes across the ice. Dead straight, bank to bank, sighted against fixed marks on the rock walls.

Then he went away, and let the winter and the summer pass, and came back.

The line was no longer straight. It had bowed. Every stake had travelled down the valley. The stakes in the middle had travelled farthest. The line across the ice now hung in a long, smooth curve, like a slack rope.

Which is exactly what a current does. The middle of a river runs fastest. The edges drag.

One row of sticks, one year of patience, and the question was settled. The ice was not a frozen field. It was a river, with a current, moving too slowly for eyes but not for stakes.

Much of what we know about the slow world was learned exactly this way. Put a mark down. Wait honestly. Come back.

As it goes, the ice works.

It does not slide over the valley so much as wear it. Patiently, the way a hand wears the varnish off a banister.

The underside of the glacier has picked up stones, and grit, and sand. All of it is held firmly in the ice, like tools in a hand. And with them, century after century, it grinds the rock beneath.

It smooths whole valley floors. It rounds off ridges. It polishes granite until the bedrock gleams. The valleys it leaves behind are wide and round-bottomed, unmistakable forever after as places where ice has been.

And all that grinding produces something important for our story. Powder.

Rock ground against rock makes a flour. A dust of stone so fine that it floats in water, and takes days to settle. It is called, plainly and nicely, rock flour.

The glacier makes it constantly, tonne after tonne, in the dark under its own weight. Remember the flour. In a little while, it is going to paint everything we see.

Not everything the glacier carries is dust. Some of its luggage is enormous.

Boulders fall onto the ice from the peaks above, or are plucked whole from the valley sides. And the glacier accepts them all without comment. A boulder the size of a cottage rides the ice as easily as a pebble. It travels at the same unhurried pace, for as long as the journey lasts.

When the ice finally melts from underneath its cargo, the cargo is simply set down. Wherever that happens to be.

That is why you sometimes find a single vast boulder sitting alone in a field. Gentle green farmland, far from any mountain. And one great grey stranger in the middle of it. The wrong stone, in the wrong place — granite resting on chalk, sitting where no cliff could ever have dropped it.

Geologists call them erratics. The wanderers. Some rode the ice a hundred miles or more, in an age when the glaciers reached far out over the lowlands. They were left behind when the ice drew back to the peaks.

Farmers plough around them, century after century. Children climb them. Whole villages use one as a landmark, and a meeting place, and mostly nobody asks how it got there.

It got there at a few centimetres a day, first class, on the ice.

The old glaciers left one more parting gift, and whole countries are made of it.

When the great ice sheets drew back, they did not leave tidily. Here and there, vast blocks of ice broke off and were left behind. Stranded, and part-buried in the gravel the melting ice was dumping everywhere.

Each block took decades to melt, or centuries. And when it finally went, it left its own shape behind. A round, deep hollow, pressed into the land. Like the dent left in a cushion.

The hollows filled with rain, and became ponds. Round, quiet, tree-ringed ponds, with no river flowing in and none flowing out.

There are regions of the north absolutely freckled with them. Finland's lakes number in the hundreds of thousands. A whole American state calls itself the land of ten thousand lakes, and is being modest.

Nearly every one is a memory of a single block of stranded ice. Each holds rainwater in the dent it made, ten thousand years after the melting.

People fish on them, and skate on them in winter, and grow up beside them. The ice age is not really gone. It is dimpled into the map, everywhere, full of perch.

Time passes. A great deal of it. Decades of slow descent, our flake riding deep in the blue, going the only way there is.

And gradually, the world outside the ice is changing. The glacier is coming down out of the high cold, metre by metre, year by year, into warmer air.

On the surface, in summer, the ice now melts. Threads of bright water appear, and run, and join. Little streams braid across the glacier's back, running in channels of blue ice, clean and quick and shining.

Where the crevices open, the streams pour down into the body of the glacier. They find their way along inside it, hollowing smooth-walled tunnels of blue. They join each other in the dark, gathering.

The deep ice is warming toward its ending. Our flake, after its century of stillness, is close to moving again.

Down at the foot of the glacier, all that gathered water is about to come out.

Before we leave the ice, one small introduction, because even a glacier has residents.

On the snowfields of the north Pacific coast, there live ice worms. Real worms, thin as thread, dark as pencil lead, about the length of a fingernail.

They live in the glacier. Actually in it — in the ice itself, moving between the crystals, in ways nobody has fully worked out.

They cannot stand warmth. A few degrees above freezing is far too hot for them. So they keep to the ice, where the temperature suits them perfectly, and never changes.

By day they stay down in the glacier, away from the sun. And then, on summer evenings, they rise. As the light goes off the snow, they come up through it in their millions. They graze on the algae that tints old snowfields pink.

A whole grazing herd, each animal smaller than an eyelash. They drift up through the surface of a glacier at dusk. They sink back down before morning.

The mountains keep some very quiet livestock.

The foot of a glacier is called the snout, and it is a strange and wonderful place.

The ice ends in a ragged wall, grey and white and blue, streaked with the rock it has carried. The wall stands in its own cold breath. Even in high summer, a chill pours off it, like the open door of a cellar.

And from beneath it, out of a low arched cave of blue ice, the river is born.

Not a trickle. A river, whole and busy from its first metre. It rushes out of the dark under the ice as though it had been a river all along, and merely indoors.

Somewhere in that rush is our flake. Melted at last. Water again, for the first time in perhaps two hundred years, out in the daylight, moving fast.

And with it goes its little bubble of old air, dissolving into the morning. A breath of an old sky, two centuries stale, finally exhaled.

The water is bitterly cold, a degree or two above freezing. And it is not clear. It is pale. A milky, cloudy, grey-green pale. It is like water into which someone has stirred a spoonful of the finest possible ash.

That is the rock flour. The river is carrying the mountain's dust to the sea. And it is about to show us what that dust can do.

There is a small wooden hut beside the river here, just below the snout, and it deserves a visit.

It is a gauging station. Inside, halfway down a stilled well of river water, a float rises and falls with the stream. A pen rises and falls with the float. A slow drum turns a strip of paper past the pen.

The river writes its own diary here. Every flood and every quiet spell, drawn as a wavering line, day and night, in all weathers.

In the mountains, huts like this have been keeping their records for a century and more. Somebody visits every so often, on skis in winter. They change the paper, and check the clockwork, and note the readings in a book.

Whole shelves of those books exist now. The river's moods, hour by hour, going back past living memory. You can look up what this water was doing on a spring night eighty years ago.

The glacier got its row of stakes. The river got a pen. The slow world keeps excellent diaries, wherever anyone has troubled to leave it writing materials.

For its first miles, the young river is all energy and no manners.

It pours across a wide plain of gravel that the glacier left behind, and it cannot decide on a course. So it takes several at once.

It splits into channels, and the channels split again, and rejoin, and cross, weaving over the gravel in silver braids. From the ridge above, it looks exactly like unravelled rope — a river plaited loosely across a mile-wide bed of stones.

None of the braids is permanent. A channel silts up in a week and the water shrugs sideways into a new one. The whole pattern rearranges itself continuously, all summer, like slow weather.

This is what a river looks like before it has settled down. Too much gravel, too much energy, and no single way forward yet. It will grow out of it, a little further down.

The sound up here is wonderful. Not one roar but thousands of small ones. Water on stones, everywhere, all across the plain.

Then the valley narrows, and the river gathers itself into one channel at last, and pours into a lake.

And the lake is turquoise.

Not poetically turquoise. Actually, arrestingly turquoise. A pale, milky, glowing blue-green that looks frankly invented. It is ringed by dark pines and grey mountains, which make it look even less probable.

It is the rock flour doing it.

The finest of the flour never settles. It hangs in the water, billions of particles in every bucketful. And each particle is the right size to do one thing. Scatter light.

Sunlight comes down into the lake. The water itself quietly absorbs the reds and oranges, as water always does. And the hanging flour scatters what remains — the greens and the blues — straight back up and out.

So the lake glows. It gives back green-blue light from within itself, like something lit from below.

Every turquoise mountain lake on every postcard is this. Stone, ground to dust by a glacier, hanging in meltwater, sorting sunlight. The colour of the mountains being slowly carried away.

The lake does one more thing. It calms everyone down.

Water arrives at the top end milky and cold and hurried. And then it simply has to wait. A lake is a waiting room. The current spreads, and slows, and stops being a current at all.

In the stillness, the heavier silt sinks. Season by season it lays itself down on the lake floor, in fine, even sheets. A pale layer for summer's grinding. A dark thin layer for the quiet under winter ice. Year after year, like pages.

Our flake's water drifts through over some weeks, resting, going nowhere in particular, until it finds the far end.

And the river that leaves the lake is a different creature from the one that entered. Clearer. Steadier. Bluer and greener and calmer, its flour left behind on the lake bed, its hurry gone.

It slips out over a sill of smooth rock, and drops into the trees.

The river spends the middle of its life in the forest, and these are its easiest miles.

Pines stand close on both banks, and the air smells of resin and cold water. The river runs clear now, over stones it long ago rounded, past gravel bars where herons stand and think.

Smaller streams come down out of the side valleys and join. Each adds its own water, and its own slight taste of its own hills. The river widens, and deepens, and quietens.

Where it was once all rapids, now it alternates. A quick reach, then a long green pool. A riffle over gravel, then a slow bend where the surface goes glassy and the pines stand upside down in it.

Trout hang in the current here, facing upstream, letting the river bring them their meals.

And on a wet boulder mid-stream there is a small round bird, plain brown, doing knee-bends. It bobs, and bobs, and then does something no other songbird in the world does. It walks into the river.

Under the surface, wings half-open against the current, it strolls along the stream bed. It is calm as a hen in a yard, picking insect larvae from between the stones. Then it pops back up onto its boulder, dry as a cork, and bobs some more.

It is called a dipper. It owns this stretch. And it will sing right through the winter, over the noise of the water, when every other bird has gone quiet.

And the water that was ice within living memory is now warm enough for wading. In the shallows, at least, on a summer afternoon. Barely. The river remembers the glacier just enough to make them shriek.

By its last miles, the river has grown broad and unhurried, and it meanders.

It winds across its floodplain in wide, lazy loops, doubling back on itself. It is in no more hurry than the cattle drinking at its margins. Willows lean over it. The mountains that made it are far behind now. A blue saw-edge on the horizon, small enough to cover with your thumb.

The water is smooth and full. It carries the sky on its back — clouds sliding slowly along the surface, upstream or down, whichever way the wind prefers.

And in winter, up in the high valley we have left behind, the young river does something lovely. It goes quiet.

The frost comes, and the river's edges stiffen first. Lids of ice grow out from the banks, thin and glassy, a little further each night. The braided channels shrink under white roofs.

But the river does not stop. Under the ice, it keeps going, all winter, smaller and slower, fed by springs and by the glacier's held reserves.

If you walk up that valley in January and stand still, you can hear it. A soft, hollow, musical trickling, coming up through the snow from underneath. The river, indoors for the season, murmuring along under its own ceiling.

The whole valley sounds like that on a still winter day. Water, out of sight, keeping itself company.

And then, one evening, down here in the lowlands, the water begins to taste of salt.

The tide pushes gently up into the river's mouth, twice a day. The river leans gently back. In that long soft negotiation, the journey ends. The last of the silt drops into the brackish water, and settles. It adds its little share to the mudflats. Land is being built here, grain by grain, at the river's own pace.

The flats the river has built are busy at this hour, in a soft-spoken way. Curlews walk the shining mud on their long careful legs, probing for their suppers, calling that low bubbling call that carries for a mile over flat water.

A few seals have hauled out on a sandbar to rest, heavy and content, like luggage nobody is coming back for. The tide will lift them off it later. They know, and do not care.

Our flake slips over the bar with the ebb tide, into the grey-green swell.

The sea takes it in without ceremony. It has been expecting it, one way or another, for two hundred years.

And that is not quite the end, because this journey does not have an end. It has a circle.

The sea is patient, but the sun works on it every day, lifting water off the surface, molecule by molecule, invisibly.

Some molecule that was once our flake rises on a warm afternoon, into the air, into the making of a cloud.

The cloud drifts inland. It meets the mountains, and the mountains push it upward. The air cools. The water in it gathers itself around specks of dust.

And on some quiet, windless day, high on a wide white field, it snows.

A flake comes down in that wandering, sauntering way. It settles among trillions of others. It adds the weight of almost nothing.

And the whole long story starts again. The burial, the blue, the slow river of ice, the milky birth, the braids, the turquoise lake, the pines, the salt.

It has been running, this circle, for as long as there have been mountains and rain. It is running tonight, in the dark, in ten thousand valleys at once.

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the firn, or the bubbles of old sky, or the flour that paints the lakes. Not the braided silver channels, or the herons on the gravel bars.

It is all going on anyway, whether anyone follows it or not. Snow is settling on high fields right now, beginning journeys that will end in the sea two centuries from tonight. Long after all of us have stopped keeping track.

Let the words go. Let the picture soften, and slide downstream, and out of sight.

There is nothing out there that needs you tonight. There is nothing here that does either.

Only water, going the one way it can go, at whatever pace it is given.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
