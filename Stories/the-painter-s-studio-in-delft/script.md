Good evening.

Wherever you are as you listen to this, whatever kind of day it has been, you can let it come to an end now. It is over. It asks nothing more of you. Let your shoulders sink down, away from your ears. Let the surface beneath you take your weight completely. There is nothing left for you to carry tonight.

Let your hands grow soft. Let your breathing slow, all on its own. There is nothing here to watch for. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will need to remember in the morning.

Tonight we are going to climb a staircase, very slowly, in a small Dutch town of canals. At the top is an upstairs room, with the light coming in from the left. A painter is working there, on one canvas, at his own pace. He made only two or three pictures a year. Nobody hurried him. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

We begin on the water, south of the town, on an evening in the 1660s.

The town is Delft, in the low wet country of Holland. A canal called the Schie brings you toward it from the direction of Rotterdam. The water is flat and brown and slow. Reeds stand along the banks. A barge moves ahead of you, pulled by a horse on the towpath. It hardly seems to move at all.

The air is cool and damp. It smells of river mud, and of peat smoke from a thousand chimneys. Ahead, the water widens into a small triangular harbor. People here call it the Kolk. Boats lie moored along its edge, with their masts tilted a little, and their ropes slack.

Beyond the harbor is the town wall, low and red, and two gates in it. One is the Schiedam gate, with a small clock on it. The other is the Rotterdam gate, with two pointed roofs. And behind them, rising above every rooftop, are two church towers. One is tall, and its top still catches the sun. One is dark brick, and it leans a little, and it has leaned that way for hundreds of years.

The rest of the town is going blue. Let us go in.

Inside the walls, the town is made of water and brick.

Canals run down the middle of the streets. Trees lean over them. Small bridges cross them, arched so a barge can slide beneath. The oldest canal is called the Oude Delft, and the town took its name from it. The word means something like a ditch that was dug. That is all a town is here. A place where people dug the water into order.

The houses stand shoulder to shoulder. Tall and narrow, with stepped gables against the sky. Their bricks are the color of dried clay. Their windows are small panes of glass, set in strips of lead. Behind the glass, here and there, a candle has been lit.

At the center is the market square. It is long and wide, and paved with stone. At one end stands the town hall. At the other stands the New Church, the one with the tall tower. Its tower was finished in 1496, so it is not so very new.

On the square, in one of these houses, there was once an inn called Mechelen. A boy named Johannes grew up in it. His father kept the inn, and dealt a little in pictures on the side. Merchants came in and out. Painters came in and out. The boy learned to look.

The other church is a short walk away, beside the oldest canal. It is called the Old Church, and its brick tower leans about two meters out of true. People say it was built over a filled-in canal. It has been leaning for so long that no one worries about it anymore. It simply stands there, patient, a little tilted, keeping the time.

Now walk with me to the Oude Langendijk. It is a street just off the square, beside another canal.

Johannes married a young woman named Catharina in 1653. That same year he joined the painters' guild of Delft, the Guild of Saint Luke. From then on he was a master painter. He could sign his work and sell it. He was twenty-one years old.

Before long, he and Catharina were living here, in her mother's house. Her mother's name was Maria Thins. It was a good, solid house, and there was room in it for the whole family. And there was room in it for a studio.

The front door is heavy. The hall inside is cool, and the floor is tiled, and it smells faintly of beeswax and woodsmoke. Somewhere at the back, a kettle is warm. Voices come and go, low and ordinary.

The stairs are steep and narrow, the way Dutch stairs are. Each step is worn a little in the middle. Take them slowly. Rest your hand on the rail. Up, and turn, and up again.

At the top there is a door, and it is standing open. The room beyond is full of soft gray light. This is the studio. Let us go in quietly. He does not mind.

The room is not large. About four meters wide, and a little over six meters deep, and the ceiling is three meters up.

The walls are brick, and they have been whitewashed, so they hold the light and give it back gently. The windows run along one wall, and they face north. That is the direction painters have always wanted. North light does not change much through the day. It never turns gold or orange. It stays cool and even. A color mixed under it in the morning looks the same at four o'clock.

Each window is a grid of small panes, held in strips of lead. Some panes are clear and some are faintly green. The light that comes through them is a little softened, a little silvered. Shutters can be closed, one by one. Curtains can be drawn part way. He arranges the light the way another man might arrange furniture.

Look at his pictures, and the light almost always comes from the left. A window at the left edge. A wall running back into shadow. That is this room. Or a room very like it, seen again and again, over twenty years.

On the floor, in so many of those pictures, there are black and white marble tiles. Squares, set on the diagonal. But here is a quiet thing. This house probably never had them. Marble like that belonged to grander houses than this. What lay under his easel was more likely plain fired tile. He painted the marble in. The grid on the floor helped him build the room in perspective. And it was beautiful, which is reason enough.

On the wall hangs a map.

It is a large printed map of Holland, on paper, mounted on cloth, with wooden rods top and bottom. The paper has yellowed a little. There are tiny ships drawn on the sea. It appears in several of his paintings. Behind a woman reading a letter. Behind a soldier and a laughing girl. The same map, in the same room, for years.

There is a chair with lion heads carved on the finials. A table with a thick woven carpet thrown over it, red and blue and cream. A blue cloth. A few picture frames, painted black. These are the things the room had, and he used them again and again. He did not need new things. He needed the same things, looked at longer.

And there is a jacket. It is yellow satin, and it is trimmed with white fur. When his belongings were listed, many years from now, it will still be here. The fur may be rabbit, dyed to look like ermine. It does not matter. Painted, it looks like ermine.

It appears on a woman writing a letter. On a woman holding a pearl necklace up to the window. On a woman with a lute. On a lady handing a note to her maid. The same jacket. Perhaps it was Catharina's. Perhaps she wore it on cold mornings up here, when she sat for him.

Pearls, too. A string of them, and a pair of drop earrings. They come and go through the paintings like a small quiet tide.

Now look at the easel, and the man beside it.

He is in his thirties. He wears a plain dark coat and a soft cap against the draft. He is standing a little back from the canvas, with a brush in one hand and a palette on his thumb. He is not painting. He is looking. He has been looking for some time.

The canvas is not large. Most of his pictures are not. A woman could carry one under her arm. On it, so far, there is a room, and a window, and the shape of a figure blocked in softly in browns and grays. There is no hurry in any of it.

Here is a number to hold gently. Of all the paintings he made, only about thirty-five have come down to us. Some say thirty-four. Some say thirty-seven. Across a working life of more than twenty years, that is two pictures a year. Perhaps three. Other painters in this country turned out hundreds. One turned out more than a thousand. He turned out two.

He could afford to. His mother-in-law's house was comfortable. There was a patron in the town who bought many of his pictures. So the canvas stood on the easel for weeks. Months, sometimes. The carpet stayed on the table. The chair stayed where it was. The light came in from the left every afternoon, and he waited for it, and he added a little.

A brush touches the canvas. A soft dry whisper. Then it lifts away, and he steps back again.

Before the painting, there is the paint. Let us go and stand by the grinding table.

It is a heavy stone slab, in the corner nearest the window. It was in the house when his things were listed. A flat stone for grinding colors. Beside it lies the muller. That is a smooth stone, shaped like a small thick mushroom, with a flat bottom polished by years of use.

There is a jar of linseed oil. It is pressed from flax seed, and it is the color of pale honey, and it smells faintly of nuts. It dries slowly in air, over days, into a clear tough skin. That is what holds a painting together. Not glue. Oil, slowly becoming solid.

A little heap of dry pigment goes onto the stone. A few drops of oil. Then the muller comes down on it, and begins to move. Small circles at first. Then wider. Then wider still, until the paste is spread thin across the whole slab. Then it is gathered back to the middle with a knife, and the circles begin again.

You can hear it if you like. A low grinding hiss. Then, slowly, the sound changes. The grittiness goes out of it. When the muller moves without any sound at all, the paint is ready.

Most painters had a boy to do this. He seems not to have had one. As far as anyone can tell, he ground his colors himself. The paste was scraped up and stored in a shell, or tied into a little bladder, or set under water to keep the air away.

Some pigments he bought from an apothecary in the town, where they sat in jars beside the medicines. But one of them came from much further off.

The blue came from the other side of the world.

It began as a stone. Lapis lazuli. Deep blue, flecked with white, and with little sparks of gold-colored pyrite. It came from mines high in the mountains of what is now Afghanistan. Mines that had been worked for thousands of years already. From there it traveled by mule and by ship, hand to hand, across half the earth. The Italians called the color ultramarine. Beyond the sea.

Grinding the stone was not enough. Ground alone, it gave a dull gray-blue, because the blue is only part of the stone. So the powder was worked into a dough. Wax, and resin, and a little oil, warmed together, and the powder kneaded in.

Then the dough was placed in a bowl of warm water with lye in it. And it was kneaded there, slowly, like bread. The blue particles let go of the dough and drifted down into the water. The gray and the white stayed behind in the wax. The water was poured off, and the blue settled, and was dried.

The first washing gave the finest blue. The second, a little paler. The third, paler still. From a great deal of stone, a very little pigment.

By weight, it cost more than gold. Painters wrote it into their contracts. So much ultramarine, of such a grade, and no less. Most of them saved it for a single robe, and put cheaper blue underneath.

He did not. He used it everywhere.

Look closely at his shadows, and you will find it hidden.

It is in the shadows of the yellow jacket. Mixed into the yellow, a little blue makes the fold turn cool and deep. It is in the shadows of white cloth. A little blue in the white keeps the shadow from going muddy. It is in the gray of the plain wall behind the figure. A gray with ultramarine in it does not sit flat. It breathes.

It is mixed with red lake to make his purples. Mixed with green earth to make his cooler greens. Laid in thin glazes over the underpainting, so that a color seen through it takes on a faint coolness. Like a room in the early morning.

And where it is meant to be blue, it is very blue. The apron of the milkmaid. The headscarf of the girl with the pearl. A tablecloth, a cushion, a chair seat. Pure ground stone, bound in oil, and laid on thick.

This is the quietest kind of extravagance. Nobody looking at the picture sees the cost. They see only that the shadows are somehow alive. That the room is cool. That there is air in it. And all of that is stone from a mountain, ground by hand, washed and washed again, and spent without counting.

Breathe in, slowly. And out. There is no need to count anything tonight.

The yellow is made in a furnace.

Lead-tin yellow, it is called now. Lead and tin, as oxides, mixed together and heated until they fuse. Seven or eight hundred degrees. What comes out is a pale clear lemon yellow. It grinds soft, and it covers well, and it does not fade. Painters used it for three hundred years. Then, around the end of his century, it quietly disappeared. It was forgotten so completely that no one knew what it was. It was found again in old paintings, in the last hundred years.

The yellow of the milkmaid's bodice is this yellow, most likely. The yellow of the satin jacket, too, with a little ochre in it where the light falls away.

The ochres came out of the ground. Yellow ochre and red ochre and brown umber. Earth, dug and washed and dried and ground. They were the cheapest colors there were, and the oldest. He used them for the warm underlayers that everything else sat on.

The red is a plant. Madder root, boiled, and the color drawn out of it and fixed onto a white powder. It gives a red that is transparent and cool. He glazed the girl's lips with it. He glazed his deeper reds with it.

The white is lead. Strips of it, hung over vinegar in warm clay pots, for weeks, until a crust of white grows on the metal. Scraped off, washed, ground. Heavy and dense. It makes a thick paint that holds the mark of the brush.

Seven colors, mostly. White lead, yellow ochre, vermilion, madder, green earth, umber, ivory black. From these, and the blue, nearly everything.

Now watch how the picture is built. It is built in layers, and it is slow.

The canvas has been stretched on a wooden frame and given a ground. A layer of glue, and then a thin warm gray or brown, to take away the white. He never painted onto white. He painted onto a soft middle tone, so that both the lights and the darks were a step away.

Onto this goes the first laying-in. Broad shapes. Thin paint. The wall, the floor, the window, the figure. Browns, grays, a little of the blue. It is only a plan. It is allowed to dry. Days, in this damp air.

Then the working-up. The real colors go on now, thicker, and while they are wet he works into them. A stroke of light into a stroke of shadow. The edge between them softened with a dry brush. Wet into wet. This is where the folds of the cloth come, and the roundness of a cheek, and the pale curve of a jug.

Then it dries again. And then the glazes. Thin films of transparent color, oil with just a little pigment, laid over a dry passage to deepen it. Madder over a red. Ultramarine over a shadow. Each one a change so slight you would not notice it alone. Together they make the picture glow from underneath.

Between the layers, he waits. The room is quiet. The oil is doing its slow work in the dark of the paint. Nothing can be hurried. A painting is partly made of time.

Then, near the end, he does the smallest thing of all.

Onto the finished surface, with a fine brush, he sets small round dots of thick pale paint. Lead white, or lead-tin yellow. Each one is a tiny bead standing up from the canvas. People who study him call them pointillé. Little points.

They sit on the crust of the bread. Along the rim of a wicker basket. On the brass nails of a chair. On the edge of a window frame where the light catches. On the surface of a pearl. On the thread of milk falling from a jug.

They are not the shape of the thing. They are the shape of the light on the thing. A crust of bread does not really have circles on it. But when light strikes a rough surface, it sparkles, and the sparkle is round. He painted the sparkle.

Up close, they look like nothing. Small blobs. Step back, across the room, and they become the very thing that makes the bread look like bread. A painted loaf that you could break, and hear the crust give.

It is the last thing he does to a picture, and the lightest. A touch, and a touch, and a touch. Then he steps back. And then, very likely, he does nothing at all for a long time.

Here is a question that people have argued about for a hundred years, gently, without settling it.

In his time there was a device called a camera obscura. A dark room, or a dark box, with a small hole or a lens in one side. Light comes through the hole and throws a picture of the world onto the wall opposite. Upside down, and in full color, and moving. It is only the way light behaves. Any dark room with a small hole does it.

Some people think he used one. They point to the way his highlights are soft round dots, like the blur a lens makes on a bright point. They point to the way the nearer things in a picture are sometimes a little out of focus, and the farther things sharp. A lens does that. An eye does not.

Other people are not persuaded. There is no letter that mentions one. No inventory lists one. No friend ever wrote that he had one. And a painter who could see this well, they say, hardly needed a box to see for him. The soft dots may be simply how he chose to paint light.

Both sides may be partly right. He may have looked through a lens, and learned from it, and then painted from his own eye. We cannot know. It does not need settling tonight.

What we have is the picture. Light, gathered from a north window, laid down in oil and stone, and still there.

While he paints, the whole town is making color.

Delft is a pottery town. Along the canals stand the workshops, with their kilns, and the warmth of the kilns leaks out into the evening street. Thirty of them or more, in these years. They make earthenware the color of clay. Then they cover it in a white glaze made with tin, and paint on it in blue, and fire it again.

The blue is cobalt. Ground fine, it goes on nearly black. It only turns blue in the fire. The painters at their benches work in a dark gray, on a chalky white, and trust the kiln to turn it into sky.

They are copying, at first, the porcelain that comes from China. Ships of the East India Company bring it in, crate after crate, blue and white. It is thin and hard and rings like a bell. The Delft clay is thicker and softer. But the glaze is white, and the blue is blue, and from across a room it will do. And soon it is its own thing. Tulips and ships and windmills and little Dutch houses, in blue, on white, on the shelves of every kitchen.

The kilns burn all night. The town smells faintly of hot brick and woodsmoke. And in the morning the racks are full of blue.

Across the town, another man is bending over something small.

His name is Antonie van Leeuwenhoek. He was born here in Delft, in the same year as the painter, in 1632. He is a draper. He sells cloth. And to judge the quality of cloth, a draper uses a small magnifying glass to count the threads.

He has become interested in the glass.

In the evenings he grinds lenses. Tiny ones. A single bead of glass, no bigger than a lentil, ground and polished by hand until it is perfectly round. He sets it between two small plates of brass, with a pin beside it to hold whatever he wants to look at. The whole instrument is about the length of your thumb. You hold it up to your eye, close, in good light, and you look.

Through his best lenses, things become two hundred times larger. More. Nobody else in the world can grind glass this fine. He never tells anyone quite how he does it.

In 1674 he takes a little water from a lake outside the town. In summer, the water turns cloudy and green. Through the lens he sees green threads wound in slow spirals. And among them, moving, very many tiny living things, swimming. He calls them little animals.

Nobody had ever seen them before. They had always been there. In every drop of pond water, all along, going about their lives.

Two men, born in the same small town, in the same year. One grinding stone into blue, one grinding glass into a window. Both of them looking, very closely, at things other people walked past.

Let us go and look at one of the paintings, quite close. Not this one. One he finished a few years ago.

It shows the town itself, from across the water, from where we began tonight. The harbor in front. The wall and the two gates. The rooftops behind, and the tower of the New Church above them.

It is early morning. The clock on the gate says a little past seven. A bank of cloud has drifted across the sun, so the near part of the town lies in cool shadow. The wall, the gates, the boats. But the cloud does not reach the far side. There, the sunlight has got through, and it lies along the rooftops and the tower, warm and yellow.

The water is still. The town is in it, upside down, softly. A few small figures stand on the near bank, waiting for the ferry. Nobody is doing anything. It is a town waiting for the day to start.

And along the wet edges of the boats, and the shadowed brick, he has set those small dots of pale paint. Light on water. Light on tar. It is a very large picture of a very quiet moment.

People who know it well say it is not quite the view you would see if you stood there. He shifted a few things. He tidied the town, gently, the way you might straighten a blanket. Not to deceive anyone. Only so the light could lie where it wanted to.

Now come close to another. A kitchen, and a woman, and a jug.

She stands at a table by the window. Strong arms. A yellow bodice, and a blue apron, and a white cap. On the table is bread. A round loaf, a broken loaf, a basket of rolls. An earthen jug, and a bowl.

She is pouring milk. A thin white thread of it, from the jug into the bowl. That is the whole event. Nothing else in the picture moves. She does not move. The light does not move. Only the milk.

She is looking down at her work, and her face is entirely calm. She is making, most likely, a simple dish of old bread soaked in warm milk. Nothing is wasted in this kitchen.

The wall behind her is bare and pale, with a nail in it, and the small shadow of the nail. Light from the window on the left falls across her forehead, and the fold of her sleeve, and the crust of the bread. On the bread the light breaks up into those small round beads of paint. You could pick the roll up.

On the floor, behind her, is a small wooden box with holes in the top. A foot warmer. You put a pot of glowing coals inside it, and rest your feet on it, and the warmth comes up through the holes. It is a cold room, this kitchen. Look at her, and you know it. And there, in the corner, is the small warmth waiting.

Her apron is the blue from the mountain. Her bodice is the yellow from the furnace. The bread is ochre and white and a little dot of light. And all of it, together, is a woman standing quite still, doing one small thing well.

And there is the pearl.

The most famous of his pictures is only a face. A girl, turning to look over her shoulder, against a dark background. A blue and yellow cloth wound round her head. Her lips a little parted. And in her ear, a pearl.

It is a very large pearl. Too large, some people think, to have been real. So let us wonder, gently, what she wore, if she wore anything at all.

Perhaps it was glass. Venice made glass pearls then, blown thin and filled with a pale paste, and they were sold everywhere. Perhaps it was polished tin. Perhaps it was only paint, and there never was an earring, and he made it up. Nobody knows. In 2018 the picture was studied for two weeks under microscopes and scanners. They learned a great deal. They did not learn that.

What they did learn is that the pearl is almost nothing. Two strokes of white. One soft one at the top, where the light of the window lands on it. One at the bottom, where the white of her collar is reflected up. Between them, nothing. No line around it. No hook to hold it. The pearl is a suggestion, and your eye finishes it.

Her lips are glazed with madder. Her headscarf is the blue from the mountain, and the shadow in the yellow of her jacket is that blue too. The dark around her was once a deep glossy green, laid in thin glazes. It has grown darker with the years, the way the evening does.

Let it be a glass pearl, or a tin one, or none at all. It is a small round place where the light landed, and stayed.

Listen. From across the roofs, the bells.

The New Church has a new set of bells in its tower. Thirty-six of them, cast by a bell-founder named Hemony, and hung up in 1660. At the hour they play a tune. The notes come in through the leaded glass a little muffled, a little blurred, the way light comes through it. Then the big bell strikes the hour underneath. Then the Old Church, a moment later, a little lower, agreeing.

Down on the canal, a barge is passing. You can hear the water fold around its bow. The soft knock of a pole against the stone edge. A voice saying something to a horse. Then it is past, and the ripples reach the wall, and stop.

The light in the studio has changed. It is going from gray to blue. The window panes are darker than the wall now. The colors on the palette have gone quiet. You cannot tell the madder from the umber anymore.

He sets the palette down. He cannot see well enough to go on, and he knows it, and he does not mind. He takes the brushes one at a time, and wipes them on a rag, and works them in a little dish of oil, and lays them in a row. The smell of linseed rises in the cool air.

Then he stands for a moment longer, in front of the canvas. Looking at what the last of the light is doing to it. Then he goes down the narrow stairs, slowly, one hand on the rail, and the room is empty.

And the studio is quiet.

The easel stands in the dark with the canvas on it. The oil in the paint is drying, so slowly you could never see it. Turning from liquid into something that will last for hundreds of years. The blue is in the shadows. The dots of light are on the bread. Nothing needs him now.

The map hangs on the wall. The chair stands by the table with the carpet on it. The yellow jacket lies folded where it was left. Down in the corner, the grinding stone holds the coolness of the evening, and the muller rests on it.

Outside, the canals are still. The towers stand over the roofs, one tall, one leaning. The kilns along the streets are banked for the night, and their warmth leaks softly into the dark. Across the town a man puts down a bead of glass and rubs his eyes.

Everything here was made slowly. Stone ground into blue. Glass ground into a lens. Light gathered from a north window and laid down a little at a time. Nobody in this town is in a hurry. The pictures will wait until morning. And the morning light will come from the left, as it always has.

So let it be quiet now.

Let the light go out of the window, pane by pane. Let the canals go on holding the town upside down in the dark. Let the bells go on marking the hours for nobody in particular. Let the paint go on drying, all night, in the empty room.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the blue, not the yellow, not the names, not a single fact from the whole quiet evening. It will all keep itself, as it has for more than three hundred years. Without any help from you tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slow, and slower. Let the last of your thoughts settle, softly, like pigment settling in a bowl of still water. Let the cool of the north light fade. Let the warmth of the foot warmer come up around you, gentle and complete.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
