Good evening.

However the day has gone, it is finished now. It has nothing more to ask of you. Let your shoulders come down away from your ears. Let the bed take your whole weight. Nothing tonight needs carrying any further.

Let your hands fall open. Let your jaw soften. Let your breathing settle into whatever pace it wants. Nothing here needs watching. Nothing needs deciding. Nothing is waiting on you.

Tonight we are going to follow a piece of patient work. It took several decades, and a great many cold nights on mountains. Numbers were written down one at a time, by a lot of people.

What they were doing was measuring how far away things are. And when they had measured enough of them, a shape appeared that nobody had expected.

We will start in a small room on a mountain in Arizona. Then we will go outward, slowly, until we can see the shape they found.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

It is the early nineteen eighties, and it is night.

Picture a dome on a ridge, above the desert, with the lights of nothing much in the distance. The air is thin and very dry and very cold.

Inside, the dome is open to the sky, so it is the same temperature as the night outside. There is a smell of cold metal and machine oil.

Someone is sitting at a desk beside the telescope, in a coat, with a mug going cold beside them.

There is a low hum from the drive motors. Every so often the whole dome rotates a few degrees, with a sound like a distant train, and then stops.

They are working through a list. On the list are galaxies — hundreds of them, faint smudges catalogued from photographs.

The job is to point at one, collect its light for half an hour, write down what came back, and move to the next.

That is the entire job. Some nights they manage twenty. Some nights the clouds come in and they manage none.

What they are collecting is a spectrum, and it is worth knowing what a spectrum gives you.

When you spread a galaxy's light out by wavelength, it is not smooth. There are dark gaps in it, at very specific places, where particular elements have absorbed particular colours.

Those gaps are a fingerprint. Hydrogen makes its marks in one pattern. Calcium makes another. In a laboratory on Earth, those patterns sit at exactly known wavelengths.

But in the light of a distant galaxy, the whole pattern is shifted. Every gap has moved towards the red end, all by the same proportion.

The shape is intact. It has just been slid along.

That happens because space itself has been expanding while the light was travelling. The light gets stretched along with it.

So the amount of the shift tells you how long the light has been in flight. Which tells you how far away the galaxy is.

That method was worked out long before anyone knew what these smudges even were.

In nineteen twelve, at a small observatory in Arizona, a man called Vesto Slipher pointed a telescope at the faint oval in Andromeda. He collected its light, patiently, for a very long time. The exposures took whole nights. Sometimes several nights, stacked together, for one object.

Nobody at the time was certain whether these things were clouds inside our own galaxy or something else entirely.

By nineteen fourteen he had fifteen of them, and he read the results to a meeting in Illinois. Almost all of them were shifted to the red, and by large amounts. The room stood up and applauded, which is not a common thing at an astronomy meeting.

By nineteen seventeen he had twenty-five.

He was not trying to discover that the universe expands. He was measuring how fast some faint ovals were moving. The meaning arrived years afterwards, once other people worked out how far away they were.

The person who did most of the follow-up work had an unusual route into it.

His name was Milton Humason. He left school early, and he first came to Mount Wilson as a mule driver, hauling equipment up the mountain during construction.

When the observatory was built, he took a job there as a janitor. Then he worked in the darkroom. Then he was made a night assistant, because it turned out he was extremely good at the actual work of observing.

It is a delicate job. You are guiding a telescope by hand for hours, in the cold. A faint smudge has to stay exactly on a slit, so that its light goes where it needs to go.

He became one of the best observers of his generation. He spent years collecting the spectra of very faint, very distant galaxies, and those measurements are what turned Slipher's curiosity into a law.

And that is the trick the whole thing rests on, so let us be clear about why it matters.

A photograph of the sky is flat. It gives you two directions and no depth at all.

Two galaxies can appear side by side in an image and be a billion light years apart, one far behind the other. The picture cannot tell you. It never could.

Every catalogue of galaxies before this was, in effect, a very detailed picture of a wall.

The redshift is what turns it into a room. Measure the shift for each smudge, and each smudge acquires a distance, and the flat picture opens up into three dimensions.

But it has to be done one galaxy at a time. There is no shortcut. Every single point in the map costs somebody half an hour on a mountain.

So they did that, for years.

Drive up the mountain in the afternoon. Open the dome at dusk. Work through the list. Close up at dawn. Sleep. Drive down. Come back.

Weather takes about a third of the nights. Equipment takes some more. Nothing about it is fast, and nothing about it is glamorous, and the individual measurements are not interesting on their own.

One number per galaxy. Then the next galaxy.

Over several years a group working out of Harvard built up eleven hundred of them, in a particular wedge of sky.

Eleven hundred is not many. It is a very small sample of the universe. But it was enough, because of how they chose to plot it.

They took a thin slice.

Rather than scattering the galaxies through a three-dimensional volume, they picked a narrow strip of sky. A band a few degrees tall. Then they plotted only what was in it.

Direction across the page. Distance outward from the centre.

A wedge, like a slice of pie, with us at the point of it.

The expectation was straightforward. Galaxies were assumed to be scattered fairly evenly, with some clumping here and there. Plotted this way, the slice ought to look like a slightly lumpy spray of dots.

In nineteen eighty-six, three of them — Valérie de Lapparent, Margaret Geller and John Huchra — published the plot.

It did not look like a spray of dots at all.

It looked like bubbles.

The galaxies sat in thin curved chains, and the chains joined up, and between them were large round regions with nothing in them.

Not fewer galaxies. Effectively none.

The dots were arranged as though they were sitting on the surfaces of soap bubbles, packed together. Clustered along the films. Gathered where several films met. And absent from the insides.

Nobody had drawn the universe this way before because nobody had had the depth to draw. And the moment the depth existed, the structure was simply there in the data, unmistakable, from eleven hundred points.

There is a nice detail in that plot. Right in the middle of it there is a shape that looks like a little person, with arms and legs. People started calling it the stick man.

That is not a structure. It is one dense cluster of galaxies, smeared out along the line of sight. Their own motion within the cluster does that, and it makes any cluster look stretched in this kind of map.

It is an artefact. It is also, for a whole generation of astronomers, the most familiar shape in cosmology.

That smearing is worth a moment, because it stopped being a nuisance and became a tool.

A redshift is not purely a distance. It also picks up whatever motion the galaxy has of its own, on top of the general expansion.

Inside a dense cluster the galaxies swing about at considerable speeds. Some are coming towards us a little, some going away a little. The map reads that as distance, and stretches the cluster out into a spike pointing straight at us.

For years that was simply an error to be tolerated.

Then people realised the size of the smear tells you something. How fast galaxies fall towards dense regions depends on how strong gravity is on those scales, and on how much unseen mass is there.

So the distortion became a measurement of how quickly the structure is growing.

It is a nice pattern in this kind of work. The thing that spoils your map turns out to be carrying information, once you look at it directly.

Since then the language has settled down, and the pieces have names.

The dense knots where several strands meet are clusters. Thousands of galaxies, held together, in a region a few tens of millions of light years across.

The strands running between the knots are filaments. Long, thin, threaded with galaxies.

The broad flat regions are walls, or sheets. Wide and comparatively thin.

And the great rounded spaces enclosed by all of it are voids.

Put together, that is the cosmic web. Knots joined by threads, threads bounding sheets, sheets wrapped around enormous quiet interiors.

It is worth saying what a filament would actually be like to be inside, because the word suggests something denser than it is.

You would not notice one. That is the first thing.

Standing anywhere along a filament, the sky would look ordinary. Dark, with the stars of your own galaxy across it, and a scattering of faint smudges beyond.

A filament is a strand only in the sense that a very sparse mist is a shape. The galaxies within it might be a few million light years apart. You could travel through it your whole life and never sense the strand.

The structure exists at a scale where individual galaxies are dust. It is a shape made of dots that are themselves enormous, spread so far apart that the shape is invisible from inside it.

Which is why nobody suspected it was there. You cannot see it by looking up. You can only see it by measuring several thousand distances and then drawing them all on one page.

One of the walls they found in that early data is worth a moment.

It is a sheet of galaxies about six hundred million light years long. It is two hundred and fifty million across. And it is only about thirty million thick.

So it is genuinely a wall. A vast flat quilt of galaxies, comparatively thin, hanging in the map.

They called it the Great Wall, and for some years it was the largest single thing anyone had found.

Nothing dramatic is happening in it. It is not moving anywhere in particular. It is simply an enormous number of galaxies arranged in a sheet, sitting there, minding themselves.

And now the other half of the picture, which is the part I find most restful.

The voids are large. Much larger than people expected.

There is one in the direction of the constellation Boötes that is around three hundred and thirty million light years across. In a region that size you might reasonably expect a couple of thousand galaxies.

It has a few dozen.

It was found by accident, in nineteen eighty-one, by another team doing exactly the same patient business of measuring redshifts. They kept expecting the galaxies to start again, and for a very long stretch of the survey, they did not.

Here is a thought that goes with it, and I like this one very much. Suppose our own galaxy sat near the middle of a void like that. The sky would look almost the same to the naked eye. But the nearest other galaxies would be terribly distant and faint. We might not have worked out that other galaxies existed at all, until well into the twentieth century.

A quiet neighbourhood. That is all it would be.

Voids are not actually empty, and what is in them is rather lovely.

They contain a scattering of galaxies — small ones, mostly, faint and blue, with plenty of gas still in them and no large neighbours anywhere.

Galaxies in crowded regions get stirred up. They pass close to each other, they pull gas out of each other, they end up older and redder and more settled.

A void galaxy has had none of that. It has simply sat in its own enormous room, making stars slowly out of the gas it started with. Undisturbed, for the entire history of the universe.

They are the least eventful objects there are, and there are a surprising number of them.

And the voids themselves are still gently emptying. The little material left inside them continues to drift outward towards the walls, because that is where the pull is.

So they grow. Very slowly, over billions of years, the quiet rooms get larger.

So where does a pattern like this come from? The answer is slow and it is very gentle.

Go back to when the universe was young and hot and almost completely smooth. Almost. Not quite.

There were tiny variations in density from place to place — differences of about one part in a hundred thousand. Faint ripples in an otherwise even sea.

Gravity does one thing with a difference like that. It makes it bigger.

A region that is very slightly denser pulls very slightly harder. So it gathers a little more. Which makes it denser still. Which makes it pull harder still.

And the regions that were very slightly thinner give their material up to their neighbours, and become thinner, and give up more.

Run that for thirteen billion years and the ripples become this. The dense parts become filaments and knots. The thin parts drain, and keep draining, and become voids.

Nothing pushed anything. The material simply moved slowly downhill, for a very long time.

There is one more ingredient, and most of the web is made of it.

The galaxies you can see are not doing the structural work. They are sitting on top of something far more massive that gives off no light at all.

That material is detected by what it pulls on. Galaxies rotate too quickly for the stars they contain. Clusters hold together more firmly than their visible mass allows. Light passing near them bends more than it should.

All of those point the same way, and they point at a great deal of unseen mass, arranged in the same web.

So the honest picture is this. There is an enormous invisible scaffold, shaped like a web. And the visible galaxies are lights strung along it, marking out the shape of something you cannot see directly.

Rather like frost picking out a spider's web on a cold morning. The frost is not the web. It is what lets you see it.

And even the ordinary matter is mostly not in the galaxies, which took a long time to establish.

For years there was a straightforward accounting problem. Theory said how much ordinary matter the universe should contain — the kind that atoms are made of. Add up everything visible, in all the stars and all the gas in all the galaxies, and you get about half of that.

The other half was missing. Not mysterious in nature, just unlocated.

The suspicion was that it was strung along the filaments as extremely thin gas. Hot enough to be hard to see, and spread so wide that it barely registers.

Confirming that has taken twenty years of careful, indirect work. Several different methods were used, with nothing much in common between them. The answer has come out consistent.

The gas is there. The filaments are mostly gas, with galaxies as the occasional bright knot in it.

So the threads are real threads. They are simply made of something too faint and too spread out to photograph.

And there is a scale built into the web that comes from sound, which is the loveliest fact in this whole subject.

In the very early universe, everything was a hot dense plasma. Hot dense material carries pressure waves. Which is to say, it carries sound.

Those slightly denser ripples we mentioned each sent out a spherical sound wave, moving outward, very fast.

Then, about three hundred and eighty thousand years in, the universe cooled enough to become transparent. The pressure that had been carrying the waves vanished.

And the waves stopped, exactly where they were.

Each one left a faint spherical shell of slightly denser material, at a fixed radius, around every original ripple.

That radius is still there in the arrangement of galaxies today. Galaxies are very slightly more likely to be found about five hundred million light years apart than at any other separation.

A preferred distance, frozen into the sky, left over from a sound that stopped playing thirteen billion years ago.

That frozen distance turns out to be extremely useful, and it changed what the surveys were for.

Suppose you know how long a thing actually is. Then you measure how long it looks. From those two numbers you can work out how far away it is. That is how a ruler held at arm's length works.

The sound scale is a ruler of known length, and copies of it are scattered through the entire universe at every distance.

So a large enough map lets you measure the ruler nearby, and then further out, and then further still. And that tells you how the expansion of space has behaved over time.

Which is why these surveys stopped being about drawing a picture and started being about measuring something.

The picture was the beginning. The ruler is the reason it kept going.

The equipment changed as the ambitions grew, and the way it worked is a lovely piece of craft.

For one of the big surveys they used aluminium plates. Round, about eighty centimetres across, and three millimetres thick.

For each patch of sky, a plate was drilled with six hundred and forty holes. The pattern of holes matched the exact positions of the galaxies in that patch.

Then, every afternoon, people would sit with the plate and plug an optical fibre into every one of those holes. By hand. Six hundred and forty of them, one at a time.

That night the plate went into the telescope, and each fibre carried the light of one object down to a spectrograph.

The next afternoon, a different plate, a different patch of sky, and the whole thing plugged again.

Thousands of plates. Millions of holes. All of them plugged by somebody's hands.

The current instrument does the same job differently, and it is a rather beautiful machine.

It sits on a four-metre telescope in Arizona. In its focal plane there are five thousand small robots.

Each robot is a little arm holding the end of one optical fibre. On command, all five thousand of them move at once, and each one places its fibre precisely on top of one galaxy.

The whole field rearranges itself in a couple of minutes. Then the telescope collects light. Then they all move again, into a completely different pattern, for the next patch of sky.

No plates. No plugging. Five thousand spectra at a time, all night, every clear night.

Earlier this year it finished the five-year map it was built to make. Forty-seven million galaxies and quasars, each one with its own measured distance.

And because it is working well, it is carrying on. There is more sky.

There is also an argument going on about what the map is showing, and it is unresolved.

The expansion of space is not slowing down. It has been speeding up for the last several billion years, and the thing responsible is called dark energy, which is mostly a name for not knowing.

The simplest assumption is that it is constant. A fixed property of empty space, the same everywhere, the same at every moment. That assumption has held up well for twenty-five years.

The new maps hint that it might not be constant. When the ruler is measured at many different distances, and therefore at many different times, the numbers seem to prefer a version that changes slightly.

It is a hint. It is not a result. The effect is small, it depends on how several independent measurements are combined, and careful people are careful about it.

If it holds up, a fairly settled piece of physics becomes unsettled again. If it fades with more data, the simple version stands.

Either way the answer comes from the same place. More galaxies, more distances, one at a time, on more clear nights.

There is a way to check all of this, and the check is done in a computer.

You take the early universe as it is actually observed — smooth, hot, with those one-part-in-a-hundred-thousand ripples. You put in gravity, and the ordinary matter, and the unseen mass.

Then you let it run forward. Billions of simulated years, in a box.

And what comes out is a web. Filaments. Knots where filaments cross. Sheets between them. Great rounded voids.

Nobody puts the web in. Nobody tells it to make filaments. It falls out of the arithmetic, from a nearly smooth start, every time.

You can put a picture of the simulation next to a picture of the survey and struggle to say which is which.

That agreement is the quiet payoff of the whole enterprise. Decades of people writing down one number per galaxy, and a physics that starts from almost nothing, arriving at the same shape.

It is worth knowing where we are in it, so let us place ourselves.

Our galaxy sits with a couple of dozen others in a small local gathering. That gathering sits towards the edge of a much larger flow of galaxies, all drifting gently in a common direction.

In two thousand and fourteen a group mapped those motions and drew a boundary around everything moving together with us. It came out at something like five hundred and twenty million light years across, containing perhaps a hundred thousand galaxies.

They gave it a Hawaiian name that means immeasurable heaven.

We are not at the middle of it. We are out on one of the strands, well off to one side, drifting slowly along with everything else nearby.

Now let us pull back and simply look at the whole thing for a while.

From far enough out it does look like a web, or like the inside of a sponge, or like foam.

Bright knots. Fine threads between them. Broad pale sheets. And the great dark rounded rooms in between, taking up most of the volume.

It is entirely still. Not truly, but as good as. The galaxies are moving, and the whole thing is very slowly stretching. But the changes take hundreds of millions of years to amount to anything.

Nothing in that picture will look different tomorrow. Nothing in it will look different in ten thousand years.

It is the largest arrangement there is, and it is doing nothing at all in any hurry.

And the thing that stays with me is not the web. It is the way it was found.

Not by a single instrument, or a single night, or a single person. By a very long series of ordinary evenings.

A cold dome. A list. One galaxy, half an hour, one number written down. Then the next galaxy.

Nobody who plugged a fibre into a hole on a Tuesday afternoon was looking at the shape of the universe. They were looking at a hole, and a fibre.

The shape only exists when enough of those afternoons are added together. It could not have been guessed at, and it could not have been rushed.

There is something restful in that. Very large things are found by very small, repeated, unremarkable acts.

You do not need to hold on to any of it.

Not the filaments, or the voids, or the frozen sound. Not the five thousand small arms moving at once, in the dark, on a mountain in Arizona.

It is all there anyway, arranged exactly as it is, whether or not anyone is thinking about it tonight. It was there long before this evening, and it will be there afterwards, changing at a rate too slow to matter.

Let the words go. Let the picture soften and drift apart.

There is nothing out there that needs you tonight. There is nothing here that does either.

Only a very old, very large, very quiet arrangement of light, holding still.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
