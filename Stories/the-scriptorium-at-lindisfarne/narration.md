Good evening.

Wherever you are tonight, whatever the day asked of you, you can set it down now. It is finished. It wants nothing more from you. Let your shoulders come down, away from your ears. Let the bed take your whole weight. There is nothing left for you to hold up.

Let your hands go soft. Let your jaw unclench. Let your breathing slow on its own, without any help from you. There is nothing here to keep watch over. Nothing to decide. Nothing you will be asked about in the morning.

Tonight we are going to a small island off the north coast of England. The year is around 715. The sea comes in twice a day and closes the road behind it. In a room on that island, a man is painting one letter of a book. He has been at it for years. Nobody is hurrying him. Neither will we.

If your attention drifts as we go, let it drift. If you lose the thread of the words, let it go. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of. So breathe in, slowly, through your nose. And let it go. And let us begin.

§

We begin on the mainland shore, late on a summer evening.

The tide is out. Ahead of you, across the flats, runs a track of wet sand and mud. It is the only way over to the island. At low water it is simply there, firm enough to walk on. Twice a day the sea comes back and takes it away again.

The sand is cool under your feet. It holds the shape of your heel for a moment, and then lets it go. Shallow runnels of water cross the track. Warm at the surface, cold underneath. Small worm casts lie everywhere, curled like soft rope.

The air smells of salt, and wet weed, and something faintly green. A curlew calls somewhere out on the flats. The note rises, and then falls away, unhurried.

Out to your right, the water is already moving. It comes in over the sand in a thin bright sheet. Not fast. But without any pause at all. It fills one low channel, and then the next one. You can hear it if you stand still. A soft running sound, very wide and very low.

People have walked this crossing for eighty years already. They know the hours of it the way you know your own street. You go when the water lets you. You wait when it does not. That is the whole arrangement.

The island lies about a mile off. Low, and long, and pale gold in the evening light. Let us cross now, while the sand still lets us. There is no hurry. There is time.

§

The ground rises a little, and you are on the island.

It is a small place. Three miles across at its widest, and less than two from shore to shore. A thousand acres of grass and sand and shelter. You could walk the whole edge of it between one meal and the next.

Underfoot now, marram grass. Stiff blue-green blades that whisper against each other in the wind. Behind them, dunes. Soft-shouldered, warm on their south side where the sun has been all day. Sand runs down them in slow trickles when you step near.

In the hollows between the dunes, the ground is damp and quiet. Small flowers grow there, close to the sand, out of the wind. The whole island smells of salt and sheep and thyme.

Oystercatchers work the tideline in ones and twos. Black and white birds with long orange bills, stitching along the wet edge. When one lifts, it calls. A thin piping note, three times, and then it settles again a little further on.

Ahead of you, on the higher ground, stands the monastery. Not the stone ruin that visitors know now. That comes centuries later. Tonight it is a low huddle of buildings inside a bank and a ditch. Timber halls. Small round cells. A church of hewn oak.

The roof of that church was thatched with reeds once, in the old Irish way. A later bishop stripped the thatch off and covered the walls and roof with sheets of lead. It is a dull soft gray now, and it holds the last of the light.

§

Let us stand outside the gate a moment, and think about how this place began.

Eighty years ago, a king of Northumbria named Oswald wanted teachers for his kingdom. He sent to a monastery on the island of Iona, far away on the west coast of Scotland. Iona sent him a monk named Aidan.

Aidan came, in about 635, and the king gave him this island for his own. It is easy to see why he took it. It is near the royal fortress at Bamburgh, just down the coast. And yet the sea shuts the door twice a day, all by itself.

That is a very particular kind of place to live. You are connected, and then you are not. The world can reach you for a few hours, and then it withdraws, and the island is only itself again.

Aidan walked everywhere rather than ride. He taught in small groups. He gave away what he was given. He stayed here until he died, sixteen years later, and the community he left went on without him.

Everything on this island grew out of that. The school. The library. The copying of books. A habit of long patient attention, brought over from Iona, and settled here in the sand and the wind.

Think of it as a chain of quiet rooms, each one lighting the next. A room on an island off Ireland. A room on an island off Scotland. And now this room, here, off the coast of England, with a lamp in it.

§

Come in through the low door, out of the wind.

The room is warm. The change is immediate and complete. Outside, the air moves constantly, and here it does not move at all.

It smells of several things at once. Beeswax. Lamp oil. The faint chalky sweetness of prepared skin. And under all of it, wood smoke, from a small fire that has been kept low.

The floor is packed earth, swept smooth. There are two or three sloped desks, made of plain oak, worn pale where forearms rest. Near the window there is a stool. A man is sitting on it, quite still. He is looking at something on the board in front of him.

The tradition gives him a name. Eadfrith. He was bishop here, and the book on his desk is the one we now call the Lindisfarne Gospels. We will come back later to how we know that. The way we know it is one of the loveliest parts of the story.

The window is unglazed, and the shutter is propped open for the light. Northern summer light lasts a long time this far up the coast. It comes in level and gold across the desk, and lies on the page like something poured.

He has been working on this book for years. Perhaps five. Perhaps ten. Nobody has told him when it must be finished. Nobody is waiting downstairs.

Watch his hands for a moment. One rests flat and still on the board. The other holds a small feather, very lightly, the way you would hold something alive.

§

Before any of it, though, there were the skins.

The pages of this book are vellum. Calfskin, scraped and stretched and dried until it is thin enough to write on and strong enough to last.

It took a great many calves. The counts that people give run from about 130 skins to about 150. Either way, the number tells you something. A book like this is not made by one person. A whole countryside stands behind it. Herds, and pasture, and years.

The making of a skin is slow and wet and quiet. The skin goes first into a bath of lime and water, and sits there for days. The hair loosens. It stops holding on.

Then the skin is taken out and laid over a curved wooden beam. A worker leans on it with a blunt two-handled blade and pushes the hair away in long strokes. The sound of it is soft and rhythmic. Nothing about the motion is fast.

Then the skin goes onto a frame. It is tied at the edges with little pegs. The pegs are turned, a quarter at a time, all the way round. The skin tightens like a drum. As it dries, it pulls, and it is tightened again.

While it dries, it is scraped. A crescent blade, held in both hands, moving in long arcs. Wetted, dried, scraped again. Thinner. More even. Paler.

At the end it is rubbed with pumice until the surface holds still. Then dusted with chalk, so that ink will sit on it and not spread.

Hold a finished leaf up to the window and the light comes through it, warm and cream and faintly veined. It is soft as cloth, and it will outlive stone.

§

Now the sheets have to be turned into pages.

The leaves are folded and gathered in eights. A stack of four folded sheets makes eight leaves, sixteen pages, and that little bundle is a unit of work.

To keep the lines straight, the page must be ruled. And here is a small detail worth carrying with you.

Along the outer margin of each gathering, tiny holes are pricked through all eight leaves at once. A sharp point, pressed through the stack, in an even column down the edge. Every leaf in the bundle now agrees about where the lines go.

Then each page is ruled between the pricks. Some of that ruling was done with a dry point, pressed into the surface, leaving a furrow rather than a mark. And some of it was done with lead.

A lead point is simply a soft metal stylus. Drawn across skin, it leaves a faint gray line, like a very pale pencil. It is one of the earliest uses of the thing anyone has found. The pencil in your drawer is a distant descendant of it, with a piece of graphite where the lead used to be.

Turn a finished page over and you can still see the working. Faint lines. Pin pricks from a pair of dividers. Circles from a compass, stepped round and round, where a pattern was laid out before it was painted.

The front of the page is the miracle. The back of the page is the arithmetic. Both of them are still there, thirteen hundred years later, one behind the other.

§

Next, the pen.

It is a feather. A large flight feather, taken from a goose or a swan, its barbs stripped back from the shaft where the fingers go.

The shaft is hollow and slightly curved. It is hardened first, in warm sand or in a low oven, until it goes from flexible to crisp. Then it is cut. A long slice off the underside. A little scoop out. A small slit up the middle of the tip, so the ink will run down and stop where it should.

That slit is the whole invention. It makes a tiny reservoir and a tiny valve. Pressure widens it, and the line thickens. Ease off, and the line goes fine again.

A quill goes soft with use. So there is a small sharp knife on the desk, kept for exactly this. Every so often the scribe stops, and pares the tip back square. That is where we get the word penknife, and it has been sitting in the language ever since.

The ink for the text is made from oak galls. These are the small hard brown balls that grow on oak twigs when a certain wasp lays an egg in the bud. The tree builds a little room around the intruder, and the room is full of a bitter substance.

The galls are crushed and steeped in water or wine. Then iron is added, in the form of a green salt, and the liquid turns dark. A little gum from a tree holds it together.

It goes on brownish, and it darkens on the page as it dries. It bites very slightly into the surface, which is why it holds. The text of this book is a dense brown-black, almost the color of a wet beach at dusk.

§

Now the writing itself.

The letters are a script we call insular half-uncial. Round, generous, and beautifully even. Each letter sits separately, without running into the next.

It is a slow hand on purpose. There are no shortcuts built into it. You cannot write it quickly and have it stay handsome. It rewards a person who is willing to put the pen down and pick it up again.

Watch the rhythm. He looks at the book he is copying from, propped up beside him. He reads a few words. He forms them. He stops. He re-wets the nib. He begins again.

The sound is very soft. A dry whisper as the quill crosses the surface. A tiny tap when he touches it to the rim of the inkhorn. Then the whisper again.

Some letters get a small flourish of red dots around the outside. Rows of them, tiny and even, following the shape of the letter like a shadow. There are tens of thousands of these dots in the book. Somebody sat and placed every one.

A page like this takes a long time. Ruling, writing, checking, correcting, decorating. And then there are more than two hundred and fifty leaves of it to do.

He is not counting. That is the thing to take with you into sleep tonight. He is not measuring the distance to the end. He is doing this letter, and then the next letter, and the evening is long, and the light is good.

§

Consider, for a moment, the other book on the desk.

He is copying from something. No scribe invents a Gospel. There is an older book propped up beside him, open, weighted at the corner.

Scholars have traced the wording of the text he produced. It is a clean Latin version, of a particular family. That family is very close to a great Bible made at Wearmouth and Jarrow. Those are twin monasteries, a day or so down the coast.

And behind that, further back, the trail seems to run to Italy. The exemplar he worked from appears to have come from Naples. Certain saints from that region turn up in the lists at the front. That is the sort of fingerprint a book cannot help leaving.

So think about the journey. A book made under a southern sun, carried north through Gaul, across the sea, up the long English coast. Handed over. Set on a desk in a room that smells of salt.

A man on a cold island copies a sentence from a warm one. He has probably never seen an olive tree. The letters do not mind. They come across whole.

That is what a library was, then. Not a building. A slow current of copies, moving north and south, carrying words from one room to another, over generations.

The front of the book is given over to something you would recognize as engineering.

Before the four Gospels begin, there are sixteen pages of tables. They are a finding system, and they are about four hundred years old by the time this book is made.

The problem they solve is simple. Four accounts of the same life, each telling some things the others do not. A reader who wants to compare them needs a way in.

So a scholar named Eusebius cut the text into small numbered passages. Then he built ten tables. One lists the passages found in all four. Another lists those found in three. And so on, down to the passages each Gospel has to itself.

Find the number beside a passage, look it up in the table, and the table tells you where its companions are. It is, in plain terms, a cross-reference index, worked out by hand, long before anything mechanical existed to help.

In this book the tables are set inside painted arcades. Rows of slim columns, with rounded arches over them, and birds and knots worked into the capitals.

That is a nice thing to sit with. Somebody looked at a page of numbers and decided it should also be beautiful.

§

Let us look at the colors, because each one came a long way.

The yellow is orpiment. A mineral of arsenic and sulfur, a warm gold-yellow, ground to a fine powder. It came in by trade from far away. It smells very faintly of the earth it came from.

The orange-red is red lead. Lead white, heated in air until it turns. A gentle chemical trick, known for centuries before this, and the result is a soft warm orange.

The white is lead white itself. Strips of lead are stood over vinegar, in a warm dark place. Then you wait, and scrape off the pale crust that forms.

The greens come in more than one way. There is verdigris, which is what happens when copper meets the fumes of vinegar and goes that particular soft blue-green. And there is a green made simply by mixing blue over yellow, which the old books call vergaut.

The pinks and purples come from plants and lichens. Lichen scraped off rock, soaked, and coaxed into giving up a color between rose and violet. There is a great deal of quiet chemistry in this room, done by people who never used the word.

All of it is ground with a stone on a stone, very fine. Then bound with glair, which is egg white, beaten to a foam and left to settle back into a clear liquid.

Egg white, and rock, and lichen. The whole book is painted with the countryside and a little trade.

§

The blue deserves a section of its own, because there is a good open question inside it.

For a long time, people said the blue in this book was lapis lazuli. That is a deep stone from the mountains of Afghanistan, ground to make the pigment we call ultramarine. It was a lovely thing to believe. A blue from the far side of the world, arriving on a small island in the North Sea.

Then the book was examined with a laser, in a way that does not touch or take anything. The technique reads the way a material scatters light, and every substance answers in its own voice.

The blues answered as indigo. A plant blue. Not a stone from a mountain range. Leaves, fermented in a vat, turning from green to blue as they meet the air.

Indigo could have come from the woad that grows in these fields, or from indigo traded up from further south. The two are chemically the same color, which is why the question is difficult and rather charming.

So the honest position is this. The blue is a plant blue. The mountain stone is no longer the answer, though some still wonder about a trace here and there.

And either way the effect is the same on the page. A deep, soft, sea-dark blue, sitting quietly beside gold and coral red. The color of the water outside the window, about an hour from now.

§

Now turn to one of the great pages, and let us look at it slowly.

Five pages in this book are given over entirely to pattern. We call them carpet pages, because they look like woven rugs laid flat. Each one carries a cross, set into a field of ornament that runs to every edge.

The pattern is knotwork. Ribbons that cross over and under each other, endlessly, without a loose end anywhere. In among the ribbons, if you look, there are creatures. Long birds with their beaks caught in the weave. Slim dogs with their limbs drawn out into more ribbon.

It looks free, and it is not. Underneath it lies a grid.

The page was divided into squares. Circles were stepped round with a compass. Dividers were walked along a line to mark equal distances. Then the ribbons were drawn between those marks, and only then painted.

Some of this was laid out on the back of the leaf. It was worked through from behind, with the light coming through the skin. The vellum is thin enough for that. The design shows as a shadow, and the brush follows it.

Come close to the surface. Some of the strands are less than a millimeter wide. In places the painter kept a line true across a whole page without a wobble you can see with the naked eye.

Stand back again and none of that shows. It is simply a field of color, and a cross standing inside it, quiet and complete.

§

There are portraits in the book as well, one before each Gospel.

Four men, seated, each one writing. Matthew with a book on his knee. Mark, and Luke, and John. Their robes fall in long ordered folds, colored in rose and gold and blue.

Each of them has a creature with him, up in the corner, as a kind of sign. A man for Matthew. A lion for Mark. A calf for Luke. An eagle for John. These four had been paired that way for centuries already, and the painter simply keeps the custom.

Three of them are shown at work, heads bent over the page, doing precisely what the painter himself is doing. The fourth sits facing out, holding his scroll toward you.

There is something very homely about that. A man painting a picture of a man writing a book, inside a book he is writing. Nothing about it is showing off. It is a craftsman drawing his own trade.

Beside each portrait, and at the head of each Gospel, comes a great opening letter. The first word of the text swells up and takes over the whole page. It is drawn out into spirals and trumpet shapes and little animal heads, and then filled in with color, dot by dot.

Gold appears here and there, but sparingly. A touch of it, in small places, catching the lamp when the page turns. Most of the brightness in this book is not metal at all. It is mineral and plant, and patience.

§

The book was not finished in this room, and it was not finished by one pair of hands.

The writing and painting are one job. Binding is another. The pages have to be sewn into gatherings, and the gatherings sewn to cords, and the cords laced into boards.

By tradition, a later bishop named Ethelwald did that part. He bound it. He gave it a body to live in.

Then a third man, an anchorite named Billfrith, made the outside. He worked in metal. He covered the binding with gold and silver and set stones into it, so the book shut like a small treasure chest.

That jeweled cover is long gone now. The binding you would see today was made in the nineteenth century, in the old style, to protect what is inside. The stones are elsewhere, or nowhere, and it does not much matter tonight.

What matters is the shape of the work. One man ruled and wrote and painted. One man sewed. One man wrapped it in gold. Three trades and three lifetimes, laid over one another, for a single object.

None of them saw the whole of it at once. The scribe never handled the finished cover. The metalworker never ruled a line. Each did his part, and passed it on, and trusted the next pair of hands.

§

Now let us jump forward, gently, about two hundred and fifty years.

The book still exists. It is still being read. And a priest named Aldred sits down with it and does something quietly extraordinary.

He takes the Latin, line by line, and writes the English above it. Not a summary. A word for every word, squeezed into the small space between the ruled lines, in a smaller and quicker hand.

He is not trying to be beautiful. He is trying to be useful. His letters are practical and a little cramped, and they run along above the great round Latin letters like a handrail.

That gloss of his is the oldest surviving version of the Gospels in the English language. It is also one of the longest pieces of Old English we have. That makes it precious to people who study how the language moved.

And at the end of the book he adds a short note about who made it. He names the scribe, Eadfrith. He names Ethelwald, who bound it. He names Billfrith, who ornamented it. And then he names himself, and what he did.

Almost everything we say about how this book came to be rests on those few lines. One man, three hundred years later, writing down what he had been told, in the back of the book itself.

It is a small act of housekeeping. And it is the only reason any of these names are in your ears tonight.

§

Let us step back outside for a while. The light is lower now.

There is another story on this coast, older than the book, and it belongs to the water.

A generation or so before Eadfrith, a monk named Cuthbert lived here. He was prior of this community. And after some years he asked to go further out. There is a small island a few miles down the coast, called the Inner Farne.

He built himself a cell there, round, of rough stone and turf. He dug the floor down into the rock so that the wall stood higher than his head. From inside it, sitting, he could see nothing but the sky.

The island was full of birds. It still is. Among them are eiders, big soft sea ducks. The females are a mottled brown. The drakes are black and white, with a wash of green on the neck.

Cuthbert made a rule that nobody was to take them, or their eggs, in the nesting season. It is one of the first pieces of bird protection anyone has written down.

They are still there. And in Northumberland they are still called cuddy ducks, after him, because Cuddy is the local short form of his name. A man's nickname, attached to a duck, for thirteen hundred years.

Sit with the sound of them for a moment. The drakes make a low soft call in the spring, rising and falling, a little like someone gently surprised. It carries over water better than you would think.

And there is one more creature in his story, which I would like to leave beside you.

Bede tells it, a few decades after Cuthbert died. One night a young monk followed him, curious, to see where he went in the dark.

Cuthbert walked down to the shore and waded out. He stood until the sea was at his neck. And he stayed there, singing, until the sky began to go pale.

Then he came up and knelt on the sand. And two otters came out of the water and lay across his feet, warming them, drying them with their fur. When they had finished, he blessed them, and they went back into the sea.

Whatever you make of the story, it is a kind one. It has no teeth in it anywhere.

There are otters here still, along this coast, working the rocks at the tide edge. A dark shape in the water, a line of small bubbles, a flat head that goes under without a sound. Then nothing. Then the same head, forty feet further along.

The eiders bob in the swell beyond the breakers, in loose rafts, rising and falling together. The oystercatchers work the wet sand with that steady sewing motion. Everything out here is busy in a way that asks nothing of you at all.

§

Back inside, the light has gone amber.

The book has a long journey ahead of it, and it is worth knowing that it survived.

It left the island eventually, and traveled with the community. It came to rest in one place, and then another, for centuries. Books that are looked after tend to keep being looked after. That is most of the secret.

In the seventeenth century it came into the hands of a collector named Robert Cotton. He kept his manuscripts in a small room lined with wooden presses. On top of each press stood a marble bust of a Roman emperor.

To find a book you said which emperor it lived under. And that is why, to this day, the formal name of this book includes the word Nero. A bust on a shelf in London, four hundred years ago, is still telling librarians where to look.

The collection went to the nation. From there it went into the British Museum. And from there into the British Library, where it is now.

You can go and see it. It lies open in a case, in low light, with one pair of pages showing. The light is kept dim because the colors are organic and they would fade. So the book is shown the way it was mostly kept. In the half-dark, resting.

A few times a year somebody turns the page. That is the whole of its current life. One turned page, and then months of quiet.

§

Out on the flats now, the tide has come all the way in.

The track you walked over is gone. There is only water, flat and pewter-colored, moving very slightly. The island is properly an island again, and will be for the next few hours.

Listen. Out on the sandbanks at the mouth of the bay, the gray seals have hauled themselves up. There are a great many of them, lying on their sides in the last of the light.

And they are singing. That is really the word people use for it. A long wavering moan, rising and falling, one voice starting and then another joining underneath. It comes across the water very clearly, and it sounds older than anything else here.

The oystercatchers have gone quiet. A few late birds cross the sky in a loose line, going somewhere they know.

The wind drops the way it often does at the end of a long day. The marram grass stops whispering. The water against the rocks goes softer and slower.

Inside the room, the light on the desk has finally gone. He works a few minutes more by feel and habit, and then stops.

He washes the brush. He caps the little horn of ink. He lays a clean piece of skin over the page he was painting, to keep the dust off it overnight.

§

And now nothing is being made.

The room holds its warmth for a while after the fire is banked. The smell of beeswax stays. The pages lie in their gatherings under a weight, perfectly flat, perfectly still.

The unfinished letter waits on the desk. It has waited before. It is in no distress about it. Tomorrow, if the light is good, a hand will come back to it, and the day after that.

Outside, the sea goes around the island the way it has always gone. It covers the track, and it will uncover it in the morning, and nobody has to do anything about that at all.

The lead roof of the church cools and ticks very faintly. Somewhere in the dunes, sand runs a little way down a slope and settles.

The seals are still singing out on the bar. It rises, and thins, and stops, and starts again, further off.

There is nothing to watch for here. There is no one coming across the sand tonight. The water has seen to that, kindly, the way it does twice a day, without being asked.

§

So let it all go quiet now.

Let the last of the light leave the window. Let the sea lie flat and dark all the way round the island. Let the eiders rock on the swell, asleep with their heads turned back into their feathers.

Let the great page rest in the dark under its cloth, half-painted, in no hurry whatsoever.

You do not need to keep any of it. Not the names, not the colors, not a single fact from the whole quiet evening. It has kept itself for thirteen hundred years without your help, and it will keep itself tonight.

Let your breathing go slow, and slower, and slower still. Let your thoughts settle like sand going down through still water. Let the warmth of the room come up around you. Let the sound of the sea get further and further away.

You are held. You are safe. There is nothing left to do but rest.

Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.

Goodnight.
