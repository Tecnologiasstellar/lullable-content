# Research sources — The Scriptorium at Lindisfarne

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne_Gospels — date of making (probably around 715–720); Eadfrith as scribe and painter; 516 pages of vellum from roughly 150 calfskins; gatherings of eight pricked through at once and ruled individually; insular majuscule script; text ink dense dark brown, almost black; indigo identified in place of the long-assumed lapis lazuli; gold in small details only; Ethelwald bound it, Billfrith ornamented it; jewelled cover lost, replacement binding made in 1852; now British Library Cotton MS Nero D.IV.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eadfrith_of_Lindisfarne — Eadfrith bishop of Lindisfarne c. 698–721; the tenth-century colophon is the source of the attribution; scholars disagree whether he wrote it himself or commissioned it; Michelle Brown's 715–722 dating; his restoration of Cuthbert's hermitage on Farne.

https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=1976 — Aldred, second half of the tenth century, added the colophon and a word-by-word interlinear Old English gloss; the oldest surviving translation of the Gospels into English.

https://www.medievalists.net/2014/12/domiciling-evangelists-anglo-saxon-england-fresh-reading-aldreds-colophon-lindisfarne-gospels/ — content of Aldred's colophon: Eadfrith wrote, Ethelwald bound, Billfrith ornamented, Aldred glossed.

https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/modern-europe/british-and-irish-history/lindisfarne-gospels — the Gospel text is a pure Vulgate of the Italo-Northumbrian family, close to Codex Amiatinus, with an exemplar that appears to have come from Naples; canon tables and prefaces at the front.

https://www.digitalmedievalist.com/things/manuscripts/the-book-of-lindisfarne/ — ten canon tables distributed over sixteen pages, following instructions from Jerome and Eusebius; five carpet pages of cruciform geometric design; carpet pages made with compass and straight edge.

https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=1647 — compass marks, grids and lead-point drawings visible on the backs of the carpet pages; lead stylus used to rough out designs on the reverse; a light source behind the page used in places.

https://www.patricialovett.com/the-lindisfarne-gospels/ — guidelines made by lead point, "the forerunner of the pencil"; pin-prick marks from dividers used to keep distances even on the central boss of a carpet page.

https://analyticalsciencejournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jrs.1110 — Raman microscopy pigment identification: red lead, orpiment yellow, indigo blue (both vergaut and verdigris greens), iron-gall black sometimes with carbon, lead white and chalk in pinks, organic dyestuffs for yellows/pinks/purples, metallic gold; the blue is exclusively indigo, not lazurite.

https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1395722 — pigments of the earliest Northumbrian manuscripts; black script ink of oak galls and iron salts, distinct from the carbon lampblack used in the illumination.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_gall_ink — oak galls formed when a wasp lays in an oak bud; galls crushed and steeped, iron salt added, gum binder; ink darkens on the page and bites slightly into the surface.

https://www.lindisfarne.org.uk/gospels/ — the Lindisfarne Gospels required about 130 of the best calves' skins; Eadfrith's colours from animal, vegetable and mineral sources bound with glair (egg white); verdigris from copper over vinegar; orpiment yellow; red and orange from toasted lead; blue, crimson and purple from woad and lichen.

https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/lindisfarne-priory/History/ — monastery founded about 635; King Oswald summoned Aidan from Iona; the priory ruin visitors see today is much later than the Anglo-Saxon monastery.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindisfarne — tidal causeway generally open from about three hours after high tide until two hours before the next; island 3.0 miles east to west, 1.5 miles north to south, about 1,000 acres, roughly 0.8 miles off the mainland; Aidan there from c. 634 until his death in 651; Cuthbert prior and bishop; Lindisfarne National Nature Reserve with 330 bird species recorded.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finan_of_Lindisfarne — Finan built the timber church of hewn oak thatched with reeds in the Irish manner; a later bishop, Eadberht, removed the thatch and covered walls and roof with lead.

https://www.britannica.com/biography/Saint-Cuthbert — Cuthbert prior of Lindisfarne from 664; withdrew to Inner Farne as a hermit in 676.

https://www.nationaltrust.org.uk/visit/north-east/farne-islands/history-of-the-farne-islands-inhabitants — Cuthbert's rules protecting the eider ducks and other nesting seabirds on the Farne Islands, among the earliest bird-protection laws recorded.

https://www.pilgrimageandcathedrals.ac.uk/blog/st-cuthbert%E2%80%99s-ducks-1446120484 — eiders known in Northumbrian dialect as "cuddy ducks", from the short form of Cuthbert's name.

https://schoolshistory.org.uk/topics/the-saxons/bede-the-life-of-st-cuthbert/ — Bede's account of Cuthbert's round cell of rough stone and turf on Inner Farne, sunk so that he could see only the sky; the night in the sea and the two otters that dried and warmed his feet.

https://www.lindisfarne.org.uk/general/pdf/NNRLindisfarneLeaflet.pdf — the National Nature Reserve: shifting dunes, intertidal sand and mud flats, saltmarsh, raised beaches; oystercatchers and curlews; large flocks of waders and wildfowl.

https://holy-island.uk/seals-on-holy-island-and-the-farne-islands-where-and-when-to-see-them/ — grey seals hauled out on the sandbanks near St Cuthbert's Island, and the "singing" heard across the water.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_manuscripts_in_the_Cotton_library — Robert Cotton's presses each topped by a marble bust of a Roman emperor, which is why the shelfmark carries the name Nero.

https://blogs.bl.uk/digitisedmanuscripts/2014/10/lindisfarne-gospels-in-our-treasures-gallery.html — the Gospels on display in the British Library Treasures Gallery, shown at one opening in low light, with the page turned only occasionally to protect the colours.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billfrith — Billfrith the anchorite, who covered the binding in gold, silver and gems.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_eider — the eider drake's low crooning call; the females mottled brown, the drakes black and white with green on the nape.
