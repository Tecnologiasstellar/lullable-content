# The house voice

Everything in here is a rule, not a preference. The voice is the product; it is
what makes one Lullable episode recognisable as the same thing as another.

---

## The stance

Warm, second person, unhurried. **You are tucking someone in.**

You are not lecturing, not selling, not performing. You are a calm person sitting
in the room while someone falls asleep, describing something real because it is
interesting and because following it is restful.

---

## Short sentences

The single non-negotiable rule, and the one enforced by the compiler.

- Mean sentence length under **12 words**
- Almost nothing over **22 words**
- Sprinkle three-word beats: *"In. And out." "It is over."*

**Why it is mechanical, not stylistic.** The compiler drops a break tag after
each group of roughly 5–9 words, but it can only break *between whole sentences*.
One 40-word sentence becomes one 40-word group with a single pause after it — the
cadence collapses exactly where the listener needed a gap.

Above 15.0 words per break the compiler writes nothing and names the twelve worst
sentences. Fix the prose.

---

## The arc

Every episode, the same shape.

| Beat | What it does |
|---|---|
| `Good evening.` | Fixed opening. Never varies. |
| Settling | Shoulders down, weight released, breathing slows. |
| Where we are going | Two or three sentences. No suspense. |
| Permission to drift | *"If your attention drifts, let it drift. The thread is only here to give you something gentle to let go of."* |
| Descent into the setting | Slow, sensory, spatial. |
| Factual body | The bulk. Dense but calm. Real mechanics. |
| A change of texture | A creature, a person, an object, a sound. |
| Stillness | Nothing left to watch. |
| Dissolve | *"You do not need to keep any of it."* |
| `Sleep now. Easy, and unhurried, and deep.` | Fixed. |
| `Goodnight.` | Fixed. |

Aim for 14–20 sections, separated by a lone `§` paragraph. Each seam becomes a
3.0-second pause.

---

## Curiosity without stimulation

The factual body is the product. Specific, accurate detail occupies the intellect
without triggering adrenaline.

**Depth is the point. Drama is the enemy.**

Good material: physics, geology, ecology, chemistry, craft, history-as-
architecture, how a thing is made, why a thing is the shape it is, an unresolved
scientific argument.

Bad material: jeopardy, competition, records being broken, anything with a
countdown, anything a documentary would score with strings.

---

## Zero-conflict filtration

No danger. No predators hunting. No death emphasis. No war. No sudden or loud
events. No collapse, no catastrophe, no last survivor.

Animals, weather, machines and deep time are rendered calm, spatial and safe.

**When a fact is inherently bleak**, either leave it out or reframe it gently.
Worked examples from the catalogue:

| Fact | How it was handled |
|---|---|
| Cassini was deliberately destroyed | *"it finished its work"* — the ending is simply not mentioned |
| Saturn's rings are decaying | *"the ice is going home"*, over a timespan hundreds of times longer than humans have existed |
| The Library of Alexandria was lost | The episode ends with the lamps going out for the night. The loss is never raised. |

---

## Sensory density

Temperature. Soft light. Texture. Quiet sound. Smell.

Every section should put the listener somewhere physical. *Cool water. Warm
flour. Dry papyrus. Cedar oil. Frost on parked cars.*

---

## Sleep-safe language

Describe the experience. **Never** promise sleep, treat insomnia or anxiety, or
make any medical claim.

| Don't write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "This will help you fall asleep" | "There is nothing left to do but rest" |
| "Cures a racing mind" | "Nothing here to watch for tonight" |
| "Reduces anxiety" | "It asks nothing of you" |
| "Clinically calming" | *(nothing — cut it)* |

The card copy carries the same rule. `bestFor` is a benefit, never a diagnosis:
*"Letting the day end"*, not *"For insomniacs"*.

---

## Punctuation

- Em-dashes and ellipses for drift.
- **Never** an exclamation mark. The compiler rejects the file.
- No rhetorical questions that demand an answer. A wondering question is fine:
  *"Let us stay in the dark a while, and wonder, gently, why so many of these
  creatures shine."*

---

## Card copy

Written while the story is fresh, into `card:` in the manifest.

| Field | Target | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| title | 18–48 | Specific and memorable. No SEO titles, no quotation marks. |
| subtitle | 35–90 | One calm promise. Do not repeat the title. |
| bedtimeNote | 90–180 | "Why this story tonight." 1–2 sentences. |
| bestFor | 10–24 | A benefit, not a diagnosis. |
| sleepPace | 8–22 | Short fragment. |
| atmosphere | 10–28 | 2–4 mood words. |
| description | 220–650 | 2–4 calm, specific sentences about the journey. |

Tags are fragments, not sentences — they have to read well beside their icon. The
first two sit side by side on iPhone, which is why their caps are tight.

U.S. English. One card, one language.

---

## Writing to length

First drafts come in short. Every one so far has.

**Extend by adding whole sections. Never by lengthening sentences.**

A new section adds a 3.0-second seam and keeps the cadence right. Padding
sentences fails the gate and makes the episode worse.

Reliable extension material:

- How the thing was discovered, and by whom
- What one component is actually made of
- An unresolved argument, presented from both sides
- The instrument or expedition that found it out
- A second creature, room, or object of the same kind

**Counter-intuitive but true:** splitting long sentences makes an episode
*longer*, because more sentence breaks means more engineered silence. Episode 16
went from 16.0 to 13.7 words/break and gained runtime. Shorter sentences are not
a cost.
